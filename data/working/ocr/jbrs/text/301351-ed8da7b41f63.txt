MANU IN BURMA:
SOME BURMESE DHAMMATHATS
I.
When the Pilgrim Fathers left the shores of Europe to found a new
world in the West, they took with them their supreme code of law, the
Bible. In like manner, a thousand or more years earlier, the emigrants from
India, Hindu and Buddhist, who laid the foundations of a new world in
the Tropical Far East, took with them their law-book, the Code of Manu.
Everywhere throughout this region Manu has left his mark: in Burma both
among Mon and Burman, in Siam, Cambodia, Java and Bali. To follow
him through his various incarnations, Hindu and Buddhist, in these various
countries would be a fascinating problem and should throw much light upon
the course of Indian influence in Further Asia, and it is partly as a contri-
bution to this difficult question that the present essay is intended. But it
is merely one preliminary step; it attempts to set in order of date the
Burmese dhammatħats (dhammasattha, dharmasāstra) still extant with a
view to re-examining the development of Manu literature in Burma.
1
The pioneer in the study of the history of Burmese Law was the late
Dr. E. Forchhammer, who contributed to the Notes on Buddhist Law pub-
lished by Sir John Jardine in 1882 and 1883, and stated his views at length
in the Jardine Prize Essay of 1885. Since then nothing has been done;
Forchhammer still stands alone as the only scholar who has attempted a
survey of the legal literature of Burma in the light of modern scientific
criticism, and his views have reached a wider circle through their acceptance
by Dr. M. Bode in her study of The Pāli Literature of Burma, and by
Mr. G. E. Harvey in his History of Burma. 2 But Forchhammer was work-
ing at a time when little was known of Buddhism, or of Pāli, or of the
early history of the Tropical Far East, and it is not strange that on many
points his theories need re-examination. I propose therefore to compare
his account of Manu literature in Burma with the accounts given by two
Burmese scholars, Mahā-siri-jeyasu, and the Kin-wun Mingyi, and with
occasional reference to the Kawi Lakkhana Dipani by Siri-mahā-jeyasū.
The first of these, Mahä-siri-jeyasu, entering government service under
Mindon Min, rose to high office and at one time was Keeper of the Royal
Library. This gave him exceptional facilities for a survey of Burmese
Literature which he turned to good account by publishing in 1250 B. E.
(1888 A. D.), two years after the Annexation of Upper Burma, the Piṭakat
Thamaing or History of Burmese Literature. This includes as Item No.

Forchhammer recognizes three periods in the development of dhamma-
that literature: the first ending with the break-up of Bayinnaung's empire
in 1600; the second ending with the Talaing invasion of Ava in 1750;
and the third covering the Alaungpaya dynasty. In the first period he
mentions, though sometimes rather vaguely, about 20 dhammathats; for the
second period he cites only 3, but for the third period he gives a list of 32.
It will be convenient to adopt his grouping.
II.
On Forchhammer's view Indian law was adopted by the Talaings and,
during the "first period" of Burmese law, was taken over by the Burmese,
with no alterations of any importance, in translations from Pāli-Talaing
texts. The law as it then stood contained "no decided religious element;
its dicta rested on the authority of Manu, the Rishi."

(a) The Pyū-min-hti dh., the first in Forchhammer's list; comes ninth
in the Thamaing. Like its predecessors in that list it is pre-historic. Some
of them date even from the time of former Buddhas before the present
universe took shape, and the critical reader will accept with some reserve
the statement of the editors of the Thamaing that the four oldest are still
accessible in the Bernard Free Library. It seems however that one of these,
the Manosāra (D.1, P. 1603) may be identified with a later work, the
Dhammathat-kyaw (T. 1613), and another, the Pyu-min-hti of Forch-
hammer (D. 3, T. 1609) seems to be identical with the Manusāra Kyan-
haung (T.1611) also known as the Kaing-sa Shwe Myin (D. 7). Pre-
sumably the other pre-historic dhammathats are likewise recent compilations
which pretend antiquity to strengthen their authority. We may pass on,
therefore, to the earliest that can make any pretension to be authentic.

(b) The Dhammavilāsa dh. is said by Forchhammer to have been
written by a Talaing monk of that name. He follows the legend, recorded
in the Kalyāṇī Inscription erected about 1475 A. D. by King Dhammazedi,
that a monk Sariputta was considered by Narapatisithu (1173–1219 A. D.)
for the post of Royal Preceptor but rejected on account of bodily infirm-
ity; the king however induced him to compile a dhammathat, and conferred
on him the title Dhammavilāsa, by which his dhammathat is known. This
story has found general acceptance among Burman scholars and is adopted.
by Mr. Harvey, 1 but contemporary inscriptions give it no support, and it
remains a legend of doubtful authenticity. In any case the original is no
longer extant, but Forchhammer claims to have found quotations from it
in a dhammathat called the Ko-saung-kyop, showing it to be in the main
identical with the Talaing dhammathat ascribed to Wāgarū. 2 Forchhammer
also states that a commentary on the Dhammavilasa was written about 1650
A. D. by order of King Ngã-su-dayakā, and a Burmese version by Nanda-
mālā in 1768. These statements are certainly inaccurate. Ngā-su-dāyakā
did not reign about 1650 but was one name of Nandabayin who reigned
from 1581-1599. As regards the alleged quotations in the Ko-saung-kyop,
we shall see that no such dhammathat can be traced. Neither the Digest
nor the Thamaing mentions any commentary written in 1650, but a monk
Dhammavilāsā produced a versified edition in that year, the Myingun dh.
linkā (D. 9, T. 1691). There is also still extant a version known as the
Dhammaviläsa, which purports, according to a copy in the British Museum,
to have been compiled in 1637 by a monk of that name. As for the alleged
Burmese version by Nandamālā, the Burmese scholars mention him only as
the compiler about 1788 of a Pāli version of the original Manu (D. 24, T.
1648).

(c) The Wagarû dh., is the next on Forchhammer's list. He follows
the Talaing chronicle, Rāzādirit Ayedawbon, in ascribing this to Wāgarū,
who founded a Talaing kingdom in 1287 on the break-up of the Pagan
Empire; he states that it was translated into Burmese by the jurist Buddha-
ghosa, who was one of the monks sent on a mission to Ceylon by Dhamma-
zedi, and that it was later adopted by Bayinnaung as the Manusāra Dhamma-
that. This account followed by Mr. Harvey, 2 is inherently improbable and
conflicts with Burmese tradition. It is true that according to the chronicles
a Talaing dhammathat was compiled at the instance of Wāgarū, and this
story is not incredible although, in view of the conditions then obtaining in
Pegu, it would seem open to question. 3 But there seems to be no founda-
tion for Forchhammer's statement that the Buddhaghosa who went to
Ceylon was the same man who translated the dhammathat into Burmese,
and it is very improbable that, at a time when Pegu was at its greatest under
Dhammazedi, a Mon should have translated the dhammathat into the
language of the insignificant and still barbarous principality of Toungoo.
The Burmese tradition is given in the Thamaing (T. 1611), which
states that Pyū-min-hti's Pāli version of Manu was translated into Talaing
and then, at some subsequent date, translated into Burmese by an unknown
monk; this version was then revised by a monk named Buddhaghosa during
the reign of Bayinnaung at the instance of the Crown Prince. The Buddha-
ghosa who translated the work about 1550-80 cannot possibly be the man
who was sent on the Kalyāṇī mission about 1475. The Thamaing tells us
further that Buddhaghosa's version was again revised in 1637, so it is per-
haps doubtful whether the work as we now have it goes back even so far
as Bayinnaung. The actual manuscript from which it was printed purports
to have been copied in 1707.

The book has in this essay probably received more attention than its intrinsic
value would appear to justify. It must, however be remembered that it was compiled
when Zinpyumyashin (Bayinnaung) had become lord of Toungoo, Prome, Hamsavatı
and Arakan; it is reported that he appointed a commission consisting of a number of
learned priests and noblemen from the different parts of his empire to frame
authoritative codes of law for the country. The Dhammathat-kyaw and the
Ko-saung-kyop are the results; the latter is of great importance, because it mentions
by name the law-books from which it was compiled. It cannot therefore be doubted
that the law as set forth in the Wagari and the Ko-saung-kyop was the law which
Anawratha found existing in the conquered coast provinces of Burma in the eleventh

But this whole argument starts from false premises. The Dhammathat-
kyaw dates, at earliest from the reign of Nandabayin, and in the version
quoted by the Kinwun Mingyi dates from 1733-53. The Ko-saung-kyop
cites the Kitti dh., which dates from 1733-53, and cannot therefore much,
if at all, ante-date Alaung-paya. Further, it is very unfortunate that this
dhammathat, on which Forchhammer lays so much stress, should apparently
be unknown to any scholar but himself. It looks rather as if Forchhammer
had come across some manuscript for which the compiler claimed the
authority of Bayinnaung, much as other modern works claim to have been
written by mythical scholars before the beginning of the world.
Thus of the works assigned by Forchhammer to his first period, there
remains only the Wāgaru, and even this may possibly in its present form
be the recension made at the instance of Tha-lun about 1637.

on the destruction of Burmese records while the Talaings were masters of
Upper Burma. Many copies of old dhammathats must, however, have sur-
vived in monasteries scattered over the country, and it is probable that these
formed the basis for many of the works now produced by apparently un-
distinguished people. We have for example, the Vinicchayarāsī by a monk
in a village near Popa, another version in Pali (D. 25) by a monk in the
Tabayin District, another by a villager in Alon (D. 29), another by a clerk
to a local Wundauk (D. 31), another by the clerk in charge of the Royal
Boats (D. 28); others are anonymous (D. 31 to 34). Some of these may
have been merely essays in versification, based on well known editions, but
it seems not unlikely that many were reproductions of lost works.
We are bound to recognize the valuable work done by Forchhammer
as a pioneer in studying the evolution of Burmese law, and should not over-
look the difficulties under which he worked; yet it is difficult to feel that
he always succeeded in resisting the temptation to adjust the facts to fit
his theories. One need not suppose that he was cognizant of the facts
which were inconsistent with his argument, yet he seems to have been too
ready to state as matters of fact things which were no better than guesses.
Although his main thesis as to the evolution of Burmese Law is not im-
probably correct, it is clearly impossible to accept his account in detail. In
these notes I have merely attempted to place the dhammathats in chrono-
logical order so far as our present knowledge allows. But it is high time
that some Burman scholar should undertake a critical re-examination of the
texts. Such an examination might help to elucidate not merely the history
of law in Burma, but also the history of society.
