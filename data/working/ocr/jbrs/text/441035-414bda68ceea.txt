BAYINNAUNG'S LIVING DESCENDANT:
THE MAGH BOHMONG
BY
G. E. HARVEY
SINCE we are all sons of Adam it would be surprising if ancient kings did not now
have innumerable obscure descendants. The more numerous the more obscure
because only a few, as the generations lengthened, could retain their connection
with the great. Some years ago, in Oxfordshire villages not far from where I live,
we found a farm labourer and a maidservant who, although unaware of the fact
and indeed strangers to each other, are descended from a medieval English king.
And Bayinnaung, survived by no fewer than 89 children, should have quite a number
of living descendants not that they can be identified. But here is one who can
because, like his ancestors in the intervening generations, he is a ruler.
He is the Bohmong of Bandarban (on the Sangu river 50 miles north of
Arakan's Buthidaung frontier), descended from Nandabayin, Bayinnaung's son and
successor.
At the sack of Pegu in 1599 Arakan's share of the spoil included several of
the royal family, the most easily identifiable being Nandabayin's daughter
Hkinmahnaung who now became the king of Arakan's "Pegu Queen” ranking next
to the White Elephant as his finest trophy. Manrique, meeting her in 1630, heard
that she was descended from the kings of Ayuthia. This descent would be through
her mother, Nandabayin's first queen the daughter of Tabinshwehtil; there seems
to be no list of Tabinshwehti's wives and children but he was bought off with
a princess during one of his attacks on Ayuthia2.
European sources³ say 5,000 Mons4 and Burmans headed by some of
Nandabayin's sons and his brother were taken with the princess to Arakan; the only
person named is Ximicolia, one of the sons, on whose companionship the princess
insisted as they had been brought up together. And in 1605 "the emperor of Pegu's
sons Ximicolia and Marequestão, taken with the princess," were among the Arakan
yuvaraja's lieutenants operating against De Brito in the Bassein-Negrais area; they
would be her half-brothers as none of her mother's other children were taken.
The Bohmong's own sources5 mention only the princess and his ancestor her
brother or half-brother. In 1614 this brother was made governor of Chittagong,
an important office which changed hands periodically; and in 1620, for war service
against the Mughal, he received the title Bohmong6. In 1710, when his descendant.
not only drove back the Mughal but helped Sandawizaya to gain the Arakanese
throne, the title was raised to Bohmong Gri.
The Mughal conquest of Chittagong in 1666 deprived Arakan of her
Portuguese fleet and its base but Mughal control over the interior towards the
Arakan frontier was often ineffective. Here, in this southern area, the Bohmongs
and their people, valley dwellers, lived among other peoples and chieftains in swamp

and forest, hill and plain, owing more or less allegiance to whichever power
happened to be the stronger, and sometimes fighting each other, the hill peoples in
particular raiding the plains. In 1756, four years before the British acquired
Chittagong, the Bohmong and his people, unable to withstand the Mughal, migrated
to Arakan but in 1774, complaining of oppression, they returned to British territory
settling in succession first at Ramu, Idgarh and on the Matamuri river; then at
Maiskhal in 1804; and finally, moving north, at Bandarban in 1822.
Conditions in former times were often such that a Bohmong could not survive
unless he had character. Survival was easier in British times, indeed too easy as
the British did nothing to ensure that a Bohmong's heirs were properly educated
and brought up with a sense of duty. Nevertheless there could be good Bohmongs.
The 9th, Sana Yeo, awarded the Burmese title KSM for providing the Lushai
Expedition 1889-90 with labour and supplies, was doubtless only serving his masters
but he had to serve well: the title was not given for nothing. And in 1926 J.P.
Mills ICS found the 12th, Keozan Phroo, a personality who would stand out
anywhere, trusted and loved as well as feared by his people.
Burma officers, friends of mine, meeting the 13th, Kyaw Jaw San Prue, in
1942-5, found him a much slighter character and were puzzled at his insistence that
he was "a warrior Talaing, descended from warrior Talaings" -he used the name
used by foreigners, not by the Mons themselves7.
From early times there had probably been Mons as well as Burmans in
Arakan, the latter not predominating till 1430. Moreover the Burman conquest of
Pegu in 1539 was followed by repeated rebellions and the delta Mons often fled to
Arakan where foreign observers found them still serving in the armies a century
later8. They must have been largely absorbed by 1825 as the first British
administrators noticed none, the only Mons they mention being immigrants from
Burma a few years later. But the Bohmongs were not absorbed: they retained
their identity and had they originally been Mons they would have said so before
now.
The British divided the Chittagong Hill Tracts, 5,007 sq. miles, into three
circles or subdivisions, Chakma, Mong, Bohmong, each under its own chief9.
The Chakmas are the Thet Kadu10 of Burma but their chiefs, first created
by the Mughal, for long bore Muslim names and their people, inter-
marrying with Mughal troops, were attracted to Islam; later they reverted to a
hinduised form of Buddhism; their language, formerly Burmese, seems to
be corrupt Bengali. The Mong chief is not a Mughal creation but, like the
Bohmong, a clan chief; many of his people, though much the same stock as the
Bohmong's, are hardly less indianised than the Chakmas.
The southernmost circle, the Bohmong's, 1,665 square miles, is slightly larger
than Maubin district; of its 70,000 people 29,000 are Magh11, 28,000 pagan
hillfolk, and the rest Bengali etc. It differs from the Chakma and Mong circles:

were: they might be in what the British regarded as the territory of another chief.
Again, his receipts were not taxes of which he kept a proportion, surrendering the
rest to Government, but contributions for the services he provided; he had to keep
open house, feeding the poor, and even the gifts he received during a commoner's
rise in social status (building a superior house, wearing jewels or shoes instead of
going barefoot like others) were not sumptuary taxes but gifts made at fcasts of
merit during a rite de passage. He surrendered no revenue to his overlord: he and
his people had to serve the overlord as and when required but the tribute he paid
was not a share of the revenue, only a symbolic offering, often decorative rather
than valuable.
The Bohmong's is the leading family in the Regratsa clan, the largest and most
important of Magh clans. Doubtless it was originally, during his captivity in the
Mrauk U palace, only his own family and household. Success would attract
followers till in due course they and their descendants numbered several thousand.
At times, especially in time of stress, men of merit were admitted, whatever their
origin, but the dominant tone throughout remained Burman.

The Chakmas were sufficiently distinct (unlike the Maghs who were simply
Arakanese) and important (unlike minor frontier peoples) to appear in the
Arakanese royal style "King of Arakan...of the Chakmas..." Arakanese MSS
mention a rebellion of theirs in 1594 which prevented the king's brother, governor
of Chittagong, from returning home by land: he had to go by sea. A Thetmin
was among the Arakanese commanders attacking Nandabayin in 1599; he drove off
the Siamese who tried to join in looting Pegu.
Chakmas were among the peoples of whose frontier raids, after taking refuge
in Chittagong, the Burmese complained to the British in 1787. But those who
migrated from east of the Naaf to the Chittagong Hill Tracts in 1795 and 1819 seem
to have been peaceful cultivators.
There had always been Chakmas north of the frontier where they nearly all are
now, outnumbering the Maghs and often indistinguishable from Bengalis in
appearance, dress and customs: even their “ancient traditions" have become
Hindu. Their ancestors may have migrated too early or from areas too remote to
absorb the Buddhism of their enemy Pagan, they would receive little in their new
abode and their subsequent conversion to Islam was so superficial that in 1873
their chieftainess Kalindi Rani found they had little religion of any sort; though
herself inclined to Siva and Kali, she revived their Buddhism.

Arakanese (Burmese)-speaking Buddhist Maghs such as the Bohmong's do
not call themselves Maghs. They are Arakanese living in the lowlands north of
the Arakan frontier where some either became indianised or went wild taking
heads almost as freely as the headhunter tribes on the neighbouring hills.
Those who remained under control were among the Arakanese who supplied, if
they were watermen, not only officers and rowers for the king of Arakan's
warcanoes but rowers for the galleys and maybe some lascars for the ships of
his Portuguese fleet-their slave raids in the Ganges delta made “Magh" a name
of terror.
The 1951 census classifications are not always apposite but they shew, on a
conservative interpretation, 96,000 Moghs of whom 82,000 are mostly in the
Chittagong Hill Tracts, some in Chittagong district or Tripura State; and the
remaining 16,000 in Bakarganj district.

These last, across the sea, are no survival from ancient wars:
Maghs went to Bengal as captives or refugees, the captives were absorbed and the
refugees returned when they could, collaborating in Mughal attacks on Arakan.
But Magh boatmen, pirates and traders had long known the Gonges delta (for that
matter the first British administrators in Arakan found Burmese boats from Bassein
coasting along to trade in Bengal every open season). Already in 1789 the
British in Bengal were granting Magh families rich rice-land in the Bakarganj
Sundarbans, the southernmost Ganges delta; at that time it was uninhabited swamp
and forest but these Maghs were hardy pioneers and they preferred loneliness.
Others have occasionally followed and contact with the motherland is maintained
in the trade season. Hence, though some are absorbed in the surrounding Hindu
population, the number of those who retain their identity has quadrupled since 1872
when there were only 4,000.
