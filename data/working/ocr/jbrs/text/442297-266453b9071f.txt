REVIEW
COMMENTARY ON THE POEMS OF TAUNGOO MIN
by
Shin Okkanthamala
BRS Text Publications, New Series I
Now that the Text Publication Sub-committee of the Burma Research Society has
started to function again after the war. I think we ought to revive the good practice of
writing reviews on the texts published. Hence this short review. U Wun, the lexico-
grapher is the General Editor of the New Series of texts. The first text in the New Series
is edited by U Wun himself and is a remarkable book, entitled the Commentary on the
Poems of Taungoo Min by Shin Okkanthamala. We have in the poet the most famous
writer of love poems in Burmese literature, an acknowledged Yadu form of poetry
embodying subtle thought and profound meaning. And the commentator is none other
than the monk Okkanthamala, well-known for his learning in Pali and Burmese. The
poet was born in A.D. 1578 and died at the age of 35. The commentary was written 100
years after his death, being one of the earliest specimens of commentarial literature in
Burmese. It may seem odd that a learned monk should have written a commentary on
Burmese love-poetry. But monks were the custodians of Buddhist learning, which was
not confined to religion but extended to such secular lore as astrology, alchemy, civil law
and medicine. And there was nothing odd in the learned Shin Okkanthamala's initiating a
new feature of Burmese literature by writing a commentary on the love-poems of Taungoo
Min, especially as the Poet has made many references in his poems to pagodas and
devas, Buddhist cosmology and chronicles which come within the range of Buddhist
literature.
But what is worth noting is the attitude of the commentator towards Burmese
grammar. Out of the 56 works which the Editor has been able to list as references that
the commentator has made in the course of his comments, a dozen or so are Pali gram-
matical works. In one of his comments the commentator says that although there are
grammars of Sanskrit and Pali and other languages of the world since grammar is a
universal usage, there is no grammar for Burmese because it is an easy language to
understand. Instead of writing a Burmese grammar himself, he employs Pali grammatical
terms to explain Burmese usage. Thus instead of saying in simple Burmese that cojos has
an unexpected result, he refers us to the Kaccayanadipani, which explains that usage of
လျက် in two lines of Pali verse. The usage of 500 'only' is explained not in
Burmese but by the Pali eva, which needs a number of Pali grammatical terms
to elucidate. And one must understand the meanings of the Pali Upamājotaka
to know that so is a suffix for comparison, of the Pali vicchu to know that
& is intensified in meaning when reduplicated, of the Pali Sambhāvanā and
Samuccaya to know the two meanings of cops:. And Pali grammatical authority is quoted
to justify the construing of a subject with a distant verb in Burmese. In fact, one must be
a classical Pali grammarian to know the usages of Burmese Suffixes and of Burmese
syntax. Thus the ordinary reader will find the commentary more difficult to understand
than the Burmese poems commented on.
A word of praise is due to the Editor for his scholarly edition of the work.
We
would like to know why the order of the three verses of Poem 8 is altered in the
commentary.
