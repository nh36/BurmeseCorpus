When the First Anglo-Burmese War broke out in 1824 the Kubo
Valley lying between Manipur and the Chindwin river was a Burmese
possession having been conquered at an earlier date from Manipur. Gumbhir
Singh, Raja of Manipur, joined hands with the British in 1824 and by January
1826 recovered the Valley claiming it as a part of his ancestral property.
The British recognized the annexation of Kubo to Manipur but failed to make
the necessary provision in the Treaty of Yandabo (1826) in order to legalise
a Burmese recognition of the same. Soon after the Treaty was sealed the
Burmese protested against the British attitude towards Kubo and sent two
missions to Calcutta laying claim to the Valley as being a part of ancient
Burma. The Governor-General at first ignored the Burmese claims and
decided afresh in favour of Gumbhir Singh. Henry Burney, the British
Resident at Ava, on his arrival at the Burmese capital (1830), gave the
Ministers of the King to understand that there was no hope of ever recover-
ing the Valley. Within less than two years of his stay in Ava, however,
discovering that Bagyidaw's heart was set upon the Valley, Burney changed
his mind, and advised the Governor-General, Lord Bentinck, to return the
Valley to Burma on the principle of expediency.

Governinent advancing arguments in favour of Burmese claims. One of his
arguments was a map (herewith reproduced) of the dominions of the

by Dr. Buchanan in 1795. A copy of this interesting map he later published
in the Edinburgh Philosophical Journal Vol. 2, No. 3, page 89, 14 October."

"During my residence in the Empire of Ava from the 19th of March
until the 27th of November 1795 I procured from the natives several maps.
of their country.
"The map • which now I publish, contains a general view of all the
territory that has been in any degree subject to King of Ava.
"The map accompanying this I procured from a man in great poverty,
who said, that at the time he was a slave of the Einshemayn the heir
apparent of the kingdom, and who probably had been reduced to the servile
state by debt, as his intelligence and manner denoted a person who had held
considerable rank, and received a good education. Before he succeeded so
far as he has done, he made several attempts with less success; and the
nature of our maps together with the manner of laying down places by
bearing and distance had been repeatedly explained to him.
"In this map the then boundary of the Empire of Ava is marked by
interrupted lines, while the country properly belonging to each subjugated
nation is distinguished by dotted lines. Mountains are represented by a
straight line like the plain on which they stand, while a waved line represents
their summits.
"Cities in the map are denoted by squares, those which have been the
seat of Empire having within a small square or dot, and those which are the
seat of tributary princes having within a cross.

That Buchanan obtained more than one map of the Burmese dominions
drawn by Burmans is confirmed by Symes. In his "Embassy to Ava" Symes
prefixes a map of Burma and concerning it he says in his preface:
-
"I am obliged to the kindness of Mr. Dalrymple for the construction of
the General Map prefixed to this Work, which has been compiled from the
materials collected by Dr. Buchanan and transmitted to the Court of
Directors". In the same place Symes also quotes Dalrymple's note on the
subject of the geography of Burma :-
"This part of Indian geography has hitherto remained in inexplicable
obscurity, and although much light has been thrown on the subject in
consequence of the Embassy
.. from the great mass of native

the Embassy in a medical capacity, have accumulated from various persons.
