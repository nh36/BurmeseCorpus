I believe every one of us is familiar with the story of Maung Pauk
Kyaing. To save his own life, to win the hand of a princess and to
become the lord of a kingdom he had to solve riddles. This story
belongs to the legendary period of the history of our country. From
this I may be allowed to surmise that the making and solving of riddles
is as old as the story of the Burman.
However the word "sagahta" (omcos), the Burmese equivalent
for riddle, can be traced only to the early 16th. century. It occurs in
verse 100 of Thahtegan Pyo of Shin Aggasamadhi, finished in 1527
A.D. The etymology of the word has long been forgotten and was
guessed at by the Kyaw Aung San Hta Sayadaw II in 1769, but the
result is not very satisfactory.
4
From different sources of literary evidence it appears that riddles
were very popular in the 16th. century, which, besides being the
fountain-head, was the golden age of Burmese literature. Shin
Silavamsa had his name couched in a riddle at the end of his
Taungdwinla Pyo. As a matter of fuct the whole Pyo is a series of
riddles piled up into an intriguing poem. Shin Ratthasara, one of his
contemporaries and a junior, also had his name concealed in a charade
in the epilouge of his Kogan Pyo. Wun Taung Lai Phongyi, another
fellow-poet, made riddles of a month, a flower and a lover in one of
his charming lyrics. 5 Most probably Shin Uttamagyaw also intended
a riddle when he talked about Arahatship as the triple edged
sword 6 in his famous Tawla. Riddle making, however, was viewed
with disfavour among the members of the Sangha, at least in some

Tradition says that riddles were used during the courtship of young
people. The lads and lasses tried each other's wits by setting and
solving riddles. By this method, we may presume, many a girl had

the Ramayana, Rama Yagan, U Toe introduced a riddle of love-making
in the abduction scene of Sita by Ravana on the aerial car. 9 This
may prove that riddle-making (among young lovers) was still popular

this place where the butea tree now stands."

ကမ္ဘာ့ စည်ကြီးလည်း တီးလိမ့်မည်။
မျက်နှာမဲကြီးလည်း ငိုလိမ့်မည်။
သေသူများလည်း ရှင်လိမ့်မည်။
ပုန်းသူများလည်း ပေါ်လိမ့်မည်။
The Mighty
Drum will roar:
The Dark Face pour
Its tears the Dead
Will live the Hid
Will come to light.
The solution is the coming of the rains at the end of the hot
weather. The Mighty Drum here stands for thunder. The association
between the two is quite natural. But Burmese literature seems to owe
this conception to the Holy Pitakas, which say that at the beginning of
every rainy season a battle is fought between the Sakka and the Asuras
on the slopes of the Mt. Meru. During the fight war drums are
sounded on both sides. In our childhood days we were often told
that the thunder was the sound of those celetial instruments. After
the thunder the rain falls. After the rain the grass grows. Then the
frogs and the toads come out from their hiding places and enjoy the
monsoon.
