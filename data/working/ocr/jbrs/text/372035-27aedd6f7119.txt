OUTLOOK FOR BURMA'S BALANCE
OF PAYMENTS ¹
by
U TUN WAI, M.A., Ph.D. 2
Burma's balance of payments was unfavourable in the early post-
war years of 1945/46 to 1947/48, but since 1949, it has been favour-
able. The surplus in 1950 was about 23 million kyats which increased
to 189 million kyats in 1951 and to 270 million kyats in 1952. In
spite of the recent surpluses, it is necessary to study the future for
Burma's balance of payments in order to determine whether the
country's foreign assets are sufficient for financing economic develop-
ment and for reserves.
The balance of payment deficits in the early post-war years was
inevitable as there had been considerable destruction during the war
and large imports of capital and consumer goods were needed for re-
construction and rehabilitation. On the other hand it is open to
question whether the commercial, monetary and fiscal policies adopted
in 1947 and subsequent years were the most appropriate ones. The
major weapon used to combat the early balance of payment deficits
was import and exchange controls and the government passed the
Imports Control Act and the Exchange Control Act in 1947. The
monetary policy, if it can be described as such, was one of maintain-
ing Burma on the sterling exchange standard at the pre-war parity
of 1 shilling 6 pence to one rupee, and the currency in circulation
increased or decreased according to changes in the foreign assets. The
fiscal policy was one of trying to achieve budget surpluses or at least
to reduce deficits to the minimum. The combined effect of the three
policies was to limit and cut down total national expenditure within
the country and therefore brought about a deflation. The only ex-
ception was the import and exchange controls which had an infla-
tionary aspect, through the reduction of imported goods and the
speculative motive. The deflationary effect was certainly not desirable
considering that there were unemployed resources and labour in Burma
in 1947/48 when real output was only 68 per cent of pre-war.
The surpluses in balance of payments from 1949 to 1952 have
been due to a combination of factors, the most important of which
is the deflationary impact of the commercial, monetary and fiscal
policies which has brought about international equilibrium, but at
low levels of output. Other factors include the slow implementation
of government's development programme, the favourable terms of trade
in post-war years, the nationalization of most of the export trade
through state marketing boards, and the fact that most of the flight
of capital from Burma had already taken place before trade and ex-
change controls were introduced.

The post-war shortages of rice caused its price to increase from a
little over £20 per ton in pre-war years to £35 and £40 per ton in the
early post-war years. In later years, the price increased still further,
and even the government to government contracted price increased
from £50 to £55 per ton in the second half of 1952, and to £60 per
ton in early 1953. The free market price of rice in 1952 was between
£70 and £90 per ton. Owing to such favourable prices, Burma's ex-
port earnings from rice have increased manifold, even though quan-
tum exports in post-war years have been less than half that of pre-war.
On the other hand, the government through the State Agricultural
Marketing Board, has maintained the internal price at a constant level
of K 300 per 100 baskets of paddy. The difference has been the
profits of the SAMB which have accrued to the government. The
gross incomes of the cultivators have therefore not increased as much
as the increase in international prices. Considering that in postwar
years there has been a reduction in agricultural rents and low interest
charged on government loans etc., the net incomes of the cultivators
are probably about the same as in pre-war. Had the net incomes of
the cultivators increased, the demand for imports would have been
larger. In spite of the considerable relaxation of import controls since
1950/51, imports under the OGL amounted to only 50 per cent of total
imports. On the other hand owing to the insurgents, the government
could not implement its development programme, and therefore, it
was not spending as much as it was receiving in taxes and profits from
the state trading enterprises.
Another effect of the nationalization of the rice export trade is
that the profits from the rice trade now remain within the country.
In pre-war years, foreigners earned the major portion of the profits
of this trade and these were remitted abroad.
Burma has had very favourable terms of trade in post-war years.
The index of the unit value of exports based on pre-war have increased
to 679 in 1950/51 and to 817 in 1951/52, while the unit value index
of imports increased to only 293 in 1950/51, and to 331 in 1951/52.
It is the favourable terms of trade which explains Burma's favourabie
balance of trade and payments, as both quantum exports and imports
have been much lower in postwar years with a greater decline in
quantum exports. In 1950/51 the index of quantum exports was 38
as against 49 for quantum imports.
In the early postwar years of 1946 and 1947, there was no ex-
change control, and foreigners could transfer as much money as they
wished. Even after exchange control was established, it was not so
rigidly applied in the first year. During 1946/47 and 1947/48 there
was considerable repatriation, especially to India, of foreign capital
which could not be transferrred during the war. The repatriated
foreign capital was about 304 million kyats in 1946/47 and 391 million
kyats in 1947/48 as compared with 18 million kyats in 1938/39. In
1948/49 largely due to exchange control and also as a lot of foreign
capital had aiready been repatriated, the amount totalled only 32
million kyats. By 1950/51 it had declined to 15 million kyats.

balance of payments, let us see whether it will continue to be favour-
able. The key factors which will affect Burma's balance of payments
in future are the Government's economic development programme, the
marginal propensity to import, the terms of trade, the elasticity of
supply of domestic goods especially from the export sector, the out-
look of foreign investment and disinvestment, and repayment of foreign
debts which Burma has incurred in the past.
In 1952, the Knappen Tippetts Abbett Engineering Company
together with their associated companies had drawn up, for the
government, a programme of economic development calling for 7,500
million kyats of investment in all fields including the private sector.
This investment is to be spread over eight years so that by 1959/60
capital equipment would not only be restored to pre-war but enlarged
to provide extra capital for the increased population. The real per
capita national income and standard of living would thus be the same
as in pre-war. The government has accepted this programme in
principle at the Pyidawtha conference held in August 1952, but for
the first two years it has decided on an investment programme of
1,000 million kyats of which one third or 350 million kyats will be
in foreign exchange. The direct foreign exchange requirements of
the plan in the first two years will therefore be about 175 million kyats
per year and for the eight years' period about 300 million kyats per
year. The plan will gather momentum in the later years and larger
investment will be needed. To this, must be added the foreign ex-
change requirements for larger imports arising from larger national
income. This additional requirement of foreign exchange will depend
on the marginal propensity to import. There is no ready information
on Burma's marginal propensity to import. It is also difficult to make
estimates because of import controls. However, a first approximation
may be made and we may call our estimate “the gross marginal pro-
pensity to import."
In 1947/48, when real output increased from 57 to 68 per cent
of pre-war, the gross marginal propensity to import was 19.4 per cent
of national income. In 1950/51 real output again increased from 59
to 67 per cent of pre-war and the gross marginal propensity to import
was 38.3 per cent. The difference between the two marginal propen-
sities was entirely due to relaxation of import controls in 1950/51, as
in both years the price changes were slight. In both years, the true
marginal propensities to import were suppressed though it was less
suppressed in 1950/51 than in 1947/48.
At 1950/51 prices, national income is expected to reach 5,500
million kyats by 1956 which is equivalent to pre-war output and to
7,000 million kyats by 1959/60. Considering that output in 1950/51
was 3,664 million kyats, this will mean an average annual increase in
national income of about 400 million kyats during the eight year period.
This increase includes the value of the new investment as well as its
induced income effect. Since the foreign exchange content of the new
investment has already been calculated separately, the value of the new
investment will have to be deducted from the national income to arrive
at the increase in induced income. If this is done we get an average
annual increase in induced income of 300 million kyats per year during
the eight year period. As the gross marginal propensity to import is

taken as about one-third, the foreign exchange requirements for induced
imports might on the average be about 100 million kyats per year. The
total foreign exchange implications of the developmental programme on
this basis would be of the order of 400 million kyats per year.
The development programme, of course, has its impact on the balance.
of payments, both through the propensity to import and through the
elasticity of supply of goods from the export sector. In pre-war years,
exports were 34 per cent of national income. In the early postwar
years of 1946/47 and 1947/48 it was much reduced owing to the greater
decline in the export industries e.g., rice, petroleum, timber, and mining
than in industries catering mainly for the domestic market. The rice
industry catered for both the export and domestic markets, but as
domestic requirements had to be fulfilled before exports could take
place, the exports of rice declined much more than output. In later
postwar years the importance of exports increased again and in 1951/52
was 27 per cent of national income. The later increase in importance
of exports was mainly due to the rapid increase in prices of agricultural
commodities especially of rice during the Korean war boom; quantum
exports were still only about 40 to 50 per cent of pre-war.
By 1959/60, when real national income in Burma increases to about
25 per cent above pre-war, one might expect that quantum exports
will at least be restored to pre-war; though there will be larger domes-
tic consumption resulting from population increases of about 25 per
cent between pre-war and 1959/60. The question is what will be the
unit value of exports by that time. The price of rice will have declined
from the abnormal levels of recent years to perhaps about 60 per cent
of the 1950/51 peak. The quantum exports of other commodities.
would have recovered, but their prices, e.g. petroleum, did not increase
as much as that of rice, and therefore will not decline as much. The
total value of exports in 1959/60 is not likely to be much different from
that of 1950/51 but it may increase by about 10 per cent.
Foreign investment in Burma may increase but it is not likely to
be large, and in any case it will definitely be smaller than the repatria-
tion of foreign capital which will take place when the land nationaliza-
tion programme is implemented and compensation paid to the foreign
landlords. Considering that foreigners own about a quarter of the rice
growing land in Burma and calculating compensation at the govern-
ment fixed rate of twelve times the land revenue on rice land, the
foreign exchange liability of this programme will be about 100 million
kyats. It is possible of course that the government may pay in govern-
ment bonds and may permit transfer of capital in instalments only.

reconstruct the changes in the main items in the balance of payments
position for 1959/60. The changes will be given with reference to the
average of the eight years between 1952 and 1960. On the receipts.
side, value of exports may increase by 100 million kyats, while non-

trade items, consisting mainly of foreign investment, both governmental
and private may increase by 50 million kyats. On the payments side,
however, imports of goods for economic development and for consump-
tion will increase by about 400 million kyats per year; while on non-
trade items, considering the increase in government expenditure, private.
remittances, etc., they may increase by about another 100 million kyats
per year. Therefore, the balance of payment position will deteriorate
between now and 1959/60 on an average of about 350 million kyats
per year. Since in 1952 there was a surplus of about 270 million kyats,
and as in actual fact the deficits will be smaller in the earlier years,
Burma will start having a deficit in balance of payments in 1954. In
1953, the surplus should be smaller than the one in 1952.
The above projection has not taken the foreign debt position into
account as its final fate is not yet clear. At the end of 1952, Burma
had foreign debts totalling 855 million kyats. Of this amount, the debt
to the United States on lend lease account is negligible and is being
repaid in local currency. The debt to the United Kingdom amounting
to 356 million kyats was incurred prior to independence for certain
expenditures of the British Military Administration in Burma during the
postwar years of 1945/46, 1946/47 and part of 1947/48. Part of it is
for some military equipment taken over by the Burma government
when Burma was given independence. According to Article 6 of the
Nu-Attiee Agreement, this interest free debt must be paid by Burma
in twenty equal instalments commencing from April 1952. The gov-
ernment of Burma has not begun repaying as it feels that it is not in a
position to do so. In 1952, the government sent a mission to discuss
the scaling down of the debt. The question was to have been dis-
cussed again when Burma sent some representatives to attend the
coronation. The debt to India was incurred in pre-war years when
on separation from India, the Burma Government inherited the assets.
and liabilities in Burma of the Government of India. All of the assets
consisting mainly of railways and other public property have been
damaged or destroyed during the war. It is understood that discussions
between the governments of Burma and India are to be held in the
near future on the question of repayment.
The question of the adequacy of the present foreign exchange
reserves of around 1,000 million kyats can now be answered. These
reserves are sufficient to take care of seasonal requirements and possi-
bility also for part of the foreign exchange needed for internal stability
during cyclical fluctuations. They are definitely not sufficient to satisfy
the foreign exchange requirements of the eight year economic develop-
ment programme. Nevertheless, this does not mean that part of the
foreign exchange reserves should not be used for financing economic
development. They should be used and it is better that the foreign
exchange reserves be utilized so that investment takes place internally
than abroad. In fact in the context of the present situation in Burma,
the monetary and fiscal policies should be re-examined if foreign
exchange reserves do continue to accumulate.

In our analysis above, however, we have given reasons for think-
ing that foreign exchange reserves will not continue to accumulate
and that by 1954 Burma should have balance of payments deficit.
The main reasons are the impact of the development programme
which will be implemented as peace and order is restored and also
the great likelihood of a sharp deterioration in terms of trade in the
next year or two. People should not be alarmed when in 1954 the
balance of payments turns from a surplus to a deficit. But one thing,
however, is very clear, and that is, unless larger amounts of foreign
investment are forthcoming, either Burma's economic development
programme will have to be cut or controls will have to be used to
cut down domestic consumption.
