A POLICY FOR HIGHWAY TRANSPORT IN BURMA
by
Virgil D. Cover, Ph. D. 2.
In recent years the transport situation in Burma has been discussed
in at least two important reports. The K. T. A. report of 1953 3
presented a rather extensive survey of transportation, and of the legal
framework under which transportation was carried on. The Locklin
report of 1955 4 was primarily an analysis of the rate structures of
Burma Railways and of the Inland Water Transport Board, but it also
addressed the larger problem of regulation. Certain recommendations
were made by both reports and the passage of time has only served to
give point to the recommendations. It is proposed to examine a part
of the ground again.
The Organization of Transport in Burma
Burma's internal transport presents a "mixed situation" to say the
least. We have a railway system of 1787 route miles and 2219 track
miles 5 which is owned by the Government. Railway transport in
Burma is a government monopoly. The railways are immensely
important in Burma and their ability to carry on their work efficiently
should be jealously guarded.
Inland water transport in Burma is also of tremendous importance.
There are some points which are reached by no other form of transport.
The delta area represents an almost unique opportunity for inland water
transport. In few countries in the world is such a great portion of the
nation's commerce carried by this means.
The Inland Water Transport Board is a government board, but
unlike the railways, it does not have a monopoly of its kind of trans-
port. Instead, privately owned craft compete with the craft of the
Inland Water Transport Board, and such craft also operate in areas not
served by I. W. T. B. There are also privately owned craft which
carry for their owners. Thus, much of the inland water transport field
is an area of competition in which the allocation of the nation's
resources depend on private initiative and in which many of the rates
for carriage are likewise in the private domain.

The airways in Burma are, like the railways, a government
monopoly. The Union of Burma Airways is the sole operator within
the nation. Some air transport is provided by company owned and
other private aircraft but the amount is small.
Road, or highway transport in Burma is largely an unknown
quantity. The fact that a Road Transport Board existed leads the
newcomer to believe that the Union Government conducted a high-
way transport business in the same way it conducts rail, water, and air
transportation. The Road Transport Board, however, existed for the
purpose of providing and maintaining motor vehicles for the govern-
ment and its agencies. It carried on no transportation business. Instead,
the business of highway transportation has been and is in the hands of
private enterprise. Some vehicle owners carry their own commodities
as a part of another business. Some offer their vehicles for hire.
However, there is no such thing as common carriage by motor truck,
the carriers being under no legal obligation to accept commodities for
movement, to operate on routes other than those they choose, to
operate at times other than those they choose, or to remain in service
if they wish to withdraw. There is no legal responsibility on the part
of motor carriers to offer satisfactory service and no accountability if
they do not. Their rates may be on a monopolistic level, and on the
other hand they may indulge in cutthroat competition with other
carriers and force rates to an unremunerative level. 6
The Union of Burma Shipping Board conducts coastal shipping in
the name of the Burmese Government. At present the Board does not.
have sufficient vessels to handle all of Burma's coastal shipping and
vessels of other nations are permitted to enter the businees after
securing a licence from the Nautical Adviser. When the Board acquires
sufficient tonnage it will follow the practice of other nations of
reserving Burma's coastal trade for vessels under the Burmese flag.

At one time Burma also had another form of transport: pipeline.
Petroleum was carried from the Chauk fields to the refinery at Syriam.
At present this movement is carried by the Inland Water Transport
Board, and it was the recommendation of K. T. A. that this service be

the pipeline the extent of the damage to which is known only to be

In summary, the different forms of transportation in Burma are
under quite diverse systems of ownership and management. We have
complete government monopoly with the railways and the airways. We
have government ownership in the inland waterways and in ocean
shipping, but there is substantial competition from private craft on the
inland waters, and the coastal movement is shared with foreign vessels.
The highway transport business is occupied by private enterprise, there
being both privately owned vehicles which carry for hire, and privately
owned vehicles which carry on the business of their owners.
The results have been unhappy, particularly for the government
owned railway and inland water transportation agencies. Highway
transportation is a competitor the strength of which is not known. It
is a difficult competitor to meet because its manner of business is
informal, and subject to change without notice. It has few of the
characteristics of stable public service organizations. The railways
especially have felt the force of this competition, the annual reports of
Burma Railways making reference to highway competition in many
years.

Burma's Interest in a Highway Transport Policy

The transportation industry occupies an important segment of any
nation, and Burma is no exception. Transportation is a public service
industry and its failure to perform effectively will amount to a greater
misfortune than the failure of an industry outside the public service
category.
The provision of good transportation implies much for the
commerce of a nation, for its postal service, its national defence,
and its general welfare. In Burma where we have such a large public
investment in transport plant, we might add the preservation of this
investment as one of the purposes of a transportation policy, although
such a purpose should be supported only with definite reservations.
9
It is estimated in the United States that one sixth of the cost of
goods purchased is made up of cost of transportation and the propor-
tion in many industries runs as high as 25 to 30 per cent. In Burma
where so much of what is consumed is imported it is probable that
tranportation cost would be even more important. It would also be
more important in Burma because it is a higher percentage in the case
of products of the extractive industries than in the case of manufactured
products. It is thus to Burma's interest to have the most efficient
transport system possible.
Burma's postal service uses highway transport only in the remote
areas where there is no other form of transport. This is a logical
development, particularly when so many of the communities can be
reached only by highway. It will only be a matter of time, however,
until mail will be carried by highway between the important cities of
Burma. Rolling post offices will speed up mail delivery very substan-
tially, and these will follow shortly after transport of mail by truck or
bus. Dependable, rapid communication through the postal service is
a minimum requirement to Burma's progress and highway transport
can play a large part.

Though Burma has a foreign policy of neutrality, her expenditures

own needs to some extent. However, in an emergency great reliance
will have to be placed on vehicles owned by others. If the vehicles of
private owners perform effectively in the interest of national defence
the military authorities may be expected to leave the control of those
vehicles with the private owners. If the industry does not perform
effectively in an emergency, the military may be expected to cover the
industry into the military organization as soon as a breakdown becomes

The general welfare may be described in any terms one may choose
to use. It may imply political unity in which we try to convince those
in northern Burma that national policy is drawn for them as well as
for the residents of Rangoon. Many parts of Burma can be reached
only by highway, or by air. Highway transport will provide access to
the more remote areas and will bind them to the more populous
centres of the Union. Though at the present time the highway may be
crude and the ox-cart the only wheeled vehicle, as time passes improve-
ments will be made in both road and vehicles and the ties between
distant places will be strengthened.
The general welfare may also imply social intercourse for reasons
other than those with commercial or political connotations. Burma
has many places of historical interest and many religious shrines which,
under ideal conditions, could be visited more easily by passenger car
than by any other means. The coming years will see a substantial
development of the tourist industry in Burma with both Burmese and
others participating.
For the reasons given it is important that Burma do whatever is
necessary to assure that good transportation will be provided. Depend-
able service for the carriage of both passengers and freight is a
minimum requirement. Dependable service rests on facilities that are
adequate from a number of points of view. First, the facilities must
be sufficient in amount. Burma's bus service does not meet this
requirement at present. Second, because transportation experiences

The Different Transport Agencies Compared
Having each agency of transport perform the services for which
each is best suited has long been a goal in all countries where the
problem of a proper transport policy has been addressed. You may
go back over twenty five years and find that students in the United
States, faced with the rise of highway and air transportation, were
manufacturing such phrases as: we must guide the development of
transportation so that each agency will perform that service for which
it is best suited. It was in connection with transportation that the
word "coordination" came to be so widely used that eventually it had
almost no meaning at all.
However, there is little question that railway transport is more
efficient than other forms in performing certain services. Large ship-
ments of bulky goods, particularly over long distances are best handled
by railway, or by water transport. The railway has a particular
advantage when it comes to reducing the cost of transportation in that
a train is like an accordion given a locomotive and a brake van, there
is much less than a proportionate increase in cost as additional wagons
or carriages are included in the train. On the other hand, the railway
is slow, and it is costly on short hauls and for small shipments.
Water transport resembles rail in that it excels for mass move-
ments. It has the
It has the further advantage that it uses a highway that is

needs a great deal of expenditure on it and therefore is not free, but it
remains true that many waterways operate with relatively little expendi-
ture for a right of way.
Motor transport competes directly with rail transport on many
hauls, and less directly with water and air transportation. As high-
ways are improved in Burma and as more vehicles are imported,

competition will become more vigorous. Though all forms of transport
would participate in increased business activity, the share of motor
transport would probably increase faster than that of the others. This
would result from the undeniable advantages of motor transportation.
Railways and waterways are tied to the location of their roadbed
and their channels and it is unlikely that the mileage of these will be
increased greatly. On the other hand, automobiles will go wherever
there is a highway, and in fact, they often make their own highways.
They are flexible in use and are able by themselves to provide a
complete service from consignor to consignee, and by doing so they
reduce time in transit. They also reduce the costs of handling because
there are no transfers from one carrier to another, or fewer transfers.
Damage to goods is reduced, and the amount of packing that is
required is also reduced.
Motor transport is expensive by comparison with rail and water
however, because each vehicle is a unit in itself and must be individually
propelled by a power unit, and individually driven by a human being.
A railway can add wagons or carriages to a train, and inland water
transport can add barges to a barge tow, but additional capacity is
available in highway transport only by adding a complete carrying unit.
The outstanding advantage of air transport is, of course, its speed.
It is also the only form of transport that can reach many remote
sections, although some sort of landing field is required in all cases.
The vehicle for air transport is an expensive one and, as in motor
transport, additional carrying capacity* requires an additional carrying
unit. Air transport is premium transport, and will remain so for some
time to come. In Burma the unusual demand for air transport has
given it an impetus that has made it a profitable industry; a financial
status achieved by few of the world's airways.
Some Plans for Highway Transport
How can Burma best guide the development of her transportation
facilities so as to get the greatest possible use out of her existing
resources, and to direct any additions to her resources to those facilities
which will contribute most to Burma's economic and social development?
One possible course of action, and one not seriously proposed here
is that a policy be adopted of protection of the government's invest-
ment in the railways, waterways and airways. For-hire transport by

Another course of action would be to convert Burma Railways into

permit would be based on a showing that public convenience and
necessity would be served by the proposed facilities and service.
While the foregoing do not exhaust the possibilities, they give a
basis for seeking a solution. What are the merits and weaknesses of
each proposal?
An Evaluation of the Plans
The prohibition of use of the highway by for-hire transport would
be a mistake. Though the government's investment in rail, water, and
air transport might initially receive some protection from such action,
it would be necessary to prohibit even private ownership of motor
vehicles if the prohibition were to be effective. Otherwise, private
ownership of highway transportation vehicles would be given an unusual
stimulus and private owners would do what for-hire operators were
prohibited from doing.

This proposal also merits rejection on the ground that highway
transport has definite advantages over other forms and to attempt to
handicap it would be to prejudice Burma's progress. In addition a
prohibition of this sort would only delay the emergence of the industry.
Highway transport is a dynamic, volatile, industry and attempts which
have been made to throttle it have eventually failed.
How about converting Burma Railways into a Burma Land
Transport Board? What would happen if this were done?
First, the two agencies, highway and rail, might be administered
so that each would perform the functions for which each is best
adapted. Long haul transport, and transport of bulky commodities
would be a function of the railroad. Commodities requiring premium
service would move by highway. The Land Transport Board would
make the decision as to which would be used.
Second, highway transport might be used as a feeder to the rail-
way. The railway at present falls far short of covering all the inland
points in Burma, and these points are not served by either the Inland
Water Transport Board, or the Union of Burma Airways. If they are
to be served at all it must be by highway. In view of the general
scarcity of resources of all kinds, it makes sense to allocate on a
scarcity basis those which Burma does have. It must be kept in mind,
however, that the use of the railway for trunk line haulage and the use
of the highway as a feeder would mean that extra costs would be
involved. It would be necessary to transfer goods from truck to rail
wagon at the rail line and another transfer from rail wagon to local
delivery vehicle would be required at destination. These costs would
have to be offset by the economies of the rail haul. If this happened,
the joint service would mean an economical use of Burma's scarce
resources.
This plan is not without its shortcomings. In the first place, it
would eliminate the competition which now exists between rail and
highway transport. This competition is not without its ill effects, and
not all of it can be termed "fair competition," but much of it is of a
'salutary nature and it should not be discarded without serious delibera-
tion. Competition can lead to better service at lower rates. High-
way competition has resulted in improvements in railway service and
lower rates wherever it has existed.

In the second place, there is serious doubt that any Burma Land
Transport Board which might be established would give highway
transport a fair opportunity to develop. Instead, highway transport
would be placed in a position subordinate to the railway. This would
happen because the majority of the personnel of any Burma.
Land Transport Board would be drawn from the previously existing
Burma Railways Board. Such personnel would be accustomed to
thinking in terms of conducting the transportation business by railway.
Highway transportation would be strange to them and it would not be
impossible that they would even be antagonistic to the use and develop-
ment of trucks and buses.
The preceding thought may seem to be unduly pessimistic and
even unfair to a fine body of railway people. It is suggested, however,
that had the development of highway transport in the United States
been left to railway management, the highways would definitely have
played a subordinate role. The evidences of "coordinated" transport
in the United States under which railways and trucks and buses work
together have come only after the advantages of coordination have
been so obvious that they could not be denied. Those few railroad
men who experimented in the use of highway transport were regarded
by the majority of their colleagues as heretics and even as plotting the
downfall of the rail transportation system. The "piggy-back"
arrangement under which a highway trailer is loaded on a low-sided
railway wagon, and the trailer and its contents are hauled to destina-
tion, was adopted generally by American railroads in 1954 although
a few venturesome railroads had introduced the plan before World
War II. Many of the railroads which finally adopted theplan in 1954
imposed conditions for its use that restrict measurably the extent of its
use.
Though the idea of a Burma Land Transport Board has many
appealing features it is rejected because it is not believed that it will
result in the most efficient use of land transport facilities, and that it
will not insure the progress which is desired of transport on Burma's
highways.

Private initiative and free enterprise are at their best in highway
transport and the threat of large, monopolistic concerns is remote.
Highway transport is also a fluid, flexible industry. It serves best
when its decisions can be made from moment to moment, and its
effectiveness would be much diminished if it were to be put into a
framework of institutional procedures, whether this happened in Burma
or in any other country. Highway transport will move perishables
such as fruits and vegetables and milk. Highway transport makes
possible the carrying on of merchandising with small store inventories.
An immediate response to the need for service is required where the
margin for survival in business is a narrow one, and because this margin
is narrow in merchandising, the merchant is placing greater and greater
. reliance on rapid highway transport.
A Recommendation
On balance it is believed that Burma's needs will be served best if
her highway transport is left in private hands. If this is done,
however, Burma should do it only after having decided that it is the
best course to follow. The highway transport industry and its
competitors, particularly Burma Railways, are all entitled to know
what to expect for the future.
This is not to say that the highway industry should simply be
allowed to drift unguided. Recommendations for the control of road
transport were included in both the K. T. A. and the Locklin reports
and there does not appear to be any reason for deviating from them.
Either or both of them included recommendations regarding safety,
weight, and tax programs, and regarding economic regulation.
Measures to promote safety have to do not only with loss of life
and damage to property, but are related also to the necessity for
maintaining an adequate rate structure. Weight regulation is designed
to conserve the highways of the nation but they also are related to the
necessity for maintaining an adequate rate structure, An adequate tax
program is designed to insure that those who use the highways as a
place of business shall make a proper contribution to the government
for the use of the highways. Again, the ability to make such a con-
tribution depends on an adequate rate structure. It might not be
amiss to comment here that one of the most controversial problems in
transportation in the United States is that of measuring the amount

The basis for economic regulation is the right to operate. The
K. T. A. report spoke of this right as resting on a permit", which

lower than railway rates. Burma Railways comments on the destructive
competition the truckers present, but because truck service is so far
superior to rail service, truck rates can be and are higher than railway
rates. However, information on rates is sketchy as no tariffs are
published, and at least one trucking association takes the attitude that
its rate schedules are secret.

transport industry. Conducting such a survey would present real
problems. Because the industry is a small-scale one, no one operator
would be typical of the industry, and to get a reliable sample it would
be necessary to make a great many contacts. Because no business man

The analysis of the foregoing information and its use so far as
transport policy is concerned should be the responsibility of a Transport
Commission. A recommendation that such a Commission be set up
in the Ministry of Transport and Communications was included in
both the K. T. A. and the Locklin reports and the recommendation is
repeated here. The K. T. A. report described the Commission's
functions as follows: "I
"To encourage fair and healthy competition between the
several transport agencies, to control their standards of service,
to insure coordinated services and economic use of joint
facilities, and to control rates in the interest of the public,
there must be a regulating commission, free of operating
responsibilities and pressures, and staffed and equipped to
analyse the social, economic, and technical aspects of the
national transport problems. "
Burma is now only at the beginning in her consideration of a high-
way transport policy. It may be a matter either of encouragement or
of discouragement to her if she examined the experience of both the
United States and Great Britain in this same area. Britain nationalised
much of her highway transport, and now is in the process of de-nationa-
lising it. The United States is now in the process of considering
recornmendations made by a committee of President Eisenhower's
cabinet on Transport Organization and Policy. Many of the recom-
mendations had to do with highway transport and its place in the
whole transport framework. Though Burma may do the best she can
to solve her transport problem for today, "she may be certain that
it will constantly present new aspects. Transportation is a dynamic
industry and progress in this area will require constant vigilance.
