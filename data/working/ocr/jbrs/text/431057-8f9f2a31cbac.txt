PROPOSED DATING OF THE YONOK-CHIENGSAEN DYNASTY
by
Kaehorn Sukhabanij, National Library, Bangkok
The history of Thailand is, within the last thirty years, divided into three periods, beginning
with Sukhodaya period in the 13th. century, Ayudhaya period in the 14th. century and
Bangkok period from the 18th. century to the present day.
A proposal has been made in 1958 in a book called Thailand by the Human Relations
Area Files of three American Universities (Cornell, Yale and Bennington College) to prefix
Thailand history with the Nanchao period of the 7th. century. Whether Nanchao was a
Thai state, or a Union of Thai-Lolo-Naga states is still a moot point, but her culture was
predominantly Chinese.
In this paper, I beg leave to present to you an unquestionable Thai state, having been
founded as an off-shoot of the Kingdom of Tien centuries before the rise of Nanchao, but
possessing native data in a continuous form up to the foundation of Ayudhaya in 1350.
This was the first Chiengsaen dynasty, sometimes called Yonok-Chiengsaen to link it with
her original site of Yonok Naga Nagara believed to be the present Naung Lom, a few miles
south-west of Chiengsaen.
A word about the town of Chiengsaen. Chiengsaen is now a district of Chiengrai,
but possessing old ruins of archaeological value generally of the 15th. century or later. It
was rebuilt by Mengrai's grandson, by name of Saen-p’u in A.D. 1287 from the old ruin of
Ngoen-Yang the second Chieng-saen dynasty. The name Chieng-saen, though connected
with her founder Saen-p'u, is a link with Ngoen-Yang, a few miles to the East of it, and
also with Yonok, some miles south-west of it. In old records, we often come across the
names of Yonok-Chiengsaen, Ngoen-Yang Chiengsaen, and also Chiengsaen Luang. These
three names are evidences of the three periods of Chiengsaen, though their locations are
a few miles away from one another.
The importance of Ngoen-Yang Chiengsaen is that it was the origin of Chiengmai
dynasty of the 13th. century, whose founder Mengrai was a contemporary of Ramkhamheng
of Sukhodaya. Professor Coedes has accepted this view in his book: Les Etats Hin-
douises D'Indochine et D'Indonesie, 1948, and has placed the town of Ngoen-Yang in his
map at the end of the book, in parenthesis for the present town of Chiengsaen. But of the
town of Yonok, nothing has been said. In our palm-leaf manuscripts and also in some
astrologers' notebooks, however, there occur the name of Yonok Chiengsaen and her famous
prince, Prince P'rom, with inference that it was from Prince P'rom that the Ayudhaya
dynasty derived her origin. We also have an independent source, a book written by a
French Ambassador to the Court of King Narai in 1687, M. de La Loubere, which con-
firmed this view. M. de La Loubere had this to say concerning the origin of the Siamese:

The year An. 1300 supplied by M. de La Loubere as the beginning of the reign of
Prince P'rom is of the Buddhist Era, equivalent to A.D. 757, and his royal seat was called
Jayaburi by this French source. In a palm-leaf manuscript written in the script and dialect
of Chiengman, the town founded by Prince P'rom was called Jayaprakara in the year 300 of
the Yonok Second Era. I beg leave to propose here that the year 300 of this Yonok Second
Era was the Buddhist Era, but omitting the thousandth-digit as was commonly practised
by old historians and epigraphists. Hence this native datum is confirmed by the independent
French source of M. de La Loubere.
To compare the founding of Jayaburi, or Jayaprakara, with that of Nanchao, we have
M. Emile Rocher's Histoire des Princes du Yunnan, T'oung Pao, Volume X, 1899, which
records the founding of the Ta-moung dynasty in Yunnan in A.D. 649 by Si-nou-lo, he
being the 37th. generation from Ti-moung. Prince Prom himself was the 41st. generation
from Prince Singhanawati, a son of a Thai Muang prince of Yunnan. It is proposed that
Ta-moung, Ti-Moung, and Thai Muang are synonymous, being the name of a group of
Thai tribes consolidating and emerging in history as the Kingdom of Tien in, -as M. Emile
Rocher puts it- the beginning of the Christian Era. As Nanchao was established in A.D.
649, we have the town of Jayaprakara founded by Prince P'rom in A.D. 757.
Prince P'rom was the second son of Prince P'ang, the 40th. ruler of Yonok. As his
elder brother was entitled to the throne of Yonok, Prince P'rom had to found himself a
town, hence the establishment of Jayaprakara, a day's distance south of Yonok, by the side
of the river Kok. There are two ruined towns by the side of the river Kok, one to the
East and one to the West of the present Chiengrai, but they have not been excavated.
Either one of these may be our town of Prince P'rom.
I beg to give a list of the rulers of Yonok, with the length of each reign, as recorded
in this palm-leaf manuscript. When we add these number of years together, we shall have
1,025 years as the span of Yonok existence. The city was finally destroyed by an earth-
quake in A. B. 1376, i.e. A.D. 833 The last ruler perished in the catastrophe, and
though a new town was set up to the East of the present Chiengsaen...called in the manus-
cript Vieng Pruk-sa no scion of the Singhanawati dynasty was available to rule. The Yonok-
Chiengsaen line was however continued by the Prince P'rom branch, and from him as
origin, we have King Narai of Ayudhaya whom M. de La Loubere named the 52nd gene-
ration. May I add that from King Narai to the present king of Thailand, we have again
sixteen rulers. So from Prince Singhanawati to the present day, we have in all 108 rulers,
starting with a small city-state in the province of Chiengrai in Saka 17, or A. D. 95, and
gradually emerged by the 13th. century as the dominant power over the Menam valley.

Too much emphasis has been made towards the Nanchao origin of the Thai of Thai-
land. From this paper you will see that an off-shoot of the Thai Muang group branched
out from Yunnan nearly 2,000 years ago, from which we possess the names of nearly all of
the 108 rulers, as well as their seats of government. Nanchao herself was founded in the
7th. century over 5 centuries after Yonok - and though her first dynasty was of the Thai
Muang group or Ta-Moung-we read in Rocher's article of a usurpation of the throne
in A.D. 903 by a Tcheng family, and then the founding of the Touan dynasty in A.D. 938,
another usurpation by a Yang family in 1082, the founding of the Kao dynasty in 1094, and
a reversion to the Touan dynasty again in 1097. It was under the Touan dynasty when
Nanchao fell to the attack of the famous Kublai Khan in A.D. 1253, but the Touan fami-
ly though not reinvested with kingship, was permitted to govern Tali as a vassal prince.
This Touan family continued to govern the family's domain until the year 1390 under the
Ming Period. Then other princes carried on as head of government for well-nigh 200 years
until Tali was annexed into the Chinese Empire by the Manchus in the 17th. century.
-
The belief that the fall of Nanchao to Kublai Khan in 1253 gave rise to the founding
of Sukhodaya in the Menam delta is not therefore supported by historical facts. The belief
that Yunnan was no more a land ruled by Thai princes after 1253 was also not supported
by historical facts. Yunnan was still a state, a vassal state, of the Chinese Empire, but go-
verned by Thai princes until the 17th. century, well-nigh 400 years after Kublai Khan con-
quered Nanchao.
I have appended my proposed dating of the Yonok dynasty in my Noes following the
list of the 43 rulers of that dynasty. My reason in discarding the figure of the combined
reign of 1,025 years for 43 rulers is that the average reign of 23.8 years for each ruler is too
long. The other reason is that in the text of the manuscript we have the "Saka Era 17”
as the starting point when Prince Singhanawati left his home to found a town of his own.
By clinging to this Saka Era 17 or A.D. 95, and noting the town of Yonok destroyed in
A.D. 833, we have a total of 738 years for 43 rulers, averaging just c
over 17 years for each
ruler. This figure compares well with the succeeding period, from Prince P'rom to King
Narai of Ayudhaya, where we have 52 rulers over a span of 932 years, averaging a little
under 18 years for each reign.
My proposed dating of the Yonok-Chiengsaen dynasty is from the year A.D. 95 to
the year A.D. 833. The first Yonok Era, as stated in the manuscript as Maha Sakaraja, is
taken prima facie as the Saka Era, and the second Yonok Era the Buddhist Era, with the
omission of the thousandth-digit.

7. One should mention the succession system of Yonok. With only one exception,
it was continuously from father-to-son. The exception was in Ruler No. 31,
who was a brother of No. 30. But for Ayudhaya period, this system was not
always followed. We have instances of a brother-in-law, a nephew, and very
often a brother of the deceased king who succeeded to the throne. This Ayudhaya
system was usually preceded by a palace war, between the king's son and his
uncle, or cousin. Usually the king's son was eliminated. There was only one
exception of this uncle-nephew feud,-King Narai, who defeated and eliminated
his uncle. Be that as it may, the father-to-son succession was not adopted in
the Bangkok period, until after the 1880's when the Front-Palace, signyfying the
Prince next in line to the throne, was abolished. During the first five reigns of
Bangkok, however, no Front-Palace princes outlived the reigning king.

8. The span of life, hence the span of reign, from father to son is usually longer
than that of from brother to brother ( or to cousin, or brother-in-law ).
this may account for the long average of 23. 8 years of the Yonok dynasty as
compared to the 18 years of the Ayudhaya period. If we are influenced by this
factor, and are willing to accept the 23. 8 years average, then we have also to
accept the span of 1,025 years of Yonok existence, with Yonok founding in the
year 192 B. C,-and a history of 1025 years with alas! no history. Yonok has
nothing to show us of any development, but a stagnant city-state, her kings
married within the ruling circles, and only sporadic founding of Buddhist
shrines. Also, if we accept this view, we shall be confronted with the Era Ques-
tion, the First Yonok Era, with its 17th, year in 192 B.C. One might well then
ask: What was this era?
