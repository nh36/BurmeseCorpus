THE EARLY HISTORY OF THE MALAY ARCHIPELAGO.*
The whole tropical Far East has much history in common. Even at the
present day there are features in the social life of different areas which point
to some connection between them from the very earliest times. The freedom
of women in Burma, for example, is proverbial, and is often ascribed to the
influence of Buddhism; but, in comparison with Java, a Mahommedan
country, where the women ride bicycles and use up-to-date calculating
machines in commercial offices, Burma is a back number. The essential
characters of land tenure throughout the Malay Archipelago are the customs
of dama-u-gya, thu-win-nga-t'wet and bo-ba-baing. The music of Java and
Burma are very similar and, in the Museum in Batavia, if not in the streets,
one can see chin-lon. These things go far back. Customs regarding land
tenure probably take root when a people is first settling down to agriculture;
and although there cannot well be any racial tie between the Javanese and
Burman of the present day it would seem not improbable that, before the
Christian era, the then inhabitants of Indonesia and Burma inherited a
common culture. Then, for a thousand years from the beginning of the
Christian era, colonists poured forth from India over the whole tropical Far
East. In Java, as in Burma, the rise of the Mongols brought this period to
an end. The shrines of Moslem saints, which dot the coast of Burma from
Arakan to Tenasserim, show that the Mahommedans who mastered the
Archipelago left their mark here also, and we still have our Arabs in Mergui.
After the Moslems came the Europeans; Portuguese, Dutch, French and
English. Thus, throughout the centuries, peoples similar in their original
culture have been subjected to similar influences from outside.
During the past two generations, the Dutch in Java have pieced together,
as in a jig-saw puzzle, the early history of Netherlands India and their work
has been summed up by Dr. N. J. Krom in his Hindoe-Javaansche
Geschiedenis (2nd. edition, 1931).¹ No student of the history of Burma can
afford to disregard it. But, as no translation is likely to be available, the
following summary may be of interest. Except where otherwise indicated,
all statements of fact are given on Dr. Krom's authority, and I have therefore
made no attempt to give detailed references.

recent discoveries show vestiges of human habitation and successive
migrations in the far remote past. It lies between the continents of Australia
and Asia and between the Pacific and Indian oceans, and has always formed

The earliest human remains are skulls showing an affinity with the
aborigines of Australia. Those skulls, with some Melanesian characters, are
found in the Malay Peninsula and Indo-China and justify the conjecture that
at one time Australo-Melanesian peoples were widely scattered over the
tropical Far East. There are traces also of Wedda and Negrito peoples,
who may be the actual ancestors of such jungle folk as the To-alas of the
Celebes and the Kubus of Sumatra, and have also left a fainter imprint on
the general Indonesian population. Much of this is conjectural, but we reach
firmer ground with a neolithic culture developed among the ancestors of the
Indonesians. Stone axes, of Indonesian origin, indicate four stages of
development; three of these are found over the whole Archipelago and the
fourth is found only in the West. A corresponding division between East
and West is indicated also on linguistic grounds. Another cultural division
between East and West may be found with reference to their boats. But in
this respect there is a feature of great interest and importance. Some
islanders use boats with outriggers, but in the East the boats have one outrigger
and in the West they have two; in the extreme West however, one again
finds boats with one outrigger. This suggests that the people using boats
with two outriggers were immigrants who drove their predecessors in both
directions. Some appear to have been driven so far west as Southern India,
where there are still tribes who seem to have survived from a great
Polynesian migration from the tropical East. It has been suggested that the
newcomers who used boats with two outriggers were Indonesians from the
mainland. Skulls of a pure Indonesian type have been found in Indo-China,
and also stone axes of the types associated with the Indonesians. Some
probably came round by way of the Malay Peninsula and Sumatra, but
there is increasing evidence that others came by way of the Philippines from
a region further north. One characteristic of this early Indonesian culture
is the erection of huge stone monuments, of which some resemble the figures.
of Easter Island. Like its predecessors, this Indonesian wave spread west-
wards; it is represented in India by the Shamars, and went on further west
to Madagascar, where the language is of an Indonesian type and boats with
two outriggers are found.

We must recognise then, that from very remote ages, there have been
streams of migration throughout the Archipelago, and that many forgotten
peoples are still represented by descendants among the primitive races of the
deeper forests and in some measure in the blood of the general Indonesian
population of the present day. Thus when, at a later date, probably about
the beginning of the Christian era, merchants from India opened up trade
relations with the peoples of the Archipelago, they were not sailing virgin
seas, but tracing, though in an opposite direction, a path which had long
been followed by people from the islands.
Many attempts have been made to depict the civilisation which these
Indian colonists found on their arrival. It is certain that the islanders were
not mere savages. They were already using metals; bronze, copper, iron
and gold. They undertook distant voyages and must have known something
· of astronomy and navigation. Probably they had already domesticated cattle,
and they worked irrigated rice land, which indicates a complex and well
established social order. Their dead, or at least their chieftains, were buried
under stone monuments, from which it may be gathered that their religion
included a belief in survival after death. Some of these monuments indicate
a high level of artistic sensibility.

Geographical Conditions.¹ Perhaps more than usual, the history of the
Archipelago has been conditioned by its geography. From the earliest times
the main object of traffic in these seas has been the interchange of silk from
China and spices from the Moluccas with the produce of India and the
West. The shortest route between East and West passes along the Straits
of Malacca between Sumatra and the main land. This region is marked out
by nature for a trading centre, and, since history first open, we find, on one
side or the other of the Straits, wealthy cities able to build up powerful
empires; for fifteen hundred years Srivijaya, Malayu, Malacca and Singapore
have held successively the key to commerce between East and West. But
Java has counter poising advantages in its central position, its large area
and the fertility of its soil. Until, within quite recent years, the population
increased up to and beyond the limit of its capacity for rice production, it
has always been the granary of the Archipelago, and has therefore been able
to contend for supremacy with the commercial civilisation further west. The
Sunda Straits between Java and Sumatra form a natural, but disputed,
boundary between these rival empires. Vessels to which the Straits of
Malacca were not open would pass south of Sumatra and through the Sunda
Straits. Any strong power established in Sumatra would endeavour there-
fore to control the Sunda Straits by an outpost of the west of Java, and to
bring the Sundanese country, although geographically part of Java, within
its sphere of influence. These considerations go far to explain the general
course of history in the Archipelago.

West Java 450 A.D. As mentioned above some of the oldest inscrip-
tions testify to the existence of Buddhism and Shaivism in West Java about
450 A.D. These are almost contemporary with the first detailed information
from Chinese sources. A Chinese reference indicates the rise of a kingdom
in Sumatra the second century, but for fuller details we must wait till the
visit of the famous pilgrim Fah-hien to Java (or, as M. Ferrand holds,
Sumatra) in 412-413 A.D.1 He found it "a land of heretics and Brahmans,
with not enough Buddhists to mention." Within ten years, however, the
preaching of the monk Gunavarnam, who came by way of Ceylon from
apparently, Kashmir, had led to the spread of Buddhism, probably Hina-
yanism, over the whole of Java; though this cannot casily be reconciled with
the inscriptions. These early Chinese records mention some of the commer-
cial products, but their chief interest is that they indicate no sharp distinction
between the Indian traders and the Natives, and suggest, rather, a Javanese
community under a strong Indian influence. Also, it would seem that the
colonisation was, on the whole, a peaceful process, largely the result of inter-
marriage, and that the Indians had acquired their influence in virtue of their
superior civilisation.
The Rise of Srivijaya under the Sailendras: 600 onwards. From
about 450 tp nearly 750, close on three centuries, there is a wide gap in our
information about Java, broken only by a casual allusion by I-tsing. Possibly
this may indicate a diversion elsewhere, or stagnation, or even a reaction
in the process of colonisation. In Sumatra the gap is not so wide; there are
Chinese references to it so late as 563, and already by 644 or 645 we hear
of Malayu, the present Jambi, as a centre of importance. By 683 Malayu
had yielded precedence to a new power, Srivijaya, with a capital situated
near the modern Palembang. M. Ferrand has no doubt that Srivijaya had
already come into prominence by the end of the 5th century. In 670 it
sent its first embassy to China and a glowing description of its wealth and
culture at that time was given twenty years later by I-tsing. By 686 the
Sailendras of Srivijaya had subdued Malayu, conquered the rest of Sumatra
and extended their dominion across the sea to Bangka. Before long they
were masters of a great part of the Malay Peninsula and held both sides of
the Straits of Malacca and, by extending their rule over West Java, held also
both sides of the Sunda Straits. With these two keys to eastern commerce
in their hands, they held the mastery over the whole Archipelago for many

When I-Tsing visited the Archipelago in 666, the Hinayanist form of
Buddhism predominated. He found Hinayanism in Java, which he calls Ho-ling,

and only in Malayu was Mahayanism predominant. But it was studied in

sects 2; they may have differed little more than High Church and Evangelical

coloured with Tantrism, became the only form of Buddhism throughout the

and the far reach of the trade of Srivijaya is the visit in 717 of a fleet of 35

Mid-Java. The reference of I-Tsing to Ho-ling is all we hear of Java
from about 450 A.D. until history reopens with an inscription in Mid-Java
of 732, the oldest inscription with a decipherable date. The language is
Sanskrit and the writing is in the same Pallawa script as on the earlier
inscriptions of West Java. But Buddhism has given place to Shaivism. It
mentions a King Sanjaya, who set up a Linga and "the most masterly
miraculous temple of Shaiva in the whole world." This is the first reference
to such an object as a temple, and therefore brings us to the beginning of
Javanese art. Of the temple itself there are practically no remains. But at
Dieng, which was probably a religious centre before the arrival of Indian
colonists, there are ruins which support the view that the art, represented in
the temple of Sanjaya, originated in South India, but had already been
taken over and developed by the Javanese.

we hear of a Sailendra dynasty and of Mahayanist Buddhism in Mid-Java.

But there is much about this period that is still mysterious. M. Ferrand
has no doubt that the Sailendras of Mid-Java were an off shoot of Srivijaya.
He cites an inscription of 779 which tells of an expedition "against the
Javanese king of Mid-Java" in 686, and finds confirmation of this view in a
Chinese history "which relates the transfer to Grisee in East Java of the
capital of Mid-Java, abandoned to the victorious Sailendra who held it for
more than a century.”2 But if the Sailendras of Java were subordinate to
Srivijaya, why did the Javanese princes build magnificent temples while their
overlords in Srivijaya built nothing? And what was the relation between
the Sailendras of Mid-Java and a new power which was arising in the east
of Java, just when the Sailendras were most powerful? These problems
have not yet been solved. But it is certain that in Java the Sailendras,
after flourishing for nearly a century, disappear as suddenly as they arose.
In Sumatra, however, they remained supreme, and for many centuries the
Maharaja of Srivijaya was still the outstanding political force in the Archi-
pelago.
Mataram 860 - 1006. The new power rising in East Java became
known later as Mataram, from the capital which it set up in Mid-Java
towards the end of the 9th century. In the early years of the 10th century
it ruled both Mid-Java and East Java, but subsequently was again confined
within its earlier limits in East Java. There are indications that the new
dynasty was connected in some way with Sanjaya, and it is certain that
Shaivism gathered force at the expense of Buddhism. An obvious inter-
pretation of the leading facts is that Shaivism had long been the popular
cult but that during the Sailendra period Buddhism had been favoured by
the rulers. Thus the rise of Mataram may be explained as a restoration of

and the kingdom of Mataram broke up into a number of petty states.
Kediri : 1006 - 1220. At this point, Airlangga, one of the heroes of
Javanese history, takes the stage. From the age of sixteen he set himself
to gather in his own hands all the reins of power and gradually rebuilt the
kingdom. At his death, however, he went far to undo his work by dividing
his realm between his two sons. The eastern portion, Jangala, centering
round Soerabaya was at first the more important, but it soon gave way to
the western princedom, comprising Madioen, Kediri and Mid-Java, possibly
so far west as Pekalongam. Before long the new dynasty moved its seat
to Kediri and a new era of prosperity dawned for Java.

West Java, though still it seems, nominally subject to Srivijaya, was given

yoke of Srivijaya forcibly. Kediri, on the other hand, was already supreme
over parts of Borneo and the eastern islands of the Archipelago.

had mingled with the people and given birth to an Indo-Javanese culture
which reached its highest point under the Kediri dynasty. From 1220 on-

culture becomes purely Javanese. The new strain seems to have brought
new strength. Java continued to prosper at the expense of Srivijaya. In

Java to demand his submission, and then invited him to pay homage in per-
son at the Mongol court. When he refused, an expedition was sent to
punish him. But it arrived too late. The Lord of Kediri, apparently a
connection of the old Kediri dynasty, had rebelled and siezed the throne. It
is possible that here again we may trace the influence of religion on politics.
The old Kediri dynasty had been Shaivite. But the great king of Marco
Polo's time, although he erected Shaivite temples, was a convinced and
learned adherent of Tantric Buddhism. The usurper did not enjoy the fruits
of his rebellion very long. For the Mongol soldiers, sent to punish a king
of Java, punished the king they found in Java, the usurper who had dethron-
ed and killed the man whom they were sent to punish. In the confusion
that ensued, the descendants of the great king of Marco Polo's book regain-
ed the throne.
Majapahit, First period 1292-1413. The restored dynasty founded a
new capital at Majapahit. Although two temples, one Shaivite and one
Buddhist, were erected to commemmorate the first king, Buddhism seems to
have spent its force, and everywhere gave way to Hinduism. Yet the new
civilisation was definitely Javanese, and in power and splendour far outshone
Kediri. By the end of the century, Srivijaya, Malayu and the whole of
Sumatra had been reduced to a position of dependence, and the rule of
Majapahit extended also over Borneo, part of the Malay Peninsular and
most of the islands to the east. Thus, although it did not include north
Celebes or the Philippines, the Empire of Majapahit covered roughly the
same area as the present Dutch Indies. It also had friendly relations with
the whole of Further India from Martaban to Annam, and sent embassies to
India and China.
A description of Java by a Chinese Mahommedan, Ma Huan, who visit-
ed the island in 1413 affords an interesting comparison with the accounts of
earlier Chinese writers. The city, he says, has a high brick wall and double
gates, and contains two or three hundred thousand families. The king goes
bareheaded or wears a cap with golden leaves and flowers; he wears a
sarong or slendang and is armed with two krisses; walks barefoot but usu-
ally rides on an elephant or in an ox-carriage. The common folk wear a
jacket, and all the males, from three years upwards, carry a kris with a
beautiful handle of gold, rhinoceros-horn or ivory. In a quarrel they stab
immediately; the murderer, if caught red-handed, is killed, but if he can

Numerous features in this description are said to recall modern Java,
especially the details relating to marriage customs. Allowing for the lapse
of time and the introduction of Islam, it appears that, except in religious
forms, there has been little change during the last five hundred years, so
that modern Javanese civilisation still rests on its old foundations.
Majapahit, Second period: 1400 - 1500. At the time of Ma Huan's
visit, Majapahit had already reached its zenith, which may be placed in
1382. In 1405 there was another of the usual dynastic rebellions, and,
although this was suppressed, it left the central power much weaker. Other
causes were contributing to its decline. Ma Huan found Mahommedan
settlers in the west and Chinese merchants in Tuban, Gresik and Soerabaya,
but the country as a whole was still Hindu. During the 15th century,
Hinduism, stimulated possibly by contact with a rival creed, seems to have
acquired new life which found expression in the multiplication of Hindu
temples.
j
But this was a last flicker of vitality. The fall of the old Kediri dynasty
had immediately been followed by a great extension of Moslem influence in
Sumatra probably due in part to the recall of the Javanese troops from
Malayu. From Sumatra, Islam spread rapidly along the coastal districts of
the Archipelago and the sea-board regents who accepted Mahommedanism
tended to become less dependent on their Hindu suzerain. Economic factors
were also working against the power of Majapahit. The supremacy of Java
in Sumatra had centred round Malayu. This tended to become an inland
power. Srivijaya had always held both sides of the Straits of Malacca, but
Malayu, looking inland, lost control over the coast. During the 15th century
Malacca rose into prominence and before long passed into Moslem hands.
Once again then the Archipelago was a scene of conflict between two powers,
the territorial sovereigns of Java and the commercial empire to the west.
Malacca was a centre both of trade and propaganda, and both commerce
and religion sapped the strength of Majapahit, which was attacked on both
these fronts at the same time that it was weakened by dynastic troubles.

by the time that Magellan's ships called there in 1521. In Java, however,
on the arrival of the Portuguese, Hinduism still prevailed, except at places
on the coast. De Brito, writing in 1514, says, "Java is a great island; it
has two powerful kings; on the sea coast the Moors are very powerful."
One of these kings was the Emperor of Majapahit; the other ruled over
Payayaran, the western end of Java, known to the Portuguese as Sunda.
We first hear of Payayaran as a dependent kingdom about 1330 and as
independent about 1405, a change which probably reflects the gradual
shifting of power from Majapahit towards Malacca. In 1522 Payayaran
was still Hindu and the king invited the Portuguese to build a fort there as
a barrier against the encroachments of the Moors. But on their arrival in
1526 they found the Moors already in possession. It seems that Majapahit
passed into Moslem hands at about the same time. During the 16th century
the Portuguese ruled the Archipelago from Malacca. But in the 17th century
the Dutch made good their hold over Java and also secured possession of
Malacca. Java, the geographical centre of the Archipelago, was once again
the centre of its commerce; but both Java and Malacca were subject to an
European power.
Java and Burma. The whole tropical Far East, I have said, has
much history in common; and the parallel course of history in Burma and
the Malay Archipelago should, I think, be evident even in this brief summary.
If we look back in Burma at the traces of Vishnuism and Shaivism and also
of Mahayanism before the triumph of Hinayanism, and at the foot-prints
left on the shores of Burma by colonists from various parts of India, we
must feel that from the earliest times the two threads have been running
parallel, and not very far apart. With the interruption of the Mongols,
upsetting a dynasty in Burma and a dynasty in Java at almost the same time,
the two threads may fairly be said to intersect. Then follows in each country
a period of internal strife and slow consolidation and, as Dr. Krom traces
the gradual Javanisation of an Indian civilisation, we can see what happened
with the Burmanisation of an Indian civilisation here, though, as we have no
Dr. Krom, we cannot yet trace it in such detail. And we cannot help but
realise how much all students of history in Burma owe to Dr. Krom, both
for his own researches and for collating with the hand of a master the
researches of a varied group of scholars. I trust that in attempting this
brief summary of his work I have done him no serious injustice; but I am no
more than an amateur in these matters and, if there should be any grave
blunders, you may be sure that they are mine and may not be attributed to
Dr. Krom.
