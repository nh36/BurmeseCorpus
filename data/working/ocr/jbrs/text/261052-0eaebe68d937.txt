Buddhism in the Inscriptions of Pagan
The numerous temples and pagodas which still attract visitors to Pagan
bear silent witness to the zeal with which the people professed Buddhism. We
are told plainly by the chronicles that King Anoratha and the monk Arahan
brought pure Buddhism from Thaton, thus putting an end to the evil
practices of the notorious Ari monks.1 There is a reference to the Ari monks
in the inscription L1762 which says "1000 bowls filled with cooked rice were
offered to the arañ. A silver Buddha also was given for the aran's to
worship. A well was dug. A tank was dug... My son became a monk and
listened to the first sermon. A gold Buddha was made. At the time when my
son received ordination and listened to the first sermon, slaves, cattle and
property were given ...
..." If the arañ of this inscription were really the Ari
monks, then we have an interesting instance of the Ari's passing for Buddhist
monks by performing Buddhist rites.
The Mon Inscriptions of Kyanzittha tell us how the Buddhism of Pagan
is mixed up with Vishnuism, and Nāga and spirit worship.3 In the building
of Kyanzittha's palace, Brahman astrologers take a prominent part in the
proceedings, presided over by the Buddhist monk Arahan. The thread with
which the pillars are bound is spun by young damsels, daughters of Brahmans.
Arahan himself holds a right-voluted conch, the symbol of Vishnu, while he
gives the Buddhist formula of saranasila and pronounces the paritta homily.
Å statue of the Buddha is placed side by side with that of Gavampati, the
patron saint of the Mons. More often than the statues of Buddha, Nārāyana
(Vishnu) is worshipped "with a decoration of plantains called the ox nose
adorned with young plantains and sugarcane" and with offerings of "mats,
golden flowers, altar candles, boiled rice in cup-shaped vessels with candles
stuck in it." The Nagas are also worshipped: "To the Nagas they made a
decoration of plantains for a dwelling place, spread mats and set in readiness
golden flowers and altar oblations." And in the Mon great Inscription of the
Shwezigon Pagoda the Buddha refers to Gavampati as "my son" and
prophesies the birth of Kyanzittha as the reincarnation of Vishnu, and Indra
is to take part in the building of the city of Sisit.

Such are the few but characteristic points in the Inscriptions indicating

instructive to analyse the motives and objects with which a donor sacrifices
practically all his or her wealth in building a work of merit. Faith in the
religion, fear of the round of rebirths, desire to attain nirvana are common
motives. Here is a typical passage: "Sakkaraj 554. Bhalakuin year Friday
5th waxing of Tapon, Cau Mwan San together with her daughter of boundless
faith, desirous of escaping the misery of the round of rebirths and of attain-
ing nirvana made an enclosure of brick round Amana Lake. Within the
enclosure was made a fine monastery. The lands dedicated to the shrine by
Uim Mwan San's daughter as offerings of food and oil-lights are..." (L80).

to abandon a body oppressed by countless miseries-the misery of birth, the
misery of old age, the misery of death, the misery of living with those we

getting what we want. I long to get the noble bliss of nirvana free from all
miseries. So I have resigned my dear and precious gold, silver and other
treasures and built a monastery. And I have offered all my fields, gardens.
and slaves, excepting none, for the happniess of the monks, pupils of the
Lord, who dwell therein, pure in piety and ever seeking the three graces of
virtue, concentration and understanding" (L311). Or a personal element

The word for monastery and school in the inscription is klon.
Kulā klon is the term generally applied to a brick monastery, much in
the same way as the modern Phongyi Kyaung is applied to a Buddhist
monastery. Kulā, probably the Pali Kula, is a general term in the inscriptions
for the Indians and seems to be applied to the monastery as a thing imported
together with Buddhism from India. A school is ca san klon, which has
survived to the present day. A calac klon is a monastery with a summit. In
L184 a monastery is fitted with an architectural ornament called kyakta nuiy.
L185 mentions a brick monastery, its summit and its portico (chan wan).
L261 speaks of a monastery with polished pillars (pwat tuin). The three
dwellings referred to in the extract from L259 above are a tauw kloň, îm kloň
and nā myak hna klon. A tauw klon is a forest monastery, the Pali āraññaka.
A na myak hna klon is a five faced monastery. An im klon literally means a
house-monastery and probably is a village-monastery as apposed to the forest
monastery. A house-monastery proper, that is, a house converted into a
monastery is also mentioned. Thus in L289 the foster-mother of Klacwā says:

The monks whose material wants were well provided for in their
magnificent monasteries devoted themselves well to the religion. They not
only practised piety in their huts of piety (satan tañkup L184) but also did
quite a lot of teaching. "Twenty schools within one layer of the enclosure"
are mentioned in L269. They studied the three Pitakas, which they housed
in libraries (L276). In the third Mon inscription found at Shwesandaw
Pagoda, Prome, King Kyanzittha claims to have collected and purified "the
three holy Pitakas, which had become obscured". To judge from the Pali
inscription of the Shwe Kugyi (L66) 2 they were second to none in the art of
Pali composition. They preached from the golden throne of the Law under
a gold umbrella beneath a canopy to the audience seated in the hall of
the Law (L190). The preaching of the first sermon the Dhammacakkra
(pavattana sutta) and of Malañ is mentioned in L160. Mālañ is the Pali
Mäleyya, now known as Shin Māle, a Sinhalese thera whose conversation
with the coming Buddha Metteyya is told in the Rasavāhini, a non-canonical
Pali work of Ceylon. Metteyya tells Maleyya among other things that if any
one wishes to meet him when he becomes the Buddha, "he must make offer-
ings to the present Buddha of a thousand rice-alms, a thousand sweet and sour
fruits, a thousand oil-lights, water flowers, land flowers, banners, a thousand
umbrellas, together with betel to chew and pickled tea-leaves appropriately
conveyed in hlawga boats and listen to the law of Vessantara." judging
by the offerings made to the three gems, the people of Pagan seem to have
acted on this exhortation of Metteyya, especially as Vessantara is the
embodiment of alms-giving in Buddhist literature. The Jatakas are often
mentioned (e.g. L242) and must have been, as they now are, the most popular
book of the Pali canon. "Silakhandha Vāsi the opening book of the Pitakas one
klam, Abhidhamma sangini one klam, Tassa Jataka one klam, Dhaṁmapada
one klam, Vineň (Vinaya) one passage" are quoted in L184. Klam* (modern
kyam) is the name of the boards between which palm-leaves on which writings

has been laid at the feet of the Teacher; I will carry it away with me.' So
he went to the monastery, and mingling with the crowd, entered for the
purpose of saluting the Teacher. The householder, concluding solely from the
way in which the Brahman entered that he was seeking to get possession of
the jewel, thought to himself, 'I hope he will not take it!' The Brahman
stretched out his arms at the Teacher's feet as if to salute him, took the
jewel, put it in a fold of his garment, and went out. The householder could
not retain his composure toward the Brahman. At the conclusion of his
sermon he approached the Teacher and said to him, ' Reverend Sir, thrice
have I strewn the ground about the Perfumed Chamber knee-deep with the
seven kinds of precious minerals, nor have I entertained unfriendly feelings
towards any that have taken jewels away with them; nay, my heart has
filled with joy the more. But today I thought to myself, "I hope this
Brahman will not take this jewel when he approaches !" When he took the
jewel and carried it away with him, I was unable to retain my composure
towards him.' The Teacher listened to his words and replied: 'Lay disciple, are
you not able to prevent others from taking what belong to you ? And he
taught him a way. The householder, employing the method taught him by
the Teacher, saluted the Teacher and made the following earnest wish, 'From
this day forth, may it not be possible for kings or thieves, no matter how
numerous, to defraud me of my property, though it be no more than a
single thread. May my property never be burned by fire, and may it never
be swept away by water.' The Teacher said, 'So be it,' and pronounced the
words of thanksgiving."

who, though regarded as future Buddhas, set up codes of punishment contrary
to the spirit of Buddhism. In a remarkable Edict,¹ dated sakkaraj 611, the
only one of its kind in the inscriptions, King Klacwā complains that " kings
of old put thieves to death impaling them and in various ways." The purpose
of the Edict seems to be the abolishment of capital punishment. Says the
King: "People in this world should respect these words of mine, believe
them and obey them strictly, inasmuch as these words of mine are not
invented with ingenuity, but are uttered in reliance on the omniscient word
of the exalted Buddha. Kings of old put thieves to death by impaling them
and in various ways. Not desiring the destruction of such creatures but
thinking of all creatures as myself, I have spoken these words of mercy.
Therefore they should be respected and obeyed. Wherefore should they be
obeyed? 'These words of the king are not invented with ingenuity, but
are words spoken in reliance on the omniscience of the exalted Buddha.
Thus if these words of mine are acted upon, happiness in this world and in
the next will surely be obtained.' With such intent they should be obeyed."
Impaling thieves with spikes, opening up their bosom with an axe, roasting
them with hot iron, extracting their entrails, breaking their thighs and hands,
plucking out their eyes, boring an hole on the head and pouring in hot oil,
burying them neck-deep in the earth and ploughing it with a plough, putting
them in a basket and making an elephant tread on it, making them embrace
a tree and putting spikes into them-these are some of the punishments
enumerated in the Edict. It is natural therefore that people, brought up on
such a code of punishments, should hold forth similar or worse terrors to
ward off would-be desecrators of their works of merit.
