To avert misunderstanding, it must
be emphasized at the outset that I am an
academic man and not a professional
politician. The present talk is, therefore,
a purely academic lecture and not a
political sermon. In presenting this paper
to this distinguished audience, I have three
main purposes: In the first place, it is
designed to be a small contribution to the
comparative study of the progressive ideas,
theories and experiences without discrimina-
tion between one country of origin and
another as laid down in the Burmese Way
to Socialism;' in the second place, it is
aimed at assisting my own law students,
who, incidentally, constitute the majority
of the present audience, in their study of
such subjects as History of State and Law
and Jurisprudence and Socialist Legal
Theory, which form part of the course for
the LL.B. degree. Finally, it is hoped that
this lecture would stimulate thought and
further intelligent discussion among educat-
ed men and women in this country on this
very important subject.
It is perhaps pertinent to point out
here that in the Soviet Union, which has
just celebrated the 50th Anniversary of the
October Socialist Revolution, theories of
State and Law that used to be dismissed
before as purely academic and therefore
useless, are now being widely discussed not
only in the legal and university circles, but
even in the various organs of the Party as

II. Conflicting Theories of State
Few words in the English language
are as fraught with so many meanings as
is the term 'state', which has been subjected
to divergent and conflicting interpretations.
Some writers define the state as essentially
a class-structure, ‘an organization of one
class dominating over the other classes;"
others regard it as the one organization that
transcends class and stands for the whole
community. Some interpret it as a power
system, others as a welfare system. Some
view it entirely as a legal construction,
either in the old Austinian sense which
made it a relationship of governors and
governed, or, in the language of modern
jurisprudence, as a community 'organised
for action under legal rules.” Some identify
it with the nation, others regard nationality
as incidental or unnecessary. Some regard
the state as on more than a mutual insurance
society, others as the very texture of all our
life. To some it is a necessary evil that
is or will some day be unnecessary. Some
speak of the state as one in the order of
corporations and others think of it as
indistinguishable from society itself."

IV. State And Law As 'Superstructure'

law as well as the whole of spiritual
civilization, is a 'superstructure' erected
over the material 'basis' that, in their
opinion, is formed by the relationship of
men in the process of production of
commodities. Economic factors shape and
determine the form and content of law.
All other elements are characterized as
"the political, legal and other ideological
conceptions" through which men become
conscious of the material forces of produc-
tion and its existing or developing rela-
tions.
