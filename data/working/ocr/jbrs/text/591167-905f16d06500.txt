OBITUARY
Dr. C. E. Godakumbura (1907–1977)
It is with deep sorrow that we have to put on record the demise of Dr.
Charles Edmund Godakumbura, a Sri Lankan scholar and archaeologist of vast
reputation. The sad event however took place at the Military Hospital at Mingala-
don, Rangoon, on February 9, 1977. He was then on his second visit to Burma,
the land he tremendously loved and admired.
The first time he visited Burma was in July, 1966. He came alone then at
the invitation of General Ne Win, Chairman of the Revolutionary Council. He
was Commissioner of Archaeology Deparment of Ceylon, and in that capacity, he
travelled almost all over the country visiting ancient religious edifices and archaeo-
logical sites at Thaton, Pegu, Mandalay, Sagaing, Shwebo, Halingyi, Pagan,
Nyaung-U, Prome, Beikthanomyo, Syriam and Mrohaung in Arakan. His objective
was to study Burmese archaeology and exchange archaeological knowledge with
Burmese archaeologists. U Aung Thaw, Director of Archaeology Department of
Burma, and U Saw Tun of the International Institute of Advanced Busdhistic
Studies, were on hand to see to his needs during his study tours.
Another form of his activity was giving lectures or reading papers. At the
IIABS, he gave two lectures on Ceylonese art and architecture with the aid of
colour slides. Two papers were read on the University campus - "Archaeology as
an academic subject" at the meeting organised by the Arts & Science University of
Rangoon and “The relation between Ceylon and Burma" at the meeting of the
Burma Research Society. Essentially an eminent scholar in Pali and Sanskrit, he
lectured on the philology of these two Indo-Aryan classical languages to the staff
and students of the Pali Department, RASU, for three successive days. Another
lecture was delivered at the Arts & Science University, Mandalay.

Intervals between his tours and lectures were filled with discussions mainly
held at the IIABS, Kaba-Aye, Rangoon. The participauts included scholars and
officials of the Institute, the Buddha Sāsana Council and the Ministry of Religious
Affairs. The topics centred mainly around Buddhism. Questions were put to him
from the Burmese side which he tackled scholarly. The discussions were not without
arguments and counter-arguments, but the atmosphere was always free and friendly,
even jovial at times.
By the time he returned home after three months' active stay in Burma, he
had made a number of friends among the Burmese.
After eleven long years, he paid the second visit which, alas! turned out
to be his settlement here. His visit this time was less formal; he was accompanied
by his charming wife Chandra. In fact, he purposely brought Mrs. Godakumbura
along to show her how beautiful Burma was and how practical Burmese ladies
were in their national costumes. But like the first time, he had a very busy
programme although it was more of a sight-seeing trip. U Nai Pan Hla and
U Than Swe, of the Archaeology Department, were his liaison officers this time.
The distinguished couple visited pagodas, monasteries and centres of tourist attrac-
tions in Rangoon, Pegu, and Moulmein. They went up to Mandalay and its
environs, Pagan and Nyaung-U. Having come back from Upper Burma, the visitors
travelled to Bassein and then to Sandoway with an intention to proceed to Akyab
and, if possible, to Mrohaung. It had been planned in Rangoon by the Society
that the learned scholar should read a paper on the Dipavaṁsa, the first Sinhalese
Chronicle, at the Society's meeting on their return frow Arakan. The paper was
ready, but the plan was never to come true, At Sandoway, Dr. Godakumbura
was suddenly taken ill and rushed back to Rangoon. He was treated by our most
eminent doctors and nurses with the best medical aid.

Dr. Godakumbura was born on December 12, 1907, in Chilaw, a small town
on the west coast about 50 miles from Colombo. His father was a school teacher,
and young Godakumbura went to the school where his father worked. After 'finishing
his primary education there, he moved on to Kingswood College, Kandy for his next
schooling. He studied there up to Senior Cambridge which he joined the College
as a member of the teaching staff.
He learned Pali at the Sangaraja Pirivena Malwatta School of Oriental Studies,
Kandy, where he passed the final examination in his early twenties. Just before the
Second World War, his enthusiasm in academics took him to London; he pursued
his studies at the School of Oriental & African Studies, University of London, and
won the M.A. degree with distinctions in Indo-Aryan languages in 1938 and the
Ph.D. in 1945 for his thesis on Sinhalese syntax. He returned to Ceylon in 1948
to work as a lecturer in Sinhalese at the Colombo University.
During the early days of his sojourn in London, he came across a young
Burman, and the two later became intimate friends working at the same School.
The young Burman was Dr. Hla Pe who has been Professor of Burmese for several
years at the SOAS.
Most probably, Dr. Hla Pe was his first Burmese friend, and
his friendship with Dr. Hla Pe aroused his interest in Burma and her culture which
eventually led to his fruitful research in this direction.
His second sojourn in London commenced in 1953 when he joined the SOAS,
first as Senior Lecturer and, after some time, as Reader in Sinhalese. In 1954, he
gained the D. Litt on the merit of his scholarship in the field of Pali, Sanskrit and
Sinhalese. In 1959, he left the School for good and was back home to take up.
the post of Deputy Commissioner of Archaeology. He became Commissioner in
1963 and worked until he retired in 1966. Since his retirement, he had given
lectures to post-graduate and Ph.D. students and acted as an examiner for the
University of Ceylon.

The year 1976 saw him honoured in India. The Nava-Nalanda University
of Bihar State conferred on him the coveted duble of Mahā-Acarya and Vidyā-
Väridhi. The Governor of Bihar Dr. H.V. Sohani personally donned him the acade-
mic robe at the University's Convocation in recognition of his brilliantlabours in editing
and translating into English the Sanskrit text entitled Janakiharaṇa, an episode from the
Rāmāyaṇa, which narrates the story of Rävana's (Dasagiri's) taking away of Janaki
(Sītā).
Dr. Godakumbura was associated with a number of academic bodies including
the Ceylonese Branch of the Royal Asiatic Society, of which he had been President
for some time and for the Journal of which he had written articles. He edited
some Pali texts for the Pali Text Society of London. He was also an editor of the
Sinhalese Dictionary and a collaborator for the Critical Pali Dictionary being com-
piled by a group of international scholars sponsored by the Royal Danish Acade-
my of Sciences and Letters, Copenhagen. His writings on Ceylonese archaeology
occasionally appeared in the archaeological journal published in Australia. He was
the author of Sinhalese Literature which has been a significant landmark in his
scholastic career. But he will be remembered by us chiefly for his papers contri-
buted to the Society's Journal and the Guardian Magazine such as "The relation
between Ceylon and Burma", "Chapada and Chapada Jotipāla", "The Sinhalese
and the Burmese", "Burmese Buddhist Order and Ceylon" and "The common
culture of Burma and Sri Lanka”.
I had the privilege of knowing Dr. Godakumbura personally. In 1957, I
registered myself as an M.A. student at the SOAS and went to see him at the
suggestion of Prof. J. Brough, Head of the Department. After a brief encounter,
he accepted me as a pupil of his and gave me the topic of my thesis, which he
supervised during its early stages. The following year he asked me to help him in
cataloguing palm-leaf MSS from South-East Asia collected at the Oriental Depart-
ment of the Royal Danish Library. I worked on those from Burma under his
guidance. It had always been a pleasure to work under him or learn at his feet.
I had nothing but affectionate esteem for him, whom I used to call Sayagyi.

Sayagyi Godakumbura was fascinated with the Burmese custom of christening
according to the name of the day on which a person is born. Once he told me
that he would like a Burmese name. “On which day were you born?”, I asked.
"Monday", said he. All of a sudden the word “Kyaw” which was part of my
late brother's name came to my mind. So I gave the name "Kyaw". He then asked
for another word because most of the Burmese names comprise two words. “What
about Lwin which is part of mine?” He took it without fuss. Hence his Burmese
name U Kyaw Lwin, which he inarticulately but fondly mentioned whenever he was
introduced to a Burman.
Sayagyi Godakumbura alias U Kyaw Lwin was quick-witted, soft but outs-
poken. On his first visit, a Burmese gentleman told him that Burma considered
Ceylon as its younger brother, and he retorted, "I am glad because in that case
the elder brother will look after Ceylon. He will send more rice to his younger
brother." Sayagyi was of simple taste. He would move about with his Shan bag
hanging on his shoulder. He would not leave it even when he had to see his friend
U Ne Win. He thought the top ranking Inya Lake Hotel was too big for him and
said that a small compact hotel would suit him better. This time he did not say
so, but grumbled that the food provided there was plentiful. He shared the meal
there with his wife which was normally meant for one. And he would care for
vegetables more than meat perhaps on health grounds.
Sayagyi was very fond of things Burmese, but there were two things that he
fancied most: Burmese dress and Burmese films. In fact, the moment he set foot
on Burmese soil for the first time he expressed his desire to put on Burmese
longyi and jacket. His generous host Bogyokegyi heard of this and immediately
sent some pieces of Burmese clothing which Sayagyi treasured immensely. It seemed
that Bogyokegyi's example was followed by others, for most of the presents he
received from his Burmese friends were longyis and jackets. It was only in Burmese
dress that he was seen throughout his Burmese days. As regards his fondness of
Burmese films, when somebody in Rangoon invited him to a dinner he said he
would prefer a Burmese film; he was taken to the one called “Chitmye". He also
saw two other Burmese films during his tour of Upper Burma.

During his last visit to Burma, he was inclined to see Burmese Mahātheras
of highest morality rather than movies. He paid respect to the Taungpulu Sayadaw
of Meiktila who was then on a visit in Rangoon. He approached the Tipitakadhara
Sayadaw of Mingun and the Webu Sayadaw of Kyaukse separately; he was so
fortunate that both happened to be in Mandalay during his visit there. He paid a
second visit to the well-disciplined Mahāgandhārāma monastery at Amarapura
where the Venerable Janakābhivaṁsa is Abbot and where he watched for the
second time the calm and serenity of young bhikkhus sitting and reciting words
of mettä during their morning prayers in a big hall. "Oh, they are like stone
statues!", exclaimed Sayagyi and told his wife of his profound impression. He
needed no interpreter when he conversed with the Mahāgandhārāma Sayadaw and
the Titpiṭakdhara Sayadaw, for the conversations were done in the medium of
Pali.
His religious inclinations suggested that Sayagyi knew beforehand of the time
when he would part with his family and friends alike permanently. It appeared
that in seeing the Sayadaws he was seeking their blessings or acquiring merits as
part of his preparation for hereafter. A man of pragmatism, he bequeathed that
in case of death his remains should not be flown back to Sri Lanka, but rather be
buried at the place where he died. So when he breathed his last, the burial took
place at Mingaladon, Rangoon, according to Buddhist rites and most befittingly.
The body was in Burmese longyi and jacket! The funeral was attended by a large
gathering including his Burmese friends, for he died a friend of Burma, the land
he loved so much that he came to stay there, as it were for ever.
A word about Mrs. Chandra Godakumbura. She is a brave lady. Death of
a spouse is the greatest ever blow one can imagine. The misfortune of such a
magnitude befell Mrs. Godakumbura, and it befell while they were in a foreign
country. But she most admirably and stoically took it. There were a couple of
Burmese ladies, her closest friends here, on whose shoulders she wept bitterly. But
it did not take her long to compose herself and her friends did not find it much
