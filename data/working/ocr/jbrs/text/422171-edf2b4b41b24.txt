Vernacular literature and contemporary accounts left by European visitors to
monarchical Burma provide happy hunting grounds for researchers with eyes
on the subject of Burmese kingship.
Burmese authors of the past throve on royal patronage and their writings
often contained overt or covert praises of their patrons. Even a small coterie
of scholars who did not write for self-advancement in the material sense found
the fascination of the life at court as a subject of study overwhelming. They
were stimulated by the glamour, romance and intrigues of the court circle and
their major works naturally revolved round the theme of king and court. The
historical value of those literary works cannot well be overlooked. Prolific
accounts that are impregnated with the idea of Burmese kingship are extant.
A typical formal royal address given in one Burmese literary work says:'
Within the thousand yuzanas of this extensive Jambudipa island³
there is none so glorious and powerful as your excellent majesty who
is the shining light of the Universe...
From such writings as this, one can deduce the high status of the Burmese
rulers in his domains.
Burmese official letters to foreign governments are also of interest from
this viewpoint, as they conveyed to the outside world Burmese concept of
kingship. A letter sent by Alaungpaya, founder of the Konbaung dynasty
sheds some light on the subject. It says:*
The King, Despotick, of great Merit, of great Power, Lord of the

In his kingdom, a Burmese ruler was supreme. He was (
e) "lord and master of life, head and hair of all human beings,
and king of kings". He therefore expected complete submission and
loyalty from each and everyone of his subjects. He was the fountain-head of
honour and justice and he could exalt and degrade his people at will. He
made final decisions in affairs of importance, both domestic and foreign,
and there was no one who dared to criticize-or even question - the motives
behind the policies. Sovereignty with all its attributes rested with the
Burmese monarchs and their exercise of sovereign power often resulted in
autocractic governments-less desirable forms of despotism-though bene-
volent despots were not unknown factors in Burmese history.
The importance, the dignity and power attached to Burmese kingship was
sustained throughout history, and the people accepted their sovereigns' claims
without question and often without resentment. Their passive acceptance was
partly explained by their strong belief in the Buddhist law of causation - the
doctrine of "karma" - and partly by their gratitude towards the rulers for
preserving law and order in the country and promoting the material benefits
of the ruled. In the name of security. liberty was sacrificed at the altar of king-
ship. Hence their servitude. This tendency to humour every whim of their
protector was essentially an outgrowth of the "leader-follower" relationship
that prevailed in early patriarchal societies.

the ruler and the ruled; but even if a ruler exercised his powers with utmost
severity, still he had maintained stability and unity in the realm, and it was
looked upon as a case of “the end justifying the means" and the people did
not grudge their king his inflated royal prerogatives. In general, the ostenta-
tious display of power and wealth by rulers tended to accentuate the suscepti- .
bility of the mass of people to "hero-worship" and enable them to bear with
the worse cases of tyranny.
Besides, as explained above, there was the peoples belief in "karma"
which accounted for their acceptance and recognition of the claims made by
their sovereigns. According to this belief, a person's present existence was
conditioned by his mode of behaviour in the past life, and his good deeds of
the present were his means of accumulating merit for future advancement;
adverse merits, likewise, would bring him unhappiness either in his life-time
or in the next incarnation, at the turn of the inexorable wheel of the law
"karma" ( mg ) or ( m ).

As a person's sufferings and rejoicings in the present could be explained
in the light of his past deeds, the amount of Temporal power exercised by the
king came to be regarded by his people as the criterion to judge the nature of
his "kutho" (mis)- merit accumulation in his previous existence. The
king's mundane acquisitions - his worldly riches and power - came to him
as his due by the law of "karma"; his was an outstanding example of merit
and reward, and his high status therefore should stir noble instincts of his
subjects rather than base ones; they should look upon him with admiration
rather than envy. The king's unassailable position became acceptable to his
subjects when it was interpreted as a corollary of the doctrine of “karma”.
Thus the absolutism of monarchy was rationalized and defended by a Buddhist
concept.

benign influence of the ruler being appreciated by the ruled. The
Burmese believed that good kings usually brought peace and prosperity
to the country. A benevolent monarch not only commanded the love
and esteem of his subjects but also those of the tutelary nat (spirits)
who were supposedly responsible for the country's climatic conditions.13
For instance, if a king misbehaved, the rain-making nat would cease to
function by way of showing their dispproval and there would be drought
in the country. Other nat would similarly withhold their seasonal favours.
to spite a bad king and unpredictable weather would follow which
would be a serious matter a curse for an agricultural country. In
order to enhance the material welfare of his people, the king would have to
cultivate such virtues as piety, generosity, gentleness and self-effacement. Then
only the nat would unstintingly bestow their blessings upon the king and his
subjects. Good harvests implied favours of gods and people would extol the
virtues of their king who had ingratiated himself with the nat. They would
look up to their king as their true protector and would pray for his longevity.
A king was secure so long as he had the support of his people: if they were
discontented, they would blame him for their misfortunes and retract their
allegiance to him, and this would be disastrous for him. The interdependance
of the ruler and the ruled was explicit; and happy was the monarch who
could ask his ministers during the royal audiences, "Are the rain and air pro-
pitious so that the people live in happiness and ease?" and receive the desired
answer, "The seasons are favourable and the people live in happiness".
Such being the case, it was understandable why the belief of the king and
people in the doctrine of "karma" had not precluded the rise of benevolent.
despots in Burma.
Apart from the interdependence or the "mutual-benefit" theory there
were other forces that had undermined absolutism in Burma. One consider-
able factor was the sovereigns' respect for the deep-seated conventions of the
country which had their origin in ancient Hindu polity.
It would seem strange that Hindu influences should persist in Burma
where Buddhism was the dominant religion. But one should not lose sight
of the forceful impact of Hindu culture on the early Burmese people and

If the Burmese court ceremonies of the Konbaung period were highly
reminiscent of the Hindu practices, it was because the idea of Burmese king-
ship was the product of a closely interwoven texture of Hindu and Buddhist
concepts. The Burmese court ceremonies of the later dynasties, in fact, were
Hindu court rituals of the early dynasties with Buddhistic modifications

The theory of origin of Burmese kingship itself was basically a Hindu
idea. The contex of the coronation oaths, however, reveals both Hindu and
Buddhist sentiments. The Buddhist concept of the genealogy of Gautama
Buddha-that He belonged to the Khastriya group which was one of the major
divisions in the Hindu varna or caste system-tended to link together the two
major religions of the Orient. The reflex of the Hindu-Buddhist combination
was also seen in the Burmese acceptance of the idea that their kings were in
the direct line of descent from the Khastriya and that they were also
Bodhisattva or incipient Buddhas. 14

Burmese kingship was a trust - a bi-lateral agreement rather than a uni-
lateral one; the king undertook to protect his people and to further their
interests for which he was invested with certain privileges which included the
right to impose a lawful tax-one-tenth of the produce of the land. The incum-
bency of kingship carried with it duties as well as privileges. The Burmese
kings did not rule by "Divine Right”.

and contemporary native literature of later periods stressed the point by mak-

him from becoming a tyrant. But, such fear-it was found-was not always an
effective remedy against arbitrary rule. It even provided incentive for an
oppressive rule as it tended to make a king assert his power more than before,

Far more significant than the fear of rebellion theory was the contract
theory. The idea that kingship rested on the foundations of a contract or
working agreement between king and people was retained in monarchic
Burma long after 'centripetal tendencies were at work in the state and the
exaltation of kingship was effected. For instance, the "conditional” aspect
of Burmese kingship was still emphasized during the coronation ceremonies
of the Konbaung period - the contract theory epitomizing in the coronation
oaths of the Konbaung kings.
There were fourteen kinds of coronation ceremony, out of which the
Muddha Bhiseka ceremony was the most important. Its ritual was more
claborate than that of any other; also the coronation oaths administered to
the king by people representing various strata of society - such as princes.
Brahmins and richmen-were much more severe than those in other coronation
ceremonies. Only the greatest and the strongest of kings dared to subject
themselves to the stringent requirements of the Muddha Bhiseka ceremony.
The prestige of those who went through the ceremony therefore was greatly
enhanced in the eyes of their subjects. They became for that matter the most
lustrous in the galaxy of Burmese kings. 20

The king's role as protector of his people was evidenced by his coronation
vows to watch over them as though they were his own sons and daughters.
The same vows also revealed the king's deep respect for the laws of Buddha,
and the presence of the Thathanabaing (Primate) and monks at thef Muddha
Bhiseka ceremony impressed upon the peoples' minds the king's desire to
foster and promote Buddhism.
Since the times of Anawrahta, Burmese kings were aware of the value of
the Buddhist religion as a cohesive force - a solid factor contributing towards
centralization in the state. Buddhism had inculcated in the minds of the be-
lievers ideas of Universal love, tolerance and forbearance and the king could
expect docility, meekness and humility from his subjects who were compeletely
schooled in the tenets of Buddhism. Also, the peoples' identification of their
ruler with the future Maitreya24 Buddha, furthered the cause of monarchy, and
this idea that the king was the future Buddha - had found as much favour
with the Buddhist kings of Burma as the “karma” doctrine. Buddhism indeed
was a powerful force that united the people in their loyalty to the throne.
-
The Burmese kings who were alive to the spiritual and material values
of Buddhism naturally showed a great zeal towards protecting and promoting
the religion. Viewed in this light, the king's nsatiable thirst for meritorious
deeds, and their passion for the construction of imposing religious buildings
had taken on a new meaning; those temples and pagodas the Burmese kings
had built assumed symbolic significance as pillars of Burmese monarchy.
Yet paradoxically, Buddhism, as much as it enhanced the prestige of
the king, imposed restraints upon him. For instance, although the king was
"ord of life and property” in the country, he would always have to bear in
mind the Buddhist doctrine advocating the sanctity of life and the sacrosanct
nature of the Watthugam property (religious land; or endowments). The king.
would not advance any claim over the Watthugam property; nor would he ex-
ercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction which he regarded as the close reserve or
monopoly of the Thathanabaing (Primate) who was the Head of the Buddhist
Church. It is true that the king appointed the Primate; but the fact does not
imply that the king had great influence over the Primate. On the contrary, it
was the Primate, who, as a preceptor of the king, was recognized as a superior
commanding the latter's respect and esteem. There were many instances in
history when the Primate as "moderator" had urged the king to review and

There were other unwritten laws of the country for the guidance of the
king, telling him what he should and should not do. Wise kings paid heed
to those. King Narathihapate (A.D. 1254-1287), the last of the Pagan dynasty
paid a heavy price for his neglect of those conventions. Towards the end of his
reign, his chief queen, Queen Saw, who was renowned for her sagacity, pointed
out to Narathihapate where he had gone wrong. She averred that good
kings observed golden maxims such as those saying, "Bore not thy country's
belly," "Abase not thy country's forehead," and, "Fell not thy country's
banner." She explained to her royal husband that it was not for a king to
confiscated the property of the rich just for the pleasure of squandering them;
if he did, that would be like boring the country's belly. He should not scorn
the advice given by the sages who could metaphorically be compared to the
country's forehead. He should by all means protect the country's religion.
To desecrate the Buddhist Church would be like hauling down and rending
the country's banner thus bringing shame and dishonour upon the country.
Queen Saw declared that Narathihapate had flouted all those axioms irrevo-
cably and so would have to prepare himself for the consequences.
