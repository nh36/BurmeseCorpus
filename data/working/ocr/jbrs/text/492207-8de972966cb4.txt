DAVID LESTER RICHARDSON-DIPLOMAT AND EXPLORER
R. R Langham-Carter
David Richardson played an impor-
tant part in the difficult negotiations which
had to be conducted between the British, the
Burmese and the Siamese after the first
Anglo-Burmese war and he deserves to be
rescued from the neglect with which histo-
rians have so far treated him. He was
almost the first Briton to acquire a thorough
knowledge of the Burmese language, to
marry a Burmese and to make his home in
Burma, and he did outstanding work as a
diplomat and as an explorer. His achieve-
ments become the more remarkable when it
is remembered that he came of humble
Scottish parents and that his scanty educa-
tion was intended only to enable him to earn
his living as a doctor.
Richardson was born in London on
26th December 1796 and first arrived in
Asia in 1823 when he was posted to medical
duty with the Madras Army. Little can he
then have realised that he would spend the
whole of his remaining twenty-three years
of life in Asia and most of them not as a
military doctor but as an administrator and
diplomat. On the outbreak of the war he
was transferred to Burma and saw active
service in Rangoon, Danubyu, Pegu and
Shwegyin and when the war ended he held
medical appointments in Amherst
Moulmein. He wrote no books but a num-
ber of reports on his missions have been
printed and he is mentioned a good deal in
official correspondence. All this, however,
would have left him rather a shadowy figure
but for a piece of good fortune which came
my way in 1947. I found that no less than

Officers engaged in a war do not have
many opportunties to study the country they
are serving in but Richardson took every
chance that came his way and soon became
interested in Burma and very fond of it. The
houses seem to have been the first things to
engage his attention and, within a week of
his arrival, we find him telling his father on
17th may 1824 that Rangoon "is a large
town built in the Malaya style, all of bam-
boos raised on poles of teak about ten or
twelve feet and from that down to two feet
from the ground." While he was at Danubyu
in the following February he reported that
"the houses are generally very low, all of
them built entirely of bamboos.

He had become a Burma enthusiast by
now. Writing from “a beautiful situation,
commanding a view of the river (one of the
finest in the world) for many miles," he told
his father that "the soil is the richest I have
ever seen, the fruits in comparison with
those of India are infinitely superior.....
The climate is most salubrious, for which
the inhabitants vouch by their appearance,
being by far the strongest built Asiatics I
have ever seen.” During this time of warfare
he had naturally not met many of them and
believed they were less handsome than
Indians an opinion which he presumably
changed not many years later when he
married a Burmese wife. He suffered the
inevitable discomforts in trudging across the
paddy fields from Pegu to the Sittang and
then up the river towards Shwegyin after
the monsoon of 1825, writing from
Myitkyo in December:
From the remarkably intersected state
of the country, which for one half of the
year is one uninterrupted sheet of water, the
road we wished to advance, we have been
unable to commence our march till the 21st
of the present month. And it was so far
fortunate, as had we started one week
sooner it would have been utterly impossible
to have dragged the guns through the
interminable swamps we have crossed. As it
is, we have marched three days, a great
part of the way over the ankles in mud and
water, every few miles crossing a river more

tents on the dryest spots we could find at
the end of our march.

He set off on the first of his many jour-
neys into Siam on the 11th December 1829,
travelling with a party of merchants by boat
up the Salween. Three days later they landed
at the mouth of the Yanbaing stream, be-
yond which the rapids made progress on
the Salween difficult. They then followed
the route normally taken by traders on
their way to Karenni and the Shan States,
going over the hills into the valley of the
Me Gnan and thence into that of the
Mainglongyi river. Whenever possible they
used bamboo rafts (for the area was thickly
clad with bamboo) and at other points
marched up the beds of the streams. On
occasion they were able to replenish their
food supplies in the rare Karen villages.
Richardson observed that the hills were of
limestone or of red sand-stone and or, less
often, of volcanic rock.
On the 1st January he arrived at

diverged from the trade route to Karenni,
going eastwards till he reached the Me Ping
river four days later. He travelled up the
