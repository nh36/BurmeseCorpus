The following songs have been collected since
August 1948 during the course of attempts to study
Burmese culture, in particular the everyday life of ordi-
nary people in their own homes, and to collect objects,
particularly ordinary domestic ones, illustrating the
material culture of Burma, for the British Museum and
for the Horniman Museum.
The writer is indebted greatly to numerous Burmese
friends for their ever ready and kindly help. Their ages
range from eight to eighty and many will not see these
notes, because they could not read them if they did, but
this article is presented in the hope that it will make
new friends for Burmese poetry and revive interest and
enthusiasm, particularly for the older and uninfluenced
forms of that art, among the many Burmans well
qualified, did they but realize it, to add to knowledge,
at least by collecting and recording, with as many
variations as may be found, many more of the hundreds
of folk songs not yet quite forgotten, before this valuable
and fascinating heritage is lost.
Among those who have helped in detailed study,
special acknowledgement is due to the Honorary Editor
of this Journal not only because his help has been large
but also because his patience must often have been
sorely tried by the meticulous caution of the scientist
(so often resembling something quite different); also to
the Honorary Secretary of the Burma Research Society
for his wise and cautious yet always enthusiastic
encouragement; and to many other learned friends, as
will be apparent from the variety of subjects touched
upon.
Many of the notes, Burmese readers will find
elementary. Of such notes, some are for those whose
knowledge of Burmese is as scanty as the writer's, some
for ready comparison of the ordinary meaning of a word

2. If It Rains.
The language of this little jingle, which children
were often heard singing while at play in Rangoon city
streets during the early part of the monsoon (May and
June) 1949, is of the simplest and the associations of
ideas are clear without the analysis of word-forms, sounds
and alternative meanings required for a full understanding
of more advanced compositions. Because the words
which chime are not in relative positions which form any
accepted rhyme scheme, there are, by the standards of
Burmese poetry, no rhymes in this song. The first four
lines however run smoothly and are well balanced. These
four lines are remembered from childhood by people over
seventy today but not the last two lines which, for this
reason and because they have not the smoothness and
balance of the first four, are probably a very recent
addition. Indeed these two are not known yet to many
people now only in their thirties and the mention of
money is also a very modern touch.

3. A Nursery Rhyme Without End.
Appreciated by children from earliest years until
they have learnt it themselves and from then till about
seven or eight by which time they have learnt more
intelligible songs, it is well known wherever Burmese is
spoken and in Arakan, It may be it originally told a
connected story and the words have become changed in
course of time but there have long been many searching
its now inconsequential phrases for hidden meanings of
a prophetic nature. They ask themselves what king there.
was who could be described as a monkey on the throne
or called Kya E meaning Slow (i. e. long deferred) Peace
or whether Kya E is just an ordinary name (note 4,
below) or whether it meant the British who ran away in
1942, and so on.

14. Summer.
This joyous little song is known to most Burmese,
old or young, and is believed to be quite old. Simple
and whole-hearted yet polished in diction and rhyme
scheme and dealing with the favourite theme of Burmese
song, the beauty of nature, simply for its own sake, it
is far removed from the sophistication which appears
to have come to Burmese poetry towards the end of
the eighteenth century. It pictures going out into the
country, whether to a local festival or to gather mala
buds to eat or just to gather flowers, and the time is
late summer or before the rains have set in heavily and
Lent has begun.

This collection would be very incomplete without
a paddy planting song but it has not been possible to
collect one which has not been recorded before. However,
those published in April 1981 in Vol. XXI, Part 1, of this
Journal contain so many points of special interest as to
be well worth studying afresh and the first of them is
reproduced here.
U Lu Gale's introductory note to them (XXI, 1, p.
15) is most important; but tantalising. Full information
on the exact sequence of each event--the placing of the
drum; the thanaka, comb, mirror and bracelet; the fresh
banana tree and fencing; the pig's head; the pork and
container; the offering to the Nats; the closing of the field,
the feast in the owner's house; each arrival and departure
of the men, with drums or otherwise; and of the women-
and the timing and duration of each event with reference
to the whole work of transplanting in one field, all this
would be of the very greatest value and interest.
In the following, although one third or so of the
lines are worded as though coming from a man, each line
is sung by one or more of the women (A, B etc. being
different voices but none necessarily solo) while at work
dibbling the rice seedlings into the half liquid tillage 6"
to 8" deep which has been prepared by men by ploughing,
then puddling with a harrow and again, if there was a
heavy cover of weeds, with a rolling harrow. Men have
also pulled up the seedlings from the nursery plot, tied
them in bundles of 500 to 600 each and distributed the
bundles over the field. From a bundle, each girl takes as
many seedlings as she can hold in the left hand and,
from there, 3 to 5 according to size, roots downwards,
between fingures and thumb of right hand and pushes
them down into the mud, each bunch about 5" from the
last. The bunches are planted either in lines also 5" apart
forming a pattern of 5" squares, or in lines a little closer
and alternating, forming a pattern of triangles 5" cach
way or 7 bunches along the circumference of a half circle
of about 2 diameter, 4 along the circumference of a
concentric half circle of about 10" diameter and one at
the radial centre. The last method means a little less

Although all this belongs properly to the Nyaung-ye
festival, many people place pots of water with thabye
sprigs before the shrines in their own homes at Thingyan
as well, and in villages it is quite common for people to
go to the pagoda and ceremonially pour water on the
shrines and bo trees during Thingyan as well as on the
following full moon day of Kahson.
The Nyaung-ye festival is mentioned in the song
Letwethondara wrote in exile at Meza hill in Katha
district in 1763 but the ceremonial pouring of water is
mentioned from much earlier times, sometimes in protes-
tation of innocence (Wareru, King of Pegu, 1287-96,
calling the spirits of air and earth to witness and pouring
water from a golden bowl before battle), sometimes at
the dedication of a pagoda or other work of merit (King
Kyanzittha and his son dedicating a golden image of the
Buddha, Myazedi inscription, 1112) sometimes with
imprecation (Narathihapati, King of Pagan, forced to
suicide by his son in 1287 pouring water over his ring
that he might never in any future life have man child
born to him again); and there is a popular belief that
two people who meet and quickly find themselves intimate
friends because of like tastes and like thoughts must
have poured water together in a previous existence.
King Anawrahta (1044-77) built pagodas, mona-
steries, rest-houses and irrigation works to share the
merit with his usurping fosterbrother whom he had slain
in battle for his throne. But broadly, a dedication of
any work of merit, from the building of a great pagoda
to just the ceremonial pouring of water at a Nyaung-ye
festival, is appropriation of the merit to oneself that it
may help one surmount the passions and attachments,
