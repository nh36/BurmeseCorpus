A CRITIQUE OF LOUIS J. WALINSKY'S
'ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT IN BURMA 1951-1960'*
Thet Tun**
Mr. Louis J. Walinsky, a partner in a Washington D.C. firm of economic consultants,
Robert R. Nathan Inc., spent about five and a half years in Burma from late 1953 until
early 1959 as chief economic adviser to the Burmese Government. After termination of
his firm's contract by the then Caretaker Government, he then spent over two years off
and on writing a voluminous work on his Burmese experiences. The book is in five parts,
which in the author's own words, are each 'full-bodied'.
Part I is background material, extracted from a few sources, almost all foreign and,
as admitted by the author himself, makes no pretence to authority. It therefore requires
no elaborate comments from me except to say that it was unfortunate that Mr. Walinsky
missed some good indigenous sources such as Daw Mya Sein's Administration of Burma
and U Tun Wai's Economic Development of Burma 1800-1940. His evaluation of the Two
Year Plan in the last chapter, which is in general agreement with my earlier ‘A Review of
Economic Planning in Burma', concluded with these words: 'When the American consul-
tants arrived in 1951, the Burmese already knew pretty well what they wanted – rehabili-
tation, better balance and structural reform in agriculture, rehabilitation and improvements
in transport and communications, and sizable new development in manufacturing industry
and electric power, all within the context of state socialism'.
-

American engineers and economists in 1952-53 and as it was later changed by the Burmese
authorities. According to the author, 'the actual programmes in manufacturing industry
and electric power were thus virtually independent of the KTA recommendations. The
substantial irrigation and highways programmes recommended by KTA never really got
started. The agricultural programme was in essence the Five Year Agricultural Plan plus
the independently developed land nationalization programme. And programmes for
health, education, housing and local improvements had been prepared by the Burmese
with United Nations and United States technical assistance'. What then was left of the
KTA Plan? Mr. Walinsky's curious answer to his own question was that the unique

knowledge and ideas about the Burmese economy. Viewed in this light, Mr. Walinsky's
indictment that 'the effectiveness of this (KTA survey's) contribution was limited by an
incapacity or unreadiness on the part of those whose appreciation was most required to
realize its full implications' is both preposterous and insulting to the parties concerned.
The story of the implementation of the development programme is beautifully
narrated in Part III of the book. Mr. Walinsky writes extremely well, using enough
figures in the text to dispense with frequent references to tables. He also did well to use
the 'crore' rather than the ‘million', as the magnitudes in Burmese economy lend them-
selves to easy reckoning in the former unit. But of course the story told is essentially
Mr. Walinsky's story. The world is not inhabited by economists alone and Burma is no
exception. Other dramatis personae including other economists could also have told
their own, and entirely different stories, without impinging too much upon truth.
The consultants fought and lost the battle in 1953-54 for a feasible rate of imple-
mentation, but of course this was after 'they had worked for the better part of two years
to persuade the Government to adopt an ambitious and aggressive approach'. In 1954-55
they engaged themselves in 'the fight for economic stability on the foreign exchange side',
after they had long ignored the repeated warnings of the Burmese officials, particularly at
the treasury and the central bank, on the looming foreign exchange crisis. In the 1955-56
fight for economic stability on the domestic inflation side, the consultants' main weapon
was the import of more and more textiles, as the lead factor in import prices, forgetting
of course that food and beverages form no less than 66 percent of the cost of living of
the masses.
The consultants' efforts bore fruit in the following year which saw the
doubling of textle imports, at a cost of K 35 crores of foreign exchange. Its net effect on
the cost of living index was a decline of a mere 1.3 percent in the following year. Mr.
Walinsky designated 1956-57 as the year of the fight for central responsiblity, programme
priorities and better performance. The preparation of the First Four Year Plan to replace
the second half of the over-ambitious Eight Year Plan might provide an apt illustration of
the central responsibility the consultants fought for. As of July 17, 1956, the American
consultants with their habitually optimistic resource estimates and the Burmese economists
with their relatively conservative forecast of resource position, struck a compromise at a
level of K 2031 crores of government capital expenditures for the period 1956-57 to
1959-60. The consultants then unilaterally revised the level upwards a few months later,
only to be confronted with realities as shown in the following table.

Then followed the year of political split, crisis and change in 1957-58. Soon after
assumption of office the Caretaker Government served notice of termination of services
on the consulting economists and engineers who left Burma in early 1959. Mr. Walinsky
described September 1958 to March 1960 as a period of 'cleanup and consolidation by the
military regime'. After a generally favourable appraisal of the house-cleaning efforts of
the Caretaker Government, he added a footnote which reads: 'Ironically, many of the
constructive actions taken by the Ne Win Government followed recommendations made
by the same economic sonsultants the military had abruptly dismissed, recommendations
not accepted or acted upon by the previous Government'. Then follows the usual laundry
list of economic do's and don'ts so typical of their high cost advice.
Whereas Part III tells the story of implemetation of the development programme in
a chronological order, Part IV describes the major sectors and projects in somewhat
greater detail. Mr. Walinsky's assessment of the outcomes and impact of the development
programme was that while production and income 'fell substantially short (about 50
percent) of the ambitious major goals' and 'the development programme did not bring
about revolutionary changes in the structure of the economy, either in the composition of
the nation's output or in the ownership and control of its wealth and productive processes',
'the scope and size - if not the quality - of the welfare facilities and services provided
during the development period were nothing short of remarkable'. All this is fair enough,
but when the appraisal continues that 'the impact of the programme went deep to the
core of the culture, where it registered with a certain degree of shock and initiated at least
the beginnings of a change which was bound to have a profound effect on the society and
its culture ultimately', it is certainly becoming vague and happily oblivious of the negative
virtue of the development programme having raised false hopes and frustrating the revolu-
tion of rising expectations in a newly independent country.

attempts in Part V to analyse major problems which affected implementation. The first

chapter discusses 'the problem of defects in the basic plan'. A more suitable title would
have been 'the problem of basic defects in the plan'. Mr. Walinsky admits to six major
defects in the plan including four false major assumptions of (1) an optimistic rice price
(2) a conservative estimate of rate of increase of government current expenditures (3) an
early cessation of the insurrection and (4) the assumption of administrative efficiency.
There were also weaknesses in sector programmes. Finally, according to the author, the
Burmese decision to place industrial development in the public sector, which constituted a
parameter for the planners, 'built into the plan an important element of weakness so far as
implementation was concerned'.
Since all other problems are discussed in the following chapters, only the assump-
tion about the international price of rice which was predicted by the consultants to hold
at no less than £ 50 sterling in 1959-60 but which in actual fact dropped to £ 32 by 1957-58
will be discussed here. Mr. Walinsky generously shares the honour of this miscalculation
with the experts of the U.S. Department of Agriculture and the Food and Agriculture Organ-
ization of the United Nations but fails to give credit to a Burmese member of the UN Econo-
mic Commission for Asia and the Far East, Dr. U Tun Wai, who warned as early as mid-
1952 of an impending break in the world price of rice. In connection with the many vital
consequences of this faulty rice price assumption, Mr. Walinsky has countered my earlier
criticism that it was responsible also for the planners' neglect to stress projects with a low
import content with the arguments that 'since a more conservative assumption about rice
prices would have resulted in plans for borrowing from abroad at the very outset of the
programme, it would not have been necessary to place undue weight on foreign exchange
saving, and ‘more important, because of the long-run outlook for a favourable balance of
trade beyond the eight year programme period, it would not have been economic to
overemphasize these foreign exchange criteria in relation to others'. Mr. Walinsky appears
to forget that at that time Burma had for political reasons asked the US Government to
discontinue its ECA aid and that plans for foreign borrowing could not have been enter-
tained then. As for the latter argument, my only comment is that it bears testimony once
again to the daring with which the consultants made forecasts about a developing
export economy.
Instead of asking when the civil insurrection would be over, the consultants wrote
in the assumption that it would end in a year or two and then asked the political leaders
of the day to either confirm or deny it. Coming from a democracy themselves, the
consultants should at least have known better than that. With regard to the incompa-
tibility of the fear of the Bureau of Special Investigation with the need for prompt
commercial and economic decision-making by government servants, what the author has
to say is only too true. One only wonders why the consultants never broached this
subject to the Burmese authorities during their stay here.
With regard to the expansion of general education and the training of specialized
manpower, the author appears to have been unduly impressed with the results achieved.
He seems to overlook the fact that expansion of general education has been in numbers

‘Neither in finance, nor in the many-faceted effort required to mobilize it, were the
Burmese ready and willing to pay the "price" of development. This reluctance, rather
than inability to provide the necessary finance, was the major problem', complains the
author. Granted this natural tendency to have the cake and eat it too on the part of a
low income nation when faced with an operation 'boot-strap', the consultants seem to
have overlooked the fact that taxes in Burma have already taken up no less than 20
percent of the Gross Domestic Product as the profits of the State Agricultural Marketing
Board are nothing but taxes on the farmers. The author also deplores the failure of the
Government to follow recommendations of Professor R. A. Musgrave to broaden and
strengthen the Burmese tax structure. But as ably pointed out by E. F. Schumacher,
Musgrave's recommendations, excellent as they are for a predominantly private enterprise
economy, would not be suitable for an economy like that of Burma with a sizable public
sector and a fairly equal distribution of income already achieved. For example, Dr.
Musgrave advocated increased reliance on income-tax by the lowering of exemption limits
rather than raising customs duties or deriving profits from the operation of public
enterprises.
Mr. Walinsky dismisses the problem of devaluation of the Burmese kyat in less
than a page and a half. He maintains that possible advantages of devaluation were
discussed amongst consultants in 1955-56 (a rather late timing, judging by the break in
the world price of rice in 1953), such as enhanced government revenues, ability to pay a
higher paddy price to cultivators and greater competitiveness for other exports without
further raising prices of imported goods to the consumers who were already paying prices
50 to 100 percent higher than landed cost. However ‘continued doubt whether a deva-
luation of the currency and a higher domestic price for paddy would indeed result in a
substantial increase in paddy production combined with the conviction that dynamic action
to strengthen the tax structure and to improve the efficiency of boards and corporations
operations could solve the finance problem, influenced the economic consultants against
recommending devaluation'. The glaring case of another agricultural crop, namely,
groundnut, where an attractive price stimulated production to the point of self-sufficiency
to-day should bear sufficient testimony to the error of this judgement.

author discusses, those concerning the co-ordination of current and capital budgets and
the administration of the budget deserve comments. Owing to lack of adequate
co-ordination between the Finance and Planning Ministries, 'the current budget fre-
quently, and sometimes seriously, failed to make provision for essential expenditures
complementary to the capital programme. It is indeed a pity that the concept of 'capital
outlay' used in India and elsewhere, which includes current expenditures complementary

Coming from a country with a different governmental set-up and therefore innocent of the
British system of financial administration, a colonial version of which the Burmese system
was based upon, the consultants also failed to develop their advice on financial decentrali-
zation into sufficiently concrete form.
Having made a rather clumsy classification of public enterprises into six functional
groups of (1) public utilities (2) trading organisations (3) development corporations
(4) manufacturing industries, (5) service organizations and (6) miscellaneous group,
(a simpler grouping could have been among (1) trading (2) production and (3) service
organizations), the author ascribes the failure of public enterprise management in Burma
to (a) lack of autonomy (b) lack of experience (c) lack of initiative (d) poor co-ordination
(e) lack of clear-cut and appropriate government policies (f) lack of adequate accounting
and (g) lack of effective supervision and control. The root of the problem is of course
lack of clear delegation of authority backed by a positive system of reward and punish-
ment. In the same way as it is inappropriate for executive government to copy the
committee system from the parliament, it is usually fatal to transplant the board of
directors in private enterprise where it is a necessity, to public enterprise where it is not.
Add to this the Burmans' thoroughgoing individualism and the result is either inertia or
chaos. In this connection, the recent practice of appointing an individual controller for
each state industrial enterprise may prove to be an interesting experiment. Mr. Walinsky's
prescription of management contracts with experienced foreign firms appears to be of
dubious value without the basis of a joint-ventureship as borne out by the case of the
Evans Medical Ltd.
In an assessment of the performance of the governmental machinery for supervision
and co-ordination at the highest level, namely, the Economic and Social Board and later
the National Planning Commission, the author attributes its failings to (1) the difficulty of
getting important issues before the board (2) the problem of getting firm and clear
decisions (3) weak implementation of decisions (4) failure to check on programme progress
and performance (5) weakness in the secretariat (6) confusion in the supervisory responsi-
bility and (7) confusion between the planning and supervisory agencies. In connection
with the first three weaknesses, Mr. Walinsky had some very astute observations to make.
"Questions of basic policy, organization, procedure and management had no dead-lines-
Most questions of far less importance did'. 'When they (the consultants) took a negative
position, the Board almost always upheld them. When they sought affirmative action,
they were much less successful'. 'This identification of word with deed, of order with
execution, was quite pervasive'. With regard to progress checking of projects, on the
spot field visits by individuals with authority have been found more effective than whole
committees poring over out-of-date progress reports on paper. The weakness in the
secretariat was due as much to the lack of a really expert staff servicing the Board as to
the inadequacy of technical competence or initiative of its head.

of top administrative personnel through attrition and early retirement, extremely poor pay
scales, stultifying procedures, political abuses and many other factors. He has earlier
admitted however that 'the planners had not adequately appreciated the need for a
thorough overhaul of the entire Government administrative apparatus and a similar
overhaul of its operating procedures to permit effective administration at intermediate and
lower levels as well as at the very top, with which they had been more concerned'. At the
same time he claims that 'the economic consultants, as has been amply noted, continued
to point out the weeknesses and the necessity for corrective action, both in general and
specific terms, and with increasing emphasis, over the years'. Apparently from lack of
real insight into the problems of public administration in Burma, the consultants never
got around to proposing the fundamental reforms of integration of ministries and depart-
ments and unification of the civil service. On the contrary, for reasons best known to
themselves, the consultants appeared to have favoured the organizational status quo and
to have feared the replacement of the amateurs by skilled civil servants.
Mr. Walinsky is highly critical of the Burmese Government's 'strongest and most
clearly defined central economic policies' of nationalization, Burmanization and industria-
lization. The consultants are of course entitled to their own economic opinion, but what
one cannot get over is why advisers with an ideology so divergent from the client's were
hired at all. Among other economic policies, the so-called anti-inflationary imports policy
advocated by the economic consultants needs scrutiny. Actually, as Schumacher has
pointed out, 'the repeated emphasis on the desirability of large import surpluses and of
encouraging the people's propensity to import was ill-conceived" since 'what could be a
more disastrous pre-occupation of any government interested in economic development
than the dissipation of foreign exchange reserves on consumer goods imports!' In respect
of housing policy Mr. Walinsky advocated a low-cost housing programme carried out by
private builders with simple incentives like ten-year tax exemptions and low-interest loan
capital. He appears however to have overlooked the fact that without bringing down by
means of increased production the prices of building materials from the high levels they
had attained, no low-cost housing, at a cost which would be covered by the low rentals
people could afford to pay, could have been sponsored either by the government or the
private sector.
It was on the recommendation of the ECA under the Truman Administration that
the Burmese Government engaged in 1951 the services of the KTA engineers and RRN
economists, many of them with Democratic political affiliations. With the change of the
Administration in the United States in 1952, for the rest of the period, Burma Government
was in fact employing Democrats as their spokesmen in their aid requests to the Republican
Administration. (An analogy would be using the NUF economic experts in asking
favours from the AFPFL Government!)

The private firms, such as Mr. Walinsky's own outfit, ‘were in a much better position
to select competent people, and to see to it that they were adequately paid. They also had
direct access to Government officials at high levels ... and ... also provided a desirable
continuity of experience. Job performance was better and turnover was lower'. The
foundations combined the advantages enjoyed by the private firms and those which came
with active, self-chosen 'do-it' assignments. The Israeli technicians were quite outstanding
because they were hand-picked, hardy, sympathetic, etc.
Looked as from another angle, the extraordinary virtues of the private advisory
firms the author has eloquently extolled can appear as ordinary vices. Continuity of
experience, or in other words renewal of profitable contracts, may be obtained by cajolery
and ‘politicking' rather than by giving good and blunt advice. 'Direct access' to top
levels may be necessary to avoid what the author describes as 'those baffling occasions
when a Burmese official was able, in open meeting, to refute a position or an analysis
offered by one of the foreign experts'. The constant need of the firm to justify its own
existence naturally 'developed friction when the local staff was placed on an independent
work status'. A passage from the author's discussion of the process of annual budgeting
and programming might illustrate this 'one-up-manship'. The author writes: 'Toward the
latter part of the period, the CSED prepared independent estimates of resources, require-
ments and the like, and also independent analyses of capital budget submissions for consi-
deration by the capital budget review group. By this time the consultants were able to
take the quite capable CSED estimates as a point of departure, rather than carry through
the programming exercise from the beginning on their own'. Characteristically, the author
does not mention how much progress the consultants made from the 'point of departure”.
In the last part of his book, three chapters in Part VI, the author
offers his conclusions and appraisals as 'significance of the experience' for
Burma's further economic development, in other countries and for US
and Western aid policy. Mr. Walinsky now produces a consolidated laundry
list of essentials of a programme for Burma's further economic development. The
list includes concrete commonsense projects for paddy expansion, self-sufficiency in or
larger export of other agricultural crops, improved rice-milling and saw-milling capacity
and an industrial development bank as well as somewhat general programmes like a
‘comprehensive and definitive mineral resource survey', 'completion of the rehabilitation
of transport and communications', additional technical training facilities, etc. Then, as
measures more important than and basic to these projects and programmes, Mr. Walinsky
advocates improvements in the spheres of politics and public administration. As final
assault on the obstacles to economic development of Burma, Mr. Walinsky urges
denationalization of state trading and manufacturing and promotion of the private sector.
'A comprehensive development programme built on such foundations', Mr. Walinsky
concludes, 'would produce astounding results'. What in my opinion is really astouding
is that several years' experience of economic development in Burma should have culmi-
nated in this economic widsom of vague generalities tampered by a few commonsense

The moral for other developing nations Mr. Walinsky draws from the Burmese

leadership, good political system, good implementation, good public administration, good
economic policies and good faith in private enterprise. A corollary is that by that time
