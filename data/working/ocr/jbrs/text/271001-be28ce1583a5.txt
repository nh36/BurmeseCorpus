EVENTS AT THE COURT AND CAPITAL OF AVA
DURING THE FIRST ANGLO-BURMESE WAR*.
Researchers and writers have provided us with quite good
accounts of the first Burmese War - its causes, the Burmese
initial successes in Assam and at Ramoo, and later the vain
attempt of Bundoola to stem the progress of British arms in
Lower Burma. The victorious advance of the Indo-British Expedi-
tionary Force from Rangoon to Yandabo has also been pretty.
adequately dealt with. In this paper it is proposed to lift the
curtain in order to reveal the happenings at the Royal Capital of
Ava during the course of the war. Information on this subject
I have gathered as a kind of a by-product during the course of my
researches into the History of the British Residency in Burma.
There is enough material on the subject of to night's lecture to
enable one to write a fair-sized book. I will not attempt to deal
with all or even most of the events which occurred at Ava. I shall
pick and choose facts most suited to the purpose in hand.
The sources of the subject are, firstly, a short but important
document which I came across in Vol. 339 of the Bengal Secret
and Political Consultations, 5th May, 1826, No. 10. This document
was composed by a European resident in Ava during the course
of the war. He refrains from attaching his name to the document
which was submitted to the Government of India through Mr. J. C.
Robertson, Civil Commissioner and one of the signatories of the
Treaty of Yandabo. On forwarding the document to the Supreme
Government, Robertson wrote: "In submitting the accompanying
paper to the consideration of His Lordship-in-Council I deem it
necessary to mention, that it has been drawn up by a gentleman
thoroughly conversant with the state of the Court of Ava, well
versed in the language, and intimately acquainted with the
character and manners of the Burmese. Although for obvious
reasons he does not wish to affix his name to a public document of
this description I can yet from my own acquaintance with the
writer, venture to assure His Lordship-in-Council that his narrative
may be safely received as a fair and faithful recital of events of
which he was himself an observed witness"." Other authorities
consulted and cited are Adoniram Judson and his wife the faithful
and devoted Anne Hasseltine Judson; then, Gouger, John Laird,
Snodgrass, Crawfurd, Trant, White and Laurie.

Bagyidaw (King of Burma, 1819-1837), the Royal Family,
his Ministers and the people in general had altogether too great an
idea of the power of their country. Having practically no
knowledge of world developments they had naturally no concep-
tion whatever of the realities of the British power. The tradition,
and so the firm belief, was, that no power could be mightier than
the Burmese, and no King greater than His Burmanic Majesty.
It was under these ideas that the King and his Government with
alacrity went to war with the British in 1824.

"For more than a 12 month antecedent to the breaking out of
the War, a feeling of bravado and a disposition to underrate the
British power in India, were universally manifested." Early in
the year 1823, Menthagyes, brother of the Queen, and the first
officer of the state, asked the opinion of the writer of the afore-
mentioned document on the subject of the comparative strength
of the Burmese and British nations. He replied that the British
were certainly superior: they had a large number of well-
disciplined troops always in readiness, and they manufactured
modern articles of a warlike nature not only for themselves but
also sold them to other countries. Menthagyee agreed that the
Burmans were unacquainted with discipline in the open field, but
contended that they were adepts at all sorts of stockade and bank
fighting, and that the forest, which would destroy a British army,
would serve them both for defence and food. Menthagyee
also claimed that if occasion offered they might easily take
Bengal".2

The Burmese had annexed Assam in 1819, but the fugitive

On Sunday, 23rd May, news arrived of the fall of Rangoon. It
spread like wild fire, and roused a spirit of bravado and vengeance.
The Kyee Woongyee was hurried off with an army of 12,000, with
the order to rouse the whole lower country, if necessary, against
the "rebellious" invaders. The King feared the British troops.
might abscond before the Woongyee arrived to apprehend them;
this would be very undesirable because then all the prize and spoil
to be gained from the capture of the English force would be
missed.¹ "The whole city", says Gouger, was convulsed with

there was
a feeling of satisfaction and exultation that the white
foreigners had entered into a trap from which they could not es-

had this delusion taken, that the only fear now expressed was that
their enemies would plunder the town and escape before the Royal
army had time to arrive and give them battle. Their expressions

buoyancy of feeling gave the town the resemblance of a joyous
festival, intermingled with fits of frantic rage. Many songs were

one of them was singularly refined and elegant. It contemplated

hides!"

demand of the British. The Treaty was therefore signed and
duly ratified in the Privy Council without any argument or
protest.
Thus ended the war; but it left a deep impression upon the
intelligentzia of the country. The Ministers were now convinced
of the military weakness of their country, and were determined not
to come to blows with the British again. The King's mind,
however, was embittered. He felt deeply humiliated, and from
this time onward he looked upon Europeans with suspicion. He
hated the very thought that he had agreed to the treaty; but his
desire was to save his capital, and preserve his dignity by not
fleeing from it.
