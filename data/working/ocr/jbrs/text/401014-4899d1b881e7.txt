SOME ASPECTS OF SEASONAL AGRICULTURAL
LOANS IN BURMA '
by
U Aye Hlaing
Seasonal agricultural loans are by far the most important type of
agricultural credit needed by the great majority of the cultivators in
Burma. In this paper an estimate of the annual requirements of
seasonal loans will be made and certain economic aspects of it will be
considered. The problems of provision of seasonal loans as they exist
at present will be discussed without trying to explain historically how
the present situation of rural indebtedness has come about.
Having described the scope of the paper briefly we may now
proceed to consider estimates of the annual requirements of seasonal
loans. For that purpose we may adopt a working definition of
seasonal loan requirements as follow. It is well known that farmers
borrow money to pay wages to some of the outside labourers they hire
e.g. transplanters and harvestors. Some of them need money to buy
seeds, foodstuffs and other things for themselves and their agricultural
labourers, if any. However, the great majority of farmers do not have
any funds in their hands at the beginning of the agricultural season.
Therefore loans are required to meet the paid out cost to be incurred
during the season. In other words the amount of loans required is
taken to be approximately equal to the paid out costs.
Estimates of seasonal loan requirements have been made before
and we refer particularly to those made by the Provincial Banking
Enquiry Committee 3 and by Mr. B. O. Binns, * One of the estimates
given in this paper is based on their estimates. The present estimates
were made by me when I was working in the Economic Division of the
Land and Agricultural Planning Commission, Burma, in 1954. They
are primarily based on the 1953 Census data because at that time the

managed to enumerate 47,644 farmers cultivating a total area of four

that these farmers are representative of the agricultural population of
Burma. Their proximity to the towns must have influenced the types

of loans borrowed is not as great as might have been expected.
The first approximation of the loan requirements was obtained by
multiplying the average amount of loans per acre given in the Census
by the sown acreage in 1953-54 as given by the office of the Com-
missioner of Settlement and Land Records. The former was K. 18.02
(See Table I in the Appendix) and the latter was 16.16 million acres
and therefore we get a total of about K. 291 millions. The implicit
assumption here is that the amount borrowed given in the Census is
approximately equal to the amount required. However, it must be
pointed out that there are some limitations in using the Census data in
so far as the above assumption is concerned. Firstly, the Census data
is based on information admitted by the farmers. Secondly, the
amount borrowed is not necessarily equal to the amount required. In
fact, there is reason to believe that the latter is greater than the former.
For instance, the unsatisfied demand for loans, as we shall see presently
has been reflected in increasing adoption of broadcasting method in
place of transplanting in recent years. These limitations will be
considered again later on.
In addition to these limitations it can be seen from Table 1 in the
Appendix that the variation in the average amount of loans per acre
from district to district is fairly large, ranging from K. 2.2 per acre in
Mergui District to K. 70 in Insein District. Therefore one might think
that in taking the average amout of loans per acre for the country as a
whole the extreme values should be excluded. If we exclude Insein,
Tavoy, Mergui, Shwebo, Upper Chindwin, Bhamo and Myitkyina.
districts from our calculation the average amount of loans per acre
would be K. 18.5 and therefore the total estimated requirements would
be about K, 299 millions. The difference is not significant. Neverthe-

information from other sources. One is based on the estimates given
by Mr. Binns in his book referred to above. He puts the seasonal
loan requirements for Lower Burma at Rs. 6 per acre and Rs. 3 for
Upper Burma. We inflate these figures by index numbers of agricultural
prices; using the index number of prices of cereals only for Lower
Burma and general index for Upper Burma. The relevant index
numbers for the years from 1951 to 1954 are given in Table II ( a ) in the
Appendix. We therefore inflate the Lower Burma estimated requirement
by 3 times and that of Upper Burma by 5 times giving us K. 18 and
K. 15 as the per acre loan requirements of Lower and Upper Burma
respectively. In addition we allow for the recent tendency for the
labourers to ask for wages in cash rather than in kind and therefore
we raise those figures to K. 20 and K. 16 respectively. Referring back
to the Census data we can see that in general the loan per acre is higher
in Lower Burma districts than in Upper Burma districts and the
estimates we have arrived at seem to be close to the Census data.
Therefore we apply these rates to the area sown in Lower Burma
and Upper Burma in 1953-54. This gives a total of K. 295.8 millions
as given in table II (b).

However, as mentioned above, the first two estimates are based on
the assumption that the amount borrowed given in the Census is the
amount required. We have pointed out that it seems to under-estimate
the loan requirements. On the other hand the fourth estimate tends
to over-estimate the requirements because there would doubtless be
some farmers who are able to meet some part of their paid out costs.
Consequently the difference between these estimates is to be expected.
We may therefore put the annual requirements of the seasonal loans as
of the order of K. 350 millions.
It is convenient here to mention a few other interesting facts from
the Census date. In 1953-54 the total short term loans given by the
Government was about K. 55 millions which is about 16 per cent of
the total estimated loan requirement. Assuming that amount had
actually been borrowed in that season then the Government was supply-
ing only 16 per cent of the total loans. Whereas according to the
Census the Government loans were about 30 per cent of the total.
It seems therefore that there has been undue concentration of Govern-
ment loans in the urban area as the 1953 Census covered the urban
area only. Moreover the private sources of credit still remain the major
sources of credit for the cultivators. The other point is regarding the
interest rates; for the Government loans the average rate of interest was
7 per cent and for the private cash loans 48 per cent and for private
loans in cash and in kind 60 per cent.
It will now be convenient to go from the statistical picture drawn
by the foregoing estimates of seasonal loan requirements and look at
the economic aspect of the problem. The first question is why
does the farmer need any loan? Why can he not finance his own
capital requirements for current expenses? This is a fundamental
problem. The dependence of farmers on external sources of capital
even for his short term operations is not a peculiar feature of the
Burmese farmer only but prevails almost all over the world where
agriculture is a poor industry. For a great majority of farmers, after
the harvest has been gathered in and sold, the sale proceeds are used up
to repay the loans incurred during the season. Some of them hand
over the greater part of their crop as repayment. Therefore they have
to borrow again even long before the beginning of the next season;
and when the season starts more money will be needed to meet the
expenses to pay the outside labourers, in some cases, to buy seeds and

to buy foodstuffs and other things for themselves. In the course of
village surveys conducted by the University Economic Research Project
it has been found that only a few farmers are able to keep back a stock
of paddy for their own consumption. Most of them are therefore
living on hand to mouth båsis.
Although such a situation is not uncommon, it is nonetheless a
serious state of affairs. This has always kept the farming population
at the mercy of money lenders, who have found in the exploitation of
agriculturists a profitable field for investment. The high rate of
interest may be justified by some people on a number of considerations
such as high degree of imaginary or real risk and the scarcity of capital
in the country. All the same such a state of affairs reduces the share
of the cultivators of the total value of the product making it more
difficult for him to save and to make improvement on his farm.
Another consequence of this is that the loanable funds of the country
are not diverted to industrial investments so long as the profits of
lending to farmers remain as high as they are.
On the part of farmers we may consider an example of how in
recent times the high cost and difficulty of borrowing has caused some
deterioration in agricultural productivity. In one of the surveys, the
University Economic Research Project team has obtained a calculation
showing the relative advantages of transplanting paddy compared with
the method of broadcasting for a typical 15-acre farm in the Insein
district.
A basket of seeds, which is required for one acre, yields 125
bundles of seedlings in the nursery. The cost of plucking them is K. 6
per 100 bundles and therefore for one acre the cost of plucking is
K. 7.50. 6 or 7 women are needed to transplant these seedlings in one
day; their wages are at K. 1.50 per day with food or K. 2 without
food. For fifteen acres we may put the cost of transplanting at K. 180
and therefore the total cost would come to about K. 300.
On the other hand the cost of broadcasting the seeds on the same
farm would cost about K. 150 including the extra amount of seed that
is required by this method. We may therefore estimate the cost of
transplanting to be about K. 150 more than the broadcasting for the
15 acres.

Farmers claim that transplanting increases the yield by as much as
10 baskets per acre as compared with broadcasting method. We may
take a lower estimate of 100 baskets for a 15 acre-farm. This leads
to an increased output valued at K. 300 for the farm. According to this
calculation the transplanting method would give a greater advantage
over that of broadcasting and yet there has been a distinct trend towards
more and more broadcasting in recent years. This is for two main
reasons; one is the shortage of labour and the other is the difficulty of
getting loans which even when available carry such a high rate of
interest that the interest charges may wipe out any advantage of using
the transplanting method. This is only one example of how the
problems of credit may effect the whole life of the agricultural
population.
The next interesting fact I want to bring out is the implication of
cheap loans to the farmers. Earlier I have quoted the level of interest
rates as given by the 1953 census; that of the Government loans was
about 7 per cent, that of private cash loans was about 48 per cent and
that of loans in cash and in kind was about 60 per cent. The most
conservative estimate of average loan requirements of a paddy cultiva-
tor in the Delta is about 300 kyats per year. If the cultivator were
able to get the entire amount of loans at the rate charged by the
Government the saving on the interest charges alone would be about
K. 120. The gross income of such a cultivator, according to the
Census, is about K. 1,000, so that the saving on interest charges would
be about 12 per cent of the gross income. But the more relevent
concept to use would be the net income which is given as between
K. 400 K, 500 per year. The saving would come to between 25 and
30 per cent of the net income. We at once see the tremendous benefit
that the farmers would be able to reap if the Government were able to
meet the entire loan requirements of the farmers.
We know that even at the time of the most liberal scale of
Government finance of agricultural credit, the state loans amounted to
only about 60 million kyats which is onty about one sixth of the
total requirement. The rest had to be obtained from private sources.
This leads us to the basic issues regarding problems of policy of
state agricultural credit. Agricultural credit is of fundamental import-
ance in promoting the welfare of the farm population of Burma, which
constitutes the vast majority as well as the source of the country's

economic strength. The Government has been well aware of this
great importance and has made a number of efforts to find a solution,
starting from measures to strengthen the pre-war legislation regarding
money lenders, land alienation and leading to expansion of direct
lending on a level never imagined before the emergence of the national
Government. It is also aware of the tremendous benefit that it could
bestow on the farm population by the expansion of the credit facilities.
Yet no Government can continue to carry out a programme of increased
state financing of agriculture with the present level of non-repayment
of loans. The Government's answer so far has been to insist more
and more on collective responsibility as the main weapon for securing
higher rate of repayment. This has been the basic method with all
channels of lending; either through the general administration, or the
co-operatives, or the new system of agricultural banks and the agency
loans. Up to the present we feel that recovery rates are still not satis-
factory and the system of state credit cannot be continued for a long
time, not to say of expansion, at present rates of repayment. It has
been suggested that the defect is merely one of not having strictly
enforced the principle of collective responsibility and that had this
principle been rigidly adhered to the results would have been more
satisfactory. But in the course of a number of village surveys
made under the University Economic Research Project it seems to
some of us that the sccial basis of village life, which has been
undermined by the disturbances in the country during the last 10 to 15
years, has not been sufficiently recovered. Under these conditions, it
may be difficult to produce a sense of collective responsibility among
the villagers and to rely on the social pressure to prevent wilful default
on the state loans. The situation has been made more difficult by
making an entire village or a village tract as the primary unit of
lending. Under these conditions it seems to me futile to ask for
collective action of any sort, even for something which is of such
great benefit to all of them. It seems as if we may have to give up
collective responsibility as the basis of state agricultural credit.
What then is the answer to the problem?• The conclusion is
inescapable that the state must proceed on the basis of individual
contracts or with a smaller group of persons than an entire village in
issuing its agricultural loans and then insist stringently on the principle
that a default for one season would deprive a farmer or the group of
any further access to state source of funds.

This method would, of course, have its own problems and difficul-
ties. It would require a very large organisation with its roots, if not
at the village level, at least at the township level. The sheer expense
of undertaking this organisation would be formidable. There is also
the greater difficulty of control of such an organisation. One way of
reducing the cost of such an organisation would be to combine some
other functions which the state-employed loans officers would have to
carry out in addition to the issue of agricultural loans on an individual
basis. It is natural to think of the officers appointed for this purpose
to undertake the measures to improve marketing methods in the village
level. This would require improvements in agricultural practice,
processing, grading and storage of produce, and the finding of the best.
market. In particular, it would involve the presence of a well-trained
person to educate farmers in undertaking these operations connected
with their agriculture on a co-operative basis. Thus while this sugges-
tion of lending on an individual basis may for the time being run
counter to the dominant tendencies of state policy, it is to be hoped that
it will help to establish in an enduring way the co-operative basis of the
nation's agricultural economy, a hope which finds a place in the
Constitution of Burma itself.
