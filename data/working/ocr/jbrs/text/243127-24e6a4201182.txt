THE NORTHERN HILLS OF THE PONNAGYUN TOWNSHIP*
BY
U BA MYAING
(Assistant Judge, Prome)
I. INTRODUCTION
The following notes on the manners, customs and religion of the people who
inhabit the northern hilly portion of the Ponnagyun township of the Akyab
district were made during my census duty in that area in 1921, and are
based chiefly upon information received in daily conversation with villagers
as I proceeded from village to village. I have not attempted at any originality
but have recorded the facts as they were told me.
2. The area with which my notes are concerned consists, as I have
noted, of the northern hilly portion of the Ponnagyun Township. On the
north, the Baedaung Khan Zine separates it from the Paletwa Township and
the Gaung So creek from Buthidaung Township. On the south, the Chaung
Bauk Wa village and Nishe village separate it from the rest of the synchro-
nous area. On the east, the Agu Taung hill divides it from Kyauktaw
Township. On the west, a low range of hills not far from the village of Ain
Wun running north and south separates it from Rathedaung Township.
3. The peoples inhabiting this area are the Kami, the Mro and the
Chaungtha. The Kami are of two kinds, the Ayaing and the Awa. In my
notes Kami (Ayaing), Kami (Awa) and Mro are dealt with separately. It
may be urged that Kami (Ayaing) and Kami (Awa) are one and the
same race. But there is a certain degree of difference with regard to modes,
manners and religious beliefs; and I think it best they should be treated
separately in order that the difference may appear more clearly. I have not
dealt with the Chaungtha race which is generally better known than the three
I have mentioned.
4. Going up the Yochaung we find the Kami (Awa) and then the Mro and
enter upon the Kami whom the Kami (Awa) call Ayaing. Some of the Mros
are taking the Kami (Ayaing) to wives and talking the language of their
wives; while the Kami (Awa) have not done so. Coming down the Taw-
hpya stream from the north we find first the Kami Ayaing, then the Mro and
last of all the Kami Awa.
5. The Mro generally have high cheekbones, broad foreheads and slop-
ing shoulders; they are darker in complexion and much taller than the
Kamis of either kind; their women tend to be ugly. The Kami complexion
is rather fair.
6. The words Kami and Mro in the languages of those names mean
66 man".

7. The Kamis who were called Ayaing and who talked the Kami
(Ayaing) language can hardly be said to live in a village. Most of them
live in their taungya or not far from it. The village varies in size from two
to ten houses. The village is usually on the top of a hill or along-side a
hill. There is always a main road passing through the village and the houses
lie on both sides of the road facing towards it. In a few cases want of space
has prevented the houses being built along the road. The average breadth
of the house is from 12 to 25 feet. The length varies from 20 to 56 feet.
The posts are of wood, the floor and walls of bamboo split open with the
inner portion of the knots removed and the roof of bamboo leaves or thatch.
The height of the floor from the ground is 4 to 6 feet. The roof falls from
a central ridge and meets the side walls at 4 to 6 feet from the floor. The
front and back walls also rise up to the roof. The walls inside, beams and
rafters are black with soot as cooking is done inside the hall and there is no
opening to let the smoke out.

14. Kami (Awa) women also wear short skirts open to one side. But
their skirts are longer and broader than those of the Mros, and are
worn in the same way as old Burmese women wear theirs when dressed
up for a special occasion. Besides the skirt each woman wears a broad
piece of cloth which passes under the left arm and covers both the breasts;
a small string ties the two ends of this cloth together above the right
shoulder.
15. All Kami and Mro women are fond of coloured beads which they
string together to make necklaces. Silver bangles are worn, but not silver
anklets.
16. The Kami (Ayaing) men do not wear the cloth woven by their own
women. They buy the cloth for their "lungyi" elsewhere. Their "lungyi"
which they call "Am Muck" is a long piece of cloth-they wear any colour
except the black colour-which is wound round the waist and then tucked up
by taking one end of the cloth from the front between the thighs and tying
it at the back. They tie their hair into a knot like the Arakanese and wear
white turbans round the head. The majority of them have vests or Burman
coats which they wear on special occasions.
17. The Kami (Awa) men dress like the Kami (Ayaing). Many of
them have also Arakanese thread lungyis which they wear occasionally.
18. Mro men also dress exactly like the Kami (Ayaing); but they have
also ni nha (lungyis) woven by the women of the house. Their favourite
colour is black.

husband. The marriage feast is over.

24. Amongst the Kami (Ayaing) if a man marries another man's wife,
he has to pay compensation which is at most Rs. 100/-., to the husband. In
addition, he has to kill a pig and besmear the shoulder of the first husband.
with its blood. In case of Mro also money has to be given as compensation
according to the wealth of the men. But no killing of the pig is necessary.
Kami (Awa) however do not seem to have any prevailing rule. The Court
is ordinarily sought for justice.
25. One of the most curious customs prevailing among the people
[Kami (Ayaing), Mro, Kami (Awa) ] is the right of eating together. A man
who has taken to wife a woman from a family cannot eat together fowl
curry with that family nor can he have fowl curry in the house of that
family. The member of that family, however, can have fowl curry in his
house. Strangers may eat fowl curry together. Two men who have married
women from one family may eat fowl curry together. At every feast the
rule of eating together fowl is strictly observed. There is no restriction
with regard to eating of other eatables together. There is restriction with
regard to fowl curry only.
V. BIRTH, NAMING AND EAR-BORING
26. Kami (Ayaing). On the third day after the child's birth, a pig
is killed and cow-heads and other articles in the house are besmeared with
blood. Pieces of thread are also besmeared with the blood and tied round
the right wrist of the members including the new born child of the house.
The thread is tied on by an elder member of the house who, at the time of
doing it, would bite a piece of ginger and say "Phyauk! May all of us live
together long. We have now killed a pig." Friends are invited and fed with
pork curry. Before they have their meal, the child's ears are bored with
the needle and a name is given to the child by a member-generally his

27. Mro. The naming of the child takes place a day after its birth.

child so called accordingly. If both the shells or leaves or gingers have the

bad fortune and ill-luck. Another name has then to be suggested and the
process of throwing done until the lucky sign has been obtained. A bamboo
pole is taken and the outer cover of it scraped and the bamboo slung, as it

five knots of the bamboo pole which is then placed at the top of the stairs.

the "khaung" and biting a small piece of ginger, takes up a sharpened
pointed bamboo stick and saying "Phyauk! We are killing a pig. May the
child be free from illness", kills the pig by piercing it with the bamboo point.
The pig's right ear is then cut off and cut into small slices. Each slice is

taken and cooked in a bamboo for the purpose of offering to the spirit. The
rest of the pork is cooked in a pot for the people to eat. When the pork

a leaf on the "khaung" pot, a leaf on the "Hama", a leaf at each

inside the hall, a leaf on the gong, a leaf on the dah and sickles.
Two leaves are left for later purposes. A little of the boiled rice is taken

28. Kami (Awa).-There is no curious ceremony among these people
at the time of child-birth. No killing of the pig nor the tying of the string
is necessary. The mid-wife who performs the work at child-birth smears
her joints with a piece of ginger. The navel cord can be cut by any
bamboo.
29. Ear-boring.-Among the Mros and Kami (Awa) a child's ears
may be bored at any time within one year or on the 3rd, 5th and 7th year.
Ear-boring is not allowed in the 2nd, 4th and 6th years.
VI. DEATH AND BURIAL
30. If a woman of the house dies of child-birth, all the utensils and
properties in the house together with the house have to be abandoned and
a fresh house has to be built and fresh utensils obtained. For this reason,
valuable and costly articles are removed before the woman dies. As soon as
the woman dies all things in the house cannot be used after the crema-
tion of the dead body. This custom holds good for the Kami (Ayaing),
Mros and Kami (Awa).
31. In the case of the Kamis (Ayaing) the husband-if the husband be
dead, then the head of the house where the woman lives-refrains from eating
salt for 9 days and from eating meat and fish and vegetables for 30 days.
The Mros also follow this custom, though a very small percentage of them
has dropped off this practice. The Kamis (Awa) have no such custom.
32. Kamis (Ayaing).-When a person dies, the dead body is put on a
blanket under which a bamboo mat has been placed. Two or three blankets
are then put over the dead body which is kept in the middle of the hall.
The next day a bamboo coffin - like a basket - is made and placed on the shelf
near the roof of the house. A fowl or pig or cow, according to the means
of the house is killed. Some blood is put on the dead body. The fowl or
pork is cooked and eaten by all. The next day a small house is built in the
jungle not far from the place where the body is to be cremated. On this
day, a pig and a dog are killed and the people who build the small house
which is to be used later for keeping the bone-ash are fed with the pork curry
and the curry of dog's flesh. In the evening the "khaung" is freely drunk
and the visitors are fed with all kinds of curry such as can be obtained. This
drinking and feasting will continue for at least five days but never more
than 10 days. On the last day early in the morning the dead body is placed
in the coffin brought down from the shelf and the dead body is taken to the
place of cremation and cremated. When the dead body has been well burnt
down to ashes, the bone-ash is collected and placed in a piece of blanket and

33. It is to be noted that the members of the house where death has
visited do not eat salt the next day after the cremation of the dead body.
On the second day they eat salt, but no vegetable, fish and meat. They
must not travel. On the 11th day after the cremation they may eat vege-
table, fish and meat but they may not still go out of their village. On the
12th day they may go anywhere they like.
34. Mro. When a person dies, a bamboo-coffin is made immediately.
A fowl is killed and boiled. Rice is also boiled. The boiled rice and fowl
are put in a plate which is then placed near the dead man with the saying
"eat". A pig is killed and those who made the coffin touch the blood with their
hands. The idea is to ward off ill-luck which might come on to them as
the result of making the coffin. The pork is then cooked and eaten by those
present. The corpse is usually kept for one day. Rich or well-to-do per-
sons, however, sometimes keep the corpse in the house for 8 or 9 days dur-
ing which feasting and drinking "khaung" are done. Early in the morning
one day the corpse is carried to the place of cremation. While the corpse
is being slowly burnt, a small house for keeping the bone-ash is constructed.
When the dead body has been burnt down to ashes, the bone-ash is collected,
placed on the blanket which has been used to cover the corpse and put on
the "bone-house". The plate containing the rice and curry, the water-pot,
the pots for cooking rice and curry are all brought and kept in the bone-
house. The people then come away. On arrival in the village, a young

VII. OCCUPATIONS
37. The chief occupation of the people [Kami (Ayaing), Mro, and Kami
(Awa)] is taungya cultivation. A few of the Kamis (Awa) who live on the
southern part of the area under consideration do undertake paddy cultiva-
tion. In the upper reaches of Yochaung and Tawphya, i.e., in Paung-Ra-
Hat village tract and To-Re village tract, tobacco cultivation is carried on.
No cotton is cultivated by the people living in Yochaung side. But the
villagers of Thalugyun, Wa-Bya, Naung Chaung and To Re within the
village-tract cultivate cotton. Tawphya possesses much more bamboo than
Yochaung. The people living in Tawphya side also get a little income by
cutting and selling bamboo.
VIII. RELIGION, RELIGIOUS RITES AND SACRIFICES
38. The people are animists. If a person falls sick, either a fowl or goat
or pig or cow is killed according to the indication of "We-Pa-Le ", in order
to propitiate a certain nat. The general rule of sacrifice is as follows:
A fowl or goat must be sacrificed to the nat of the stream; a dog to the
nat of the road-way and a pig to the nat of the house. The greater part
of the sacrifices are done in connection with the taungyas.
39. Kami (Ayaing). (1) When the Taungya cutting time has come
a pig is bought with the collective money of the village and killed in the
middle of the village. At the time of killing the pig, the man who is about
to carry out the work bites a piece of ginger and says "phyauk! We are
killing a pig for you. We would cut Taungya this year on that side. May
we get good crop". The pork is boiled with water and eaten by the male
persons of the village.
(2) The Taungya is cut and the wood burnt. Each household would
go to the Taungya and bring a small piece of earth. A pig would be killed
in the village and the earth besmeared with the blood with the saying "We
give you this blood. May our harvest be a good one".
(3) The seeds are sown in the Taungya. The "Taungya-dancing"
ceremony may or may not be done. If there be sickness at home it is
usually done. A small platform is made of bamboo at the Taungya. Apig
or goat or cow is taken there and to a post. The people drink khaung
and dance at the Taungya, with drums, Hai-ia, gong, and cymbals. This is
done in the day time. The pig or goat or cow is then brought back to the
village. In the case of a pig or goat the killing is carried out that evening.
In the case of a cow the dancing may be carried on till the next morning.
The animal is killed and cooked with all the ingredients and the people
partake of the curry. No Ngapi must be put in the curry. Vegetable
seeds are sown almost about the same time as paddy seeds.

IX. MUSICAL INSTRUMENTS
42. The chief musical instruments of the people are a drum, gong,
cymbal, Han Ra and flutes. The drum is made of a hollow wood which
has both faces covered with hide. The hide is kept tight by a continuous
leather throng passing up and down and stretching both ways. The “Han-
Ra" is really a copper plate which is used by the natives of India.

45. Dancing. It is unfortunate that at the time I went up, the people

the dancing of these people. I did however speak to the people that I

are superstitious and told me that it is only when a cow is killed that they
could dare to dance and rejoice; otherwise the nats might get angry and kill
them. A cow must die for my momentary pleasure and I have refrained
from having a dancing of the people.

XI. MISCELLANEOUS
47. The following remarks hold good for all [Kami (Ayaing), Mros,
and Kami (Awas) ].
48. Inheritance. Women have no right of inheritance. When a man
dies, all the properties left are shared between the sons, or if there be no
son by the male persons who are related to the dead man. If there be grown
up sons, the woman stays with them. If there be no sons, she goes back to
her own parents or if they be dead, to the relatives of her father. If she
has a very young son she has to leave him with her husband's relatives and
go away herself unless the relatives, through love and pity, allow her to
remain.
49. The wealth of the people consists of gongs and "Han-Ra"
which become valuable according to the length of time they are in the hands
of a man's ancestor.
50. The people are very fond of drinking and feasting. I think they
cannot help being so. Life must have been very dull to them who are
engaged almost all the year round in work. The fear of the anger of the
spirits perhaps keeps their mind a little lively. Heads of animals killed by
them are kept in the hall of their house and they take pride in the number
of the animal's heads they possess.
51. The people are very hospitable and they consider it a disgrace
that a stranger who arrives in the village should get no food. They are
generally simple by nature. Theft happens once in a way and the thief is
generally one who has associated with the people in the lower part of the
township and has fallen into the evil habit of eating or smoking opium.
52. The woman is the hardest worker. She does more work than her
husband.
53. The great defect in the people is that they have no unity. They
generally live with their relatives in a group and not amongst the people
who are not related to them. (They probably could not live on good terms
with strangers.) This, I think, is due not so much to their ill-nature but to
their great poverty which usually robs a man of his charitable heart.
On the whole they are a lovable people.
