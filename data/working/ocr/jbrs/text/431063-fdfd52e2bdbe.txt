A CHIENGMAI IMAGE INSCRIBED IN BURMESE AND TAI
by
Kraisri Nimmanahaeminda, B. S. (Penn), M.B.A. (Harvard)
In the Monastery of Renowned Victory (Vat Jaya Brah Kierti), Chiengmai, there is a
large bronze Buddha image with two inscriptions on the base: one in Burmese at the front,
and a longer one in Tai (Chiengmai Dharma characters) at the rear. They both bear a date
equivalent to A.D. 1565, and they shed a rather poignant light on the history of the time.
Chiengmai had already been an ancient city when the image was cast. The name, of
course, means “the new fortified city". It was built as a capital in 1296 by King Mengrai,
who had established the Tai as the dominant power in Northern Siam.
The kingdom, with Chiengmai as the capital, was known as Lanna-Tai, “the million Tai
paddy fields". Her people were called Tas Yuan or Tai Yon, sometimes "Tai" is left out,
leaving only Yuan or Yon. The kingdom was ruled by King Mengrai and his descendants
for over 250 years. Under Tilokaraja, and in contemporary with Parama Trailokanartha of
Ayudhya and Dharmacetiya of Hansavati, in the second half of the 15th century, Lanna-Tai
entered its Golden Age of art and letters. The King gave lavishly to Buddhism, founding
many monasteries, encouraging Pali studies, and casting inumerable images of the Bundha.
It should also be noted that Tilokaraja sponsored a Buddhist council called thh Eighth
Buddhist Council (Athama Sangayana) at the Monastery of the Great Bodhi Tree (Mahabo-
dharam), now commonly known as the Monastery of the Seven Spires (Vat Ced Yod). The
Eighth Buddhist Council was held under the chairmanship of Dharmadina Mahathera, a
learned abbot of the Monastery of Palm Forest (Vat Pa Tala) in A. B. 2020 (A.D. 1477).
We do not know much about the art of bronze casting in Lanna-Tai before Tilokara-
ja's time, doubtless it was far overshadowed by the art of Sukhodaya which had reached its
Golden Age nearly one hundred years before Chiengmai. In the period of Tilokaraja, Sukho-
daya lost all of her past glories to become a part of the Kingdom of Ayudhya, leaving an
undefined border between Chiengmai and Ayudhya which was the cause of wars between the
two countries. In a campaign in that region Tilokaraja captured two of its principal cities,
and we may be sure that, in accordance with the custom of conquerers in those days, he
transported the most skilled craftsmen to his own capital. This practice was still carried out
in even a larger scale when King Bayinnaung conquered Chiengmai and, later, Ayudhya,
whose skilled artisans and handicraftsmen of all sorts, together with their families, were
taken away to Hansavati. At all events, two arts for which Sukhodaya and Svargaloka had
beeen famous - the casting of bronze images and the making of celadon wares- assumed a
sudden importance in Lanna-Tai during the reign of Tilokaraja. For the most part he
might let the craftsmen to pursue their own traditions. For example, the casting of Brah
Cau Dong Dibya, a great bronze image nearly 15 feet in height, as a memorial of his suc-
cessful campaign over Nan, a part of the falling Kingdam of Sukhodaya, the style of Sukho-
daya was faithfully observed. But now and then he gave the bronze-casters new and unac-
customed tasks, commanding them to make large-scale copies of Buddha images in or

There are good reasons to believe that Tilokaraja's mission brought back not only the
plans of the temple, but also a copy of the principal Buddha image in it. The copy was
presumably a stone relief, carved several centuries under the Pala or Sena dynasty of Bengal.
This copy, which has now disappeared, seems to have been one of the foreign models that
Tilokaraja caused his bronze-casters to copy; and in 1469 there appeared the first of a
series of bronze Buddhas of the Singha or “Lion Type" inscribed with dates.
The type
is precisely that of Pala-Sena India, with a lotus knob over the ushnisha, a short fold of
cloth over the left shoulder, and the legs crossed in the vajrasana position. But the style
of modeling is not Pala-Sena at all; it is exactly what we should expect from sculptors,
trained in Sukhodaya tradition, if they were commaned to copy a Pala-Sena stone relief in
Bronze.
The Kingdom of Lanna-Thai continued to prosper for a time after Tilokaraja's death.
Bronze Buddha images continued to be cast in enormous numbers, some of great beauty;
the art gradually settled back into a comfortable conservatism; and the Lion Type went out
of fashion.
In the reign of the 13th King of Mengrai Dynasty, a series of internal feuds and
military defeats commenced. This prince, who usurped the throne from his father in 1538,
was assassinated after a five years reign of terror. His father, the 12th King, was restored;
but after a short time he too was murdered, and with him the direct line became extinct.
A period of confusion followed; and finally the chief dignitaries of Chiengmai offered the
throne to a Shan prince, Mekuti, the ruler of Muang Nai, a direct descendant of Khun
Krua, one of the sons of King Mengrai, who was sent to rule over Muang Nai at the
request of the Shans. Threatened by enemies on every side, the new king of Chiengmai
was unable to assert a strong authority.
These events did not escape the notice of the Burmese king, Bayinnaung. Having
consolidated his hold on the Shan States, he swooped down on Lanna-Tai and captured
Chiengmai in 1556. Apparently he hoped to be greeted as a liberator, as he seems to have
made a sincere effort to gain the goodwill of the conquered people. Instead of deposing
Mekuti, he treated him with consideration, made him take a vassals oath of allegiance, and
maintained him as a puppet with Burmese advisors. Later on, however, Mekuti was accused
of plotting a revolt. He was seized and sent to Burma, where he died in caqitivity a few
years later; and then, curious to relate, he became "Yun Bayin Nat", one of the Thirty--
seven Spirit Lords of the Burmese pantheon.
The Burmese then turned over the regency of Lanna-Tai to a Chiengmai princess,
hoping she would prove more reliable. The lady, who is known to history simply as
"Mahadadevi", is mentioned in our inscription.

The inscription on the front of the base is in Burmese. The following is the transla-
(Colonel Ba Shin will kindly read the original Burmese Text):
On Wednesday, the 13th waxing of Prasiuw, the 11th day of the Tithi, at the
conjunction of the four nakshatras with the planet Anga, just after noon; in the centre
of the great kingdom called Kyuin Me, the Lord Jayya Saram Bana Ca Phan-a
minister of the Righteous King who is Lord of the White Elephant and of the Golden
Palace, who is Lord of Life and superior to all kings-desiring to create a refuge for
men and devas and brahmas as long as the 5000 years of the Buddhist religion shall last,
collected all Buddha images that were broken and cast a new image of the Omniscient
One.
The inscription on the back of the base, in Tai (Chiengmai Dharma characters, similar
to characters now used in Kengtung, Sipsongpanna, and Laos), is written in verse, much
longer and more detailed. Here is its translation:
May it be of good omen! In culasakkaraja 927, in the year of the ox, seventh of the
decade (chlu chnam) as the Cambodians say; in the year of the ox, second of the
decade (tap prao) as the Tai say; at the beginning of the month of Karttika, the moon
being in ; or, as the Tai say, on the 3rd day of the waxing moon of the second
month, Saturday; or as the Tai say, the day of the tiger, third of the decade (rvay yi),
at the beating of the evening drum; an auspicious day, a very good day! General Jaya
San Ram Ca Pan-the faithful servant of the Righteous King of Kings (mahadhammi-
karajadhiraja) who is Lord of the Whith Elephant and of the Golden Palace and
Sovereign over all princes and chiefs - having been appointed as defender of religion
and protector of the people in Nabapuri Sri Mahanagara Mon Bing Jyan Hmai (i.e.
Chieng Mai) being mindful of the excellences of the religion and desirous of promoting
it, and at the same time proposing to transfer the merit of his act to the Princess
Regent (Samtec brah mahadevi cau) who is the foundation-root of the city, joined
together with the princes and officers, both military and civil, and all citizens intent
on merit-making, and collected all the Buddha images that had been damaged

The General, I take it, was a Burmese; but the title, Ca Pan, is the old Chiengmai title
for one of the four chief ministers of the kingdom (translated literally, it means "chief of
the house"). His proposal to transfer the merit of collecting the old images to the Princess
Regent is a delicate thought, doubtless part of the Burmese effort to win the goodwill. But
he plans to keep the merit of casting the image for himself, and lists the rewards he
hopes for.
What were the intentions of the participating parties in calling the image the King
Mengrai Buddha? Did the conquerers intend it as a last recollection of vanished glory? At
any rate, they gave it the name of the founder of the dynasty that was now in collapse.
Very likely they would have chosen as their model some image that dated from his reign,
if they had known where to find one; but it was an age of piety more than of archeology,
and so the Lion Type, a model dating from the reign of that other great monarch of the
dynasty, Tilokaraja, would serve equally well.
