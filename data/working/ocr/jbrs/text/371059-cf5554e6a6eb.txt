OUTLINE OF A SOCIALIST ECONOMY
FOR
BURMA.'
by
U Thet Tun, B. A. (Rgn.) B.Sc. (London) 2
I have entitled my paper an Outline of a Socialist Economy for
Burma. By this is implied that the economy of Burma as of the
present is not a Socialist Economy. One may enquire then what kind
of an economy Burma has at present. I would carry the query a little
further back and ask what kind of an economy Burma had before
the War.
The economy of Burma before the War was primarily agricultural
and extractive. About 52 per cent of the value of all domestic
production was derived from agriculture; another 9 per cent from
forestry; another 7 per cent from mining; all of them inclusive of
processing and marketing. These three fields of economic activity
between them accounted for over two-thirds of the gross domestic
product of Burma. Some 22 per cent of the value of total output was
attributable to other industries and services. Public and public utility
activities (about 6 per cent) and rents (3 per cent) made up the balance.
I have enumerated the share contributed by each sector of the
economy to the total output of Burma. But we need to enquire also
how the income generated by this output is distributed. In agriculture
the producers were, of course, mostly Burmese cultivators and
labourers with perhaps some imported seasonal Indian labour in Lower
Burma. The share received by the agricultural producers was no doubt
very small. They did not even earn enough to keep going and in the
meantime were falling into debt with their creditors and landlords.
Prominent among lenders to the agriculturists in Lower Burma were
the Chettiars who came to own 20 per cent of the best paddy land in
Lower Burma. Peasant proprietorship lost so much ground that half
the land in Lower Burma fell into the hands of non-agriculturists.
In rice-milling industry, the Steel Brothers and some other
European firms owned the larger mills while the Indians and Chinese
owned a number of medium-sized mills, Country mills which operate
for the local market were often owned by the Burmese. Marketing of
rice within the country was done by some Burmese pwesas or brokers,
and rice-mill owners, Burmese, Indian and Chinese. External marketing
was done by European, Chinese and Indian firms, who grouped them-
selves into a Rice Merchants' Association.

Mr. J. S. Furnivall in his "Colonial Policy & Practice" classified
the exports of Burma under two main heads: Rural Produce due to
native enterprise, mainly in rice cultivation; and Capitalist Produce,
due to Western enterprise in the forests, oil-fields, mines and planta-
tions. He pointed out the rising rate of increase of capitalist
produce exports relative to the exports of rural produce after1900.
Exports of indigenous produce declined as a per cent of total
exports from 75.8 to 46.6, whilst exports of capitalist produce rose
from 21.3 to 52.6 between 1868-69 and 1936-37. He concluded from
this that one outstanding feature of the first half of this century was
the increasing part played by foreign capital in the production of
wealth in Burma.
Mr. Furnival classified likewise the imports of Burma into
consumption goods, mainly for the native market and capital goods,
mainly for production by Western enterprise. He traced the growth of
import of capital goods from 13.7 per cent of total imports in 1868/9
to 33.5 per cent in 1936/37 and the decline of consumption goods from
79.6 to 57.8 in the same period. Mr. Furnivall concludes that it is
only natural that with increasing wealth a larger proportion should be
devoted to capital goods, but in Burma, as in other tropical dependen-
cies, the capitalists are Europeans or, to some extent, foreign orientals
working on capitalist lines and the growth of capitalist imports.
indicates the increasing hold of foreign capital on economic activities.
As mentioned above, Indians, Chinese and Europeans owned all the

2065 crores of rupees; of this 30.4 per cent was owned by foreign

This is the picture of the Burmese economy before the War and
I hope you will agree with me if I call it a "colonial economy".
Now let us turn to the examination of the post-war economy of
Burma.
The economy of Burma suffered a serve blow from destruction by
the War. Almost half of the national capital (excluding land) was
destroyed. Gross National Product in real terms at the end of the
War (i. e. in 1946-47) was only 5 per cent of 1938-39. There was some
recovery in 1947-48 when the G. N. P. rose to 68 per cent a truly
substantial recovery for so short a period. But in the meantime the
insurrections had broken out and this trend of recovery was not only
halted but reversed. Once again a serious toll was taken of the
nation's productive equipment and current production was seriously
disrupted. By 1949-50 production declined to the 1946-47 level (about
59 per cent). To-day total output is estimated at a little over two-
thirds of the prewar level, and is certainly less than three quarters of
1938-39.
But in the meantime the population of Burma must have grown
by about 12 per cent. Consequently output per capita in present prices
has fallen from K. 329 in 1938-39 to K. 201 (in 1950-51). Consump-
tion per capita has also declined. Import statistics register a decline
in domestic consumption of fishery products and imported foodstuffs
and goods. In the villages the cultivators have to pay a larger share
of their smaller yield in wages to the agricultural labourers. In the
cities, poverty, over-crowding, under-employment and economic strin-
gency are apparent. So it will not be too rash to conclude that the
people of Burma are living, by large, much more poorly than they did
even before the War.

Burma. The two major factors responsible for this economic destruc-

Resilience of the economy which is no doubt partly attributable to its

Another factor which favoured the Burmese economy in the post-
war period, is the huge world demand for and high prices of Burma's
staple export - rice. Whereas rice exports before the War were 68 per
cent of the total, in the post-war years, although smaller in absolute
magnitude, they constituted 75 per cent more of total value. In
1949-50 when the insurrections were at their height, rice became almost
the sole export of the country constituting 88 per cent of the total.
But for rice, the economy of Burma would have totally broken down
and the country would have met the fate of insolvency.
But of the two factors I have just cited, one, the resilience of the
Burmese economy, is due to its inherent structural simplicity and the
other, the favourable world market for rice, is also a given datum.

The first sector of the economy to be nationalised after independ-
ence was the forests. It was originally intended to take over the forests
from the lessees in three instalments. The first take-over was
effected in June 1, 1948 and the second and third were due to follow in
1949-50. With the outbreak of the insurrection in mid 1948 when
forest operations were suspended, the Government was requested by the
jessees to take over the remaining areas immediately. The Government
thereupon completed the nationalization of the forests on January 31,
1949. Thereafter operations by four indigenous lessees have been
confined to Rangoon and Moulmein. The only large mills operating
now are owned by the Government. The State Timber Board now
extracts, processes and markets a very high per centage of timber in
Burma. The Forest Department continues to be responsible for the
conservation of the forests.
The mineral industry of Burma producing non-ferrous base metals
and petroleum was an important foreign exchange earner in prewar
days. As a result of the War damage and disturbed conditions of the
country, the prewar annual export of 6 crores worth of non-ferrous
metals has now fallen to 1.3 crores in spite of the tremendous rise in
metal prices. The Government has now entered into a Joint Venture
with the Burma Corporation Ltd. and owns 50 per cent of the shares.
The Government and the former Burma Corporation Ltd., are each
represented by three directors whilst management is still left with the
company. An intensive Burmanization programme especially technical
staff, and training of Burmese personnel will enable the Government to
completely nationalize the enterprise when financial circumstances
permit.
The oil industry which used to export after meeting all domestic
requirements 14 crores worth of petroleum and petroleum products
before the War suffered considerable damage as a result of the
scorched-earth policy of the British Government and to-day Burma is
importing petroleum to the extent of more than 3 crores. Rehabilita-
tion of the oil industry has been carried out on the limited basis of a
refinery at Chauk since restoration of the pipe-line from the oil-fields.
to Syriam, where the large refinery can be operated is not feasible in
the present state of law and order. Negotiations between the
Government and the oil companies for the formation of a Joint Venture
(Government 1/3 and Oil Companies 2/3) are now successfully
progressing.
Thus in all the three major fields of economic activity in Burma
the participation of the State has remarkably increased since the War.

Now I shall proceed to examine the various economic controls
exercised by the Government.
The post-war balance of payments position was threatened by the
low level of exports and the heavy demand for imports to satisfy the
pent up demand resulting from the war. There also began in 1947-48
a drain of private remittances abroad, personal and of business profits,
and a flight of capital. Thus trade controls were instituted and
exchange regulations tightened up to cut down unnecessary imports,
prevent flight of capital and maintain a favourable balance of
payments.
Exchange control is primarily a financial control to conserve and
allocate foreign exchange resources, control capital movements and
ensure that investment at home with imported goods flows into desirable
channels. This implies the gathering in of all the foreign exchange
resources and the control over the use of payments. Exchange control
inevitably implies an element of discrimination since it sets out to
restrict certain payments whilst conserving foreign exchange for
expenditures regarded as essential.
In Burma the foreign exchange authority is the Union Bank of
Burma, the General Manager of which is the ex-officio Controller of
Foreign Exchange. All parties, including the Government, are required.
to surrender their foreign exchange earnings to the Union Bank. Any
body requiring currency for any use has to apply to the same authority
for the purpose. Concurrence of the Finance Ministry is necessary
for dollar expenditure. Lately the foreign exchange position of Burma
has considerably improved and regulations have been relaxed.

Import and export trade control was instituted in Burma in the
later half of 1947 and was administered by the then Ministry of
Commerce and Supply in collaboration with the Collector of Customs,
Controller of Foreign Exchange and the Ministry of Finance and
Revenue. Import control is by far the most important element of a
Balance of Payments policy. It is exercised through a system of
import licensing; no one may normally import certain classes of goods
except under the authority of some sort of licence. Once an importer
has obtained a licence the currency allocation follows automatically.
The Government alloted a minimum of 60 per cent of the available
foreign exchange for issue of import licences to Burmese nationals
annually. Co-operative Societies and private and public companies
were also given preference over individuals. However, about 30 to
40 per cent of the import licences alloted to the Burmese individuals
and firms are believed to have been sold to larger firms, mostly foreign.
This is a conservative estimate. Burmese traders want the Government
to retain import trade control for a period of 5 years in order to protect
Burmese importers against better established European, Indian and
Chinese importers who hold about 90 per cent of proprietary rights or
sole agencies over articles of established reputation. In view, however,
of the improved foreign exchange situation and in order to bring down
the cost of living for the people, the Government had since the latter
half of 1950 progressively liberalized the import trade policy by issuing
Open General Licences for a wide range of commodities. Export
control covers rice and other important agricultural produce and also
goods which had previously been imported into Burma.
Some important articles, the prices of which are controlled by the
Government under the Essential Commodities Act are sugar, condensed
milk, gasoline, motor vehicle and tyres.
In the field of money and banking also important steps have been
taken to strengthen central control. Under the Central Bank Act 1948
the Union Bank of Burma was established and its position was further
strengthened by the Union Bank of Burma Act 1952. The latter Act
provided for the abolition of the Burma Currency Board composed
mostly of non-Burmans and sitting in London and for the transfer of
the power of issue of currency to the Union Bank in Rangoon. It also
altered the reserve requirements for currency issue from 100 per cent
sterling reserve for all Burmese currency beyond a ten crore fiduciary
issue to a 25 per cent foreign exchange reserve against all currency
and also against demand deposit liabilities of the Union Bank. The
Union Bank has also been empowered to establish reserve requirements

Governmental fiscal policy prior to 1951-52, formulated on the
pre-war pattern, was contractionary in its effect on the economy, and is
no longer suited to the development phase which Burma is now about
to enter and in which the Government intends to play a leading role.
An attempt has been made since 1951-52 to reverse this contractionary
fiscal policy by providing for a fairly large investment expenditure and
the way has been paved for an expansionary fiscal policy to finance the
economic expansion of the country.
Burma today has a tax system which is fairly progressive. Land
revenue which was the largest source of revenue has now dwindled
because of disturbed conditions in the country-side and a reduced tax
morale. Its leading position has now been taken by customs, followed
by income-tax, commercial and excise taxes and excise duties. The
tax system is in accord with the socialist economic creed of the
Government. As the development programme gets under way and as
money income increases, the tax system must be strengthened to mop
up a large share of such increase in income to finance the Government's
development programme and to prevent inflation.
Co-operative societies, under British administration, had been
primarily an instrument of extending agricultural credit. Since the
end of the war and especially after Independence, the number and
scope of work and importance of co-operatives (consumer, agricultural
credit, marketing and others) have expanded remarkably under the
active sponsorship of the Government through its office of the
Registrar of Co-operative Societies and the Ministry of Co-operative
and Commodity Distribution.

the Socialist Government. As a result several new Labour Acts as well
as amendments designed to safeguard the interests and secure the
welfare of labour in the country have been enacted and actively
en forced. With the official encouragement of a strong and healthy

What has been the result of all these nationalization, socialization,
centralization, controls, regulations and so on? Long-term results are
still too far-off to see. But there are certain immediate results which
we cannot ignore. Firstly it has saved the country from economic
ruin and the Government from bankruptcy which would otherwise have
overtaken them amidst countrywide insurrections. Secondly, it has
prevented consumption from falling as much as output. Though per
capita output in 1950-51 was only 60 per cent of pre-war, per capita
consumption was 68 per cent of pre-war. This is in part a result of a
permanent change in the Burmese economic system. In 1938-39,
6 per cent of the output was withdrawn by foreign investors as
dividends or interest. Today, only a fraction of one per cent of
Burma's output goes abroad as profits of foreign-owned enterprises in
Burma. The remaining 99 per cent furnishes income to the people and
firms and Government of Burma.

attainment of independence into a mixed economy-a mixture, rather

This tendency is obviously not accidental but has been deliberately
fostered and encouraged by the Government. The Constitution of the
Union of Burma avowedly provided that the economic life of the
Union shall be planned with the aim of increasing the public wealth,
of improving the material conditions of the people and raising their
cultural level, of consolidating the independence of the Union and
strengthening its defensive capacity. It declares that the State, the
ultimate owner of all lands, has the right to regulate land tenure and
to exploit and develop all timber and mineral lands, forests, water,
fisheries, minerals, coal, petroleum and other mineral oils, all sources
of potential energy and the natural resources. In fact it announces
that Burma is to be transformed into a Socialist State.
Apart from the general advantage of a planned economy for an
under-developed country, there are reasons peculiar to Burma which
make the case for Socialism stronger here. In Western countries which
are now changing over from capitalism to socialism, there has sprung
up, in addition to the two opposing camps of laissez-faire and socialist
economists, a third school of advocates of the middle-way- those who
believe in the permanence and perhaps even the superiority over both
laissez-faire and planned economies, of the mixed economy. In
the United Kingdom for instance the Attlee-Morrison Wing of the
Socialist Party as opposed to the Bevanites now want to consolidate
the socialized sector of the economy and is not prepared to push socia-
lisation any further - at least not immediately. In my opinion there is
no such case for continuance of a mixed economy in Burma, Why?
Because whereas the mixed economy in U.K. is an uneasy alliance of
British public enterprise and British private capital, in Burma it is an
undersirable combination of Burmese State enterprise and foreign
private capital (European, Indian and Chinese). So the sooner the
mixed economy changes into a Socialist economy, the better it is for
Burma and the Burmese people. Moreover the absence of vested
Burmese economic interests and the existence of the precedent of state
monopoly under the Burmese Kings will make the change-over to
Socialism easier in this country than say in the United Kingdom.

The land policy of the Union Government has already been defined.
by the Constitution and by the Government and it only remains to
see to its execution. However, I have two comments to make. One
is that politics should be kept out of distribution of land as much as is

such a great scheme as this. Secondly, distribution of land to
cultivators should not let them run away with the idea that such land as
they have received will be tax-free. Gross Agricultural income
(of cultivators) consists of two parts which ought to be kept distinct.
One is the income derived from cultivating the land. The other is
the surplus accruing from the natural advantages of land for which the
cultivator can claim no credit. This surplus is unearned income and as
such and also for the sake of equity among cultivators the State is
entitled to charge a rent or whatever it may be called on such land.

With regard to the mining industry, there is no doubt that the

The industrial policy of the Government was made known in a
resolution of the Economic Council which was subsequently laid on the
tables of Parliament on 28th September 1949. Production of atomic
energy and manufacture of arms and ammunitions were reserved for
development as State enterprises and/or monopolies. But until such
time as the State can undertake sufficient production private enterprise
shall be permitted to develop them either on their own or in
partnership with the State, on terms and conditions to be settled with
each enterprise. Industries not included in the preceding two categories
were to be open to development by private enterprise. The resolution
also indicated the terms and conditions on which foreign capital may be
accepted for the rehabilitation or development of industries in Burma.
The resolution is in my opinion basically sound but it must be
borne in mind that it was passed at a time when as a result of the
insurrections there prevailed in the country a very low state of
productivity and when the pace of rehabilitation in the major industries
was extremely slow. Since then the economic situation of the country
and the financial position of the Government has improved and it has
decided to carry out a development programme largely on its own.
This I think is as it should be.

bussiness, small or big, industrial or commercial. It is equally well-

Burmese businessmen have aimed at reaping whirlwind profits out of

therefore her trade to Western countries in the Meiji era, Japanese

able to compete favourably with better-established foreign business.
Industries were set up by the State or with State aid and were later
handed over to private enterprise with the result that Japanese private
capital grew by leaps and bounds and before long the Zaibatsu had
taken control of the organism of State itself. Strong economic
interests, even if they are indigenous, should not be allowed to take
root because vested economic interest can always so to speak 'bribe'
political power even if they cannot actually usurp it.
When the development programme gets underway shortage of
foreign exchange may necessitate control of import of consumption
goods and may even justify the handling of all or a substantial portion
of imports by the Government. The case for state trading in
imports is stronger in Burma where the import business is dominated
by foreign firms which possess contact with countries of manufacture
that the Burmese firms lack. I therefore advocate re-expansion
of the Civil Supplies Board and more efficient distribution of its
commodities through the co-operatives. Another point is that the
obnoxious ways of the noveau riche may jeopardize the otherwise
completely socialistic character of Government policy.

They have to be executed and this is where the machinery for execution

If the State is to embark upon a wide range of economic activities, if

economy of Burma which I called a colonial economy. I then explained

I put forward the case for further advance towards
Socialism and outlined the future Socialist economy of Burma,
suggesting some measures for its achievement. Now I shall conclude
by uttering a note of warning about the danger of a Socialist system of
economy and government. It is not inefficiency because socialistic
methods of production are less wasteful and because socialist
competition may well be as effective as capitalistic competition. The
real danger lies in the loss of political liberty and freedom and the
consequent emergence of totalitarianism. The substitution of Socialism
for Capitalism may well mean the substitution of the "cult of power
for the "cult of money". We may be able to afford a bad government
under the laissez faire system because it interferes so little in our
individual life. But a government under the socialist system will
necessarily be a powerful government interfering in almost every aspect
of our everyday life. I do not say that the power of the State is a bad
thing in itself. On the contrary, a strong and powerful government as
the embodiment of the state power is necessary to carry out a socialist
economic policy. But we must be able to change a government with
ease if and when we find it bad. The stronger the government, the
easier it need be to change it at the will of the electorate. That is the
reason why a socialist economy must be backed by a political
democracy. They must go hand in hand for either of them alone
is undesirable if not pernicious.
