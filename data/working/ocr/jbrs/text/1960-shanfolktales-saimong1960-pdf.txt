Once upon a time the Owl and the Crow were great friends. In those days both the
birds were white in colour. They thought they would become beautiful if they had them-
selves tatooed, 1 and they agreed to tatoo each other. The Owl was the first to be tatooed
by the Crow, and as he had patience and courage to bear the pain from the tatooing needle,
the result was a beautiful design on his body. The Crow, restless and fidgety by nature,

attack him until he is driven away. All his tribe behave in the same way toward an owl.
Phya Sengtaw
During the reign of Phya Singh, son of Mangrai, in Mong Ngünyang, 2 there were
three village boys who found a wasp hive on a tree outside their village. Wishing to eat
the young wasps they agreed to bring down the hive with the vow that anyone who climbed
the tree but could not bring down the hive would be killed. Two boys went up the tree
one after another, but they were bitten by the wasps so badly that they had to come down
without the hive. The third boy went up, and although he was bitten by the wasps he
persisted, so that in the end he brought the hive down and in it found a wasp stone. When
he came down from the tree, he promptly killed his two friends for their failure. When
the parents of the two dead boys reported the matter to the king, the king said: "This boy
who killed the other two is no ordinary boy and when he killed his two friends he did but
carry out the vow made between them. The two dead boys would be of no value to any
one even as slaves. I shall adopt this boy as my son and he shall help me to fight my
enemies". The boy was then adopted by Phya Singh and renamed Prince Sengtaw
(wasp stone).
When the news of the prosperity of the kingdom of Phya Singh, Ngünyang, reached
China, the Emperor sent envoys to demand tribute, which was refused. The Emperor then
sent an army to exact tribute. Sengtaw led Phya Singh's army to fight the Chinese and
beat them off. Telling his adopted father that unless the Chinese were beaten on their
own soil they would never give his kingdom any peace, Sengtaw led his men in hot pursuit
of the Chinese and defeated them in their own country. When the Emperor heard about

Buffalo & Man
At the beginning of the world, the king of the gods ordered buffalo to come to Earth
and tell Man that he must eat one meal every three days. The buffalo thereupon made his
way from the kingdom of the gods to Earth. On reaching Earth, the buffalo came across a
clump of banana trees whose cool shade found irresistable in the heat of the day. The buffalo
lay down to rest under the banana trees, and fall asleep. On waking up he continued his
journey to give Man the Message of the king of the gods. Finally he came to Man and said:
“O Man, my lord and master, king of the gods, creator of Universe, has commanded thee
to eat three meals a day."
Having delivered his message, the buffalo returned to his lord and master in the king-
dom of the gods. On seeing his messenger, the king of the gods asked: "O buffalo, what did
you go and tell Man?”
"O king of the gods, lord of my life and creator of universe," replied the buffalo with
proper respect, “I have told Man that my lord has commanded him to eat three meals a day."
Oh hearing the animal's report, the king of the gods, waxing wroth, said, "Stupid
buffalo! I told you to tell Man to eat but one meal in three days; and now you have gone
and told him to eat three meals a day! How can Man ever work enough to support him-
self and his family with three meals day! Go, at this instant, down to Earth, stay there
eternally with Man and help him to plough his rice field."
With a sad face the buffalo came down to Earth. On his way he saw the banana clump
under whose cool shade he had slept on his last mission. He became very angry at the sight
of it, and said to himself: "Because I slept under the shade of these banana trees, I forgot
my master's proper command, and told Man to eat three times a day, instead of once in
three days. Because of these banana trees I have now become Man's beast of burden.” So
saying he began to attack the banana clump, with all his might and fury until every banana

The Cuckoo
(Co-existence between daughter-in-law and mother-in-law.)
Long, long ago there lived in the Shanland a husband called Ai Sang, and a wife called
Nang Ee.
With the couple also lived the husband's blind mother. Nang Ee looked after
the household and Ai Sang looked after the field. The wife would fetch water, look for food
and cook the meals, or feed the pig and the chicken. Being blind the mother-in-law could
not do anything to help in the domestic work. The husband would do the heavier work,
such as cutting and fetching the firewood, looking after the bullocks and the cart, working
in their small rice field, or repairing their bamboo house and its bamboo fencing.
Life for the three people went on smoothly for a time, when the wife began to get tired
of looking after the blind mother-in-law. Nang Ee felt that on account of her mother-in-
law she was tied to her house all the time. If there were pwes or festivals in the village or
in the monastery, she could not enjoy herself as other women did because her mother-in-
law kept coming to her mind. She tried hard to think of her mother-in-law as her own
mother because she loved her husband, but in vain. She knew that it was the duty of a
daughter-in-law to love and look after the husband's parents as if they were her own; but in
her case, she could not do it.
One day while the husband was away at his work for the day, the sight of the blind
lady became so hateful to Nang Ee that during the midday meal, she wrapped a small
amount of cowdung in some banana leaves, and saying that it was a baked fish pack, gave
it to her mother-in-law, together with the usual portion of rice. Nang Ee then went out
of the house to rid herself of the sight of her mother-in-law who got on her nerves so
much.
At sundown, Ai Sang returned from his work to his bamboo house. When he saw his
blind mother he greeted her as was his wont and asked her how she had fared in his absence
during the day; and she replied to his greetings with appropriate words. When the son asked
if she ate well, the mother said: "Not so well today, my son." On being asked what was
wrong, she said: "My daughter gave me a pack of baked fish; but it seems that the meat.
was high so I could not eat it but leave it near the wall there.”

Nang Upem & Khun Samlaw
Khun Samlaw was a handsome young man and a model son who lived with his weal-
thy parents in Kengtawng. 4 Being heir to a rich estate and an eligible young man, many
girls of Kengtawng tried to capture his heart, but he did not pay any attention to their
advances. Among his admirers was one by the name of Upaem 5 who was neither young
nor beautiful but she was a hard working girl and knew all about house work. When
Upaem failed to win over Samlaw by her own efforts, she worked through his mother by
trying to please her in every way. The mother wanted Samlaw to marry Upaem as she
thought she would make a good wife and an excellent daughter-in-law. Upaem's attention
and his mother's persistent entreaties made life quite miserable for Samlaw. He told his
mother he was too young and too inexperienced to marry; but his mother told him he
was old enough to be a father. Finally he told his parents that he would marry any one
they chose for him if he was first allowed to go away to trade and to widen his knowledge
and experience. His parents gladly provided him with 500 pack bullocks complete with
merchandise and camp followers.

followers travelled all over the Shan States. Towards the end of his wandering, he came

home to see his parents for a while-he had been away from them for quite a number of

Kengtawng his parents, though glad to see him, were angry with him for having married
without their knowledge or consent, and forsaken Upaem. The parents forbade him to
leave their house again.

on the ground. As people began to pick up the coins he made his way to the house,
where his route was again barred by a hostile crowd. Samlaw again scattered his coins
and finally reached the middle of the house where his wife's coffin was. He opened the
coffin lid, lay down along side Upem and with his dagger stabbed himself to death.
Samlaw's family was informed of the tragedy and when his mother arrived for the
funeral she put Samlaw's body into another coffin, and between this and Upem's coffin she
placed a three-notch bamboo carrier pole with the curse that the husband and wife should
never meet again.
Upem and Samlaw met as spirits in the world of shadows and said to each other: "If
we were reborn in water or on land, we could never escape persecution: let us be reborn as
beautiful stars in the firmament for all the world to see.”
In the sky the two eternal lovers have become stars in the constellation of Orion-
Betelgeux is Khun Samlaw and its colour is reddish because he died the bloody death of
stabbing, Rigel with its white brilliance is Nang Upem, and between them, what the Western
astronomy calls Orion's belt, is the three-notch bamboo carrier pole placed by the malicious
mother. Upem's chicken and dog also joined their mistress-the former became the
Pleiades and the latter the evening star Venus.
In Mongkung and Kengtawng today people still point to places where incidents in the
story took place.
