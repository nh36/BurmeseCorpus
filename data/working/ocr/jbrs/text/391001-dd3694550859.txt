TRANSPORTATION IN BURMA DURING THE
JAPANESE OCCUPATION'
by
Josef Silverstein B.A. (Hons.) 2
In trying to assess the impact of the Second World War on the
economic, social and political institutions of Burma, one is handicapped
by a minimum of reliable data, an absence of trustworthy statistics and
very few accurate first hand accounts by the persons who played a
leading role during the period. Nevertheless, one can find encourage-
ment in the fact that of the limited material available, some of the best
relates to key facets of the Burmese economy such as transportation.
It is possible, by collecting and assembling all the existent facts relating
to transportation, to reconstruct the broad outlines of a picture of
Burma as a whole in the time of stress. One can learn not only a good
deal about the economics of Burma at the time, but also some things
about its politics and as a result one can begin to understand and
evaluate the real impact of war on Burmese life. Transportation is no
only the life blood of the economy and the sinews that bind the
extremities of Burma with the heartland, but, in the time of war and
enemy occupation, it becomes the target of attack for the military
combatants and the object to control by the economic and political
forces that struggle to determine its use. Therefore, the purpose of
this paper is to bring together all the relevant data available about
transport during the war years in order to evaluate the impact of war
on Burma..
I propose, for the purposes of this paper, to raise two basic ques-
tions as a frame of reference for the consideration of the existing
information. First, what was the Japanese policy for the use of the
ransport network; how was it implemented and what were the effects

The chief objectives of the Japanese in Burma were to protect the
safety of their armies and to utilize all the facilities and material avail-
able in the occupied country. To do this the occupiers organized the
transport and engineering facilities of Burma, built roads, relaid track,
built ships and rivercraft. They repaired transport and aircraft,
manufactured a few light engines, and finally, made some arms and
ammunition. They distilled rubber, tung, kanyin and resin to get
substitute gasoline for civilians.³ Their policy, therefore, was to
refashion the economy of Burma to meet their military needs primarily
and to allow the Burmese people to use only the excess goods and
services.

In addition the Japanese at Bhamo captured the Assam, one of the
largest rivercraft formerly used in Burma, during the evacuation of the
British and were able to utilize it for a few months on mail steamer
service between Mandalay and Rangoon. 7 At first, in the absence of
air attacks from the Allies, the Japanese made craft available to civilians
as well as to the military for personnel and cargo transport. However,
with the changes of fortune in the war, this policy was soon altered.
From the outset, the Japanese divided the river transport between
two services, one controlled by the military and used exclusively for its
own purposes and one given over first to the Senda Company of Japan
and later to the Yamashita Kisen Kaisha, which controlled not only
river traffic, but sea transport as well. Under this arbitrary division of
service, the civilian transport was always available for military use and
there are many reports that civilian traffic could never really depend on
the service allotted to them always to be available.
The Yamashita Kisen Kaisha was empowered by the Japanese
to commandeer private motor launches and other small craft and was
permitted to make regular trips between Prome and Pakkoku. The
commandeered craft never were able to perform with efficiency because
their engines were worn and no new spare parts existed for repairs.
The Japanese depended upon locally reconditioned and made-over parts
to keep them running. The smaller boats were left in private hands,
but were subject to summary requisition by the Japanese overlords
whenever the latter demanded. The occupiers imported junks of
lengths up to 60 feet, capable of carrying 50 tons and powered by good
engines to use in the Delta and other deep water sections of the river.

The Japanese used wooden and log rafts to float cargo down stream
whenever possible and employed Burmese-owned country boats when-

In addition to salvaging and utilizing the captured and reclaimed
vessels, the Japanese put the shipyards and repair facilities back into
operation as quickly as possible. Wood, being plentiful, was the chief
material used in new construction. The occupiers converted former
sawmills and timber yards into shipyards. Not only did they double
the shipbuilding output in the Rangoon area, but they also created and
maintained shipyards at Mergui, Moulmein, Bassein and Mandalay in
the south central part of Burma and at Taungup and Akyab on the
west coast. The Japanese planned to build five types of vessels, but
only the smallest from 90 to 110 feet and a few of a 150 feet class were
constructed.
When the Allied forces intensified their bombing campaigns in
1944-5, the Japanese began construction of flat-bottomed boats in four

The railways of Burma suffered a double blow during the war.
Initially, the Japanese bombed them as part of their invasion tactics.
The British in turn, following a scorched earth policy in conjunction with
their withdrawal from Burma, destroyed or rendered useless, all the
locomotives that were at their disposal. Japanese bombing in the first
instance and British demolition in the second, accounted for the fact
that only about 126 of the 350 pre-war locomotives were fit for use
after the British evacuated. However, in spite of this concerted effort
by both sides to disrupt railway transport, British Intelligence reported
in 1943 that," the Japs appear to have been successful in maintaining
a railway service despite demolition at the time of evacuation and
subsequent bombing raids. So far as is known all lines are
running."14

Later, when the Allies had gathered their strength and were in a
position to deal a serious blow to the Japanese, they made the rail-
roads of Burma one of their chief targets. Not only did the railroads
prove to be one of the most vulnerable targets, but they proved to be
one of the most difficult to repair due to Japan's reliance upon imported
replacement parts. The Allies concentrated upon knocking out the
locomotives and were successful in destroying or rendering useless the
largest part of them for all practical purposes.
Not only was the rolling stock hit hard, but key bridges such as
the Ava and the Sittang River Bridge, were destroyed early in the war.
The Japanese made a serious effort to restore the bridges and to bring
in new rolling stock so that they could supply their forces and keep
contact with all outlying areas in Burma. By 1944, the Japanese had
made both a new bridge across the Sittang River and restored the old
However, both efforts were promptly bombed out of existence
by the Allies.15

A review of the record reveals therefore, that as with water trans-
port, the Japanese relied mainly on materials, man-power and equipment
found within Burma to alter and repair their revised railway system.
Although the Japanese brought in a few locomotives, the quantity was
never enough to meet transport demands. As a result the occupiers
were forced to convert autos into hauling power for the railway. The
railway was never widely used by civilians after the war began because
the Japanese overburdened it with war material and personnel. 21
Although they were successful in linking Burma with her neighbours, the
cost in human lives expended in building the road was so great that the
road still bears the epitaph of Railway of Death.

The Civil Transport Administration seems to have been an outright
failure in maintaining municipal transport. Intelligence reports to the
Allies stated that by April, 1942, the Rangoon Municipal Administra-
tion had managed to put four or five R. E. T. buses back on the road.
The service was gradually increased, but it never exceeded twenty
vehicles in service in the city at one time. In May, 1943, all buses had
to be temporarily withdrawn due to their overworked condition and bus
service was suspended. Before it was re-established, the government
was forced to warn the public not to overcrowd or overload the buses
nor to travel without paying. By July, 1943, gas and lubricants were
so short for civilian transport that the Transport Officer advertised in
the papers offering to purchase used motor oil for passenger buses.
As for trolley service, the Japanese were able to supply Rangoon with
enough extra current so that on June 18, 1943, 4 or 5 trolley buses
were re-employed on the route between Ahlone and Piccadilly Circus
(Kyaikkasan Race Course). The trams, however, were never
re-employed at any time. 27
By 1944, the inflation that was effecting every other facet of
civilian economic life became apparent in civilian transportation. The
fare on the buses running occasionally between Rangoon and Lepadan
stood at Rupees 50 as against Rupee 1 in the prewar years. 23 The
fares on the infrequent buses travelling between Rangoon and Insein
was Rupees 3 whereas it was Annas 3 in the pre-war period. 29

No discussion of roads and road building is complete without
mentioning the Ledo Road built by the Allies in 1943-45. It linked
Ledo in India with Myitkyina in Burma, 263 miles away. The road
crossed the Patkai Hills in Northwest Burma and passed down through:
the disease ridden Hukawng Valley to its destination. The builders
intended to link the road at Myitkyina with Bhamo, 100 miles
away. The road was built at a terrific cost in lives and material.
Its value lay not only in its immediate military worth but also in the
fact that for the first time Burma and India were joined by a modern
link capable of accommodating heavy vehicles. At War's end, the road
offered an avenue into an area that formerly had been sparsely popu-
lated and provided a means of movement into the area on a large
scale.
The broad outlines of the transportation picture suggest that
Japan's policy of living off the land, while productive in a sense and in
fact capable of keeping the services operating to back up the Japanese
army in the field, actually depleted and stripped Burma. Regardless
of how many times the Japanese repaired the roads, rebuilt the bridges,
constructed new vessels, their stock of material thinned and the
Burmese transport facilities were destroyed further.

One cannot say that the inflation of war-ridden Burma was caused
solely by the breakdown in the internal transport system but one can
say that the breakdown had a contributory effect upon the economy of
Burma and, in places and under the conditions alluded to above, trans-
portation affected very greatly the normal life of the Burmese and their
alien overlords.

On August 1, 1943, the Japanese approved the establishment of
the Independent Government of Burnia. The Head of State or

Government's plan for the development of Burma under new conditions
that existed in Asia. Burma's New Order Plan, as Ba Maw called it,
attempted to define a philosophy for the Burmese people that would
be in line with the totalitarian and authoritarian regimes in both

laying a future claim to title and control over all transportation in
Burma, since the railway was already Government property. Thus he
established a furthur precedent of State controlled transportation.

The Adipadi made no mention of Japan's contribution to inter-
national transportation, the "Railway of Death" and the new high-
ways. His concern therefore, seemed to be to get for Burma, from
the other countries of Southeast Asia, vitally needed transportation
equipment. Japan, for all her platitudes of linking the Asian States
into an area of Co-Prosperity, was depleting Burma and replacing little
or nothing that she used. That Ba Maw should speak out so forth-
rightly, indicates that conditions on both the military and civilian
fronts were worsening for the Occupiers to such a point that the
Adipadi felt free to assert the independence that the situation gave to
him. Thus, the picture that we have of Burma in the closing months
of the war is one of general deterioration of the remaining transporta-
tion equipment due to the successful invasion of the Allies and a bolder
and more adamant Burmese attitude in dealing with the Japanese.
Although the Burmese were not successful in saving or gaining control
of their transport network, they were able to state their case more
forthrightly as time passed, and in the end they were able to harass
the Japanese whose status as overlords was crumbling before their
eyes.
In conclusion, therefore, one can sum-up the effect of the period
of war and occupation in Burma on transportation as follows; 1. That
while Japan was following her war policy of being supported by the
country under her occupation, she used every available item that the
country possessed. Her primary objective was to support her army,
but secondly, to gear the economy of the occupied country to Japan's

of transportation in the economy of Burma because its breakdown and
control by an alien force clearly indicated how vital its service was to a
prosperous and productive Burma. The war-time destruction of the
transport network proved to be one of the most difficult sectors of the
economy for the Burmese to rebuild.
