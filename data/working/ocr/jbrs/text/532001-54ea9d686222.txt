Dinnyawadi and Vesali, in the Kaladan

west, receiving people and cultural influ-
ences from the west first laid the foundations
of Arakanese civilization. The Lemro
cities, Sambawak, Parein, Launggret repre-
sent another element coming into the
region from Burma proper, bringing people.
and cultural influences from that side.
The cities, hemmed in by the narrow
valley, represent the dynasties at their
weakest. Mrauk-u facing both east and
west from its site in the gap of the main
ridge, not only received cultural influences
from both sides, but at times of stability
when the neighbours were torn by strife,
was able to extend its power into these
far regions. If Arakan fell later, it was
not merely because of internal strife but
also for the fact that stronger and more
aggressive kings reigned in Burma, and in
Bengal. After the coming of the British,
Mrauk-u found herself high and dry, away
from the main sea lanes. Akyab was
therefore chosen as the capital of the
province of Arakan and Mrauk-u declined.
Times have changed. The British held
Burma by means of sea-power and Mrauk-
u, a land-locked city lost her importance
completely.
Now part of Burma, Arakan which
had often looked east and west, is now
reunited with her eastern neighbour. The
difficulties of access by land are still ob-
vious to-day, much of the movement is still
by sea, supplemented by air. It is hoped
that with the building of roads, first the
Taunggup route, and later the An-route,
Burma could bridge these defects. Still,
in this modern age of air and sea travel,
Akyab with its harbour and air field, with
its far better site would remain the capital
of the province. Mrauk-u's place could
only be that of a cultural capital, just like
Mandalay is to-day the cultural capital
of Burma Proper.
