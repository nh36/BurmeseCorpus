Any chemical element can be a trace element. To qualify, it need
only be almost, but not quite, absent. The major elements of one
material can be the trace elements in another. Those elements occur-
ring in "traces in common materials have received an uncommon
amount of attention in recent years by chemists, physicists, and biolo-
gists, all of whom have recognized that traces of chemical substances
can exert effects out of all proportion to the amount present.
Trace elements have attracted attention in a number of different
fields. Astonishingly slight amounts of certain metals have been found
to play vital roles in biological phenomena. In industry traces of
impurities must often be eliminated before a successful operation is
possible. In other cases, the right amount of some impurity is the key
to success. The purpose of the present discussion is to present to
Burmese scientific people some thoughts of one who has been engaged
in trace element research for a number of years, with a view to indicat-
ing some ways in which this area of investigation may find application
in Burma.
Before proceeding any further, however, it may be well to clear up
a possibly dubious point. We shall not be discussing tracer elements.
The latter term has been used in recent years to denote an element
added in minute quantities for the purpose of tracing the fate of a
given element in a chemical or biological system. Usually the added
tracer element is one of the rarer isotopes of the element whose fate is
being studied. Being an isotope, it is assumed to have virtually the
same behavior as the more common isotopes of the same element.
Thus, the fate of cobalt ingested by farm animals has been studied by
feeding vanishingly small amounts of a radioactive isotope of cobalt
and using a Geiger counter to detect the radioactivity in various inter-
nal organs, as well as in the excreta. It is assumed that the radioactive
atoms of cobalt will undergo a similar fate to the main amount of
cobalt that would normally be ingested. The case illustrates both
trace elements and tracer elements. Minute quantities of cobalt are

Biological Relationships
Perhaps the best known examples of the necessity of traces of
certain chemical elements occur in the biological realm. Until the
importance of these traces was realized, it was customary to state that
ten, and only ten, elements are essential for plant nutrition. The
" classical" ten essential elements of plant nutrition were carbon,
oxygen, hydrogen, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulphur, calcium, magnesium,
iron, and potassium. Research
Research during the present century,
especially during the past 25 years,
has shown that many
species of plant are unable to survive unless supplied with
traces of manganese, copper, zinc, and boron. More recently,
molybdenum has been shown to be essential for a limited
number of species. The delay in discovering the importance of the
trace elements can perhaps be attributed to the slowness of scientists in
guessing that such small amounts of chemical elements could be
biologically effective. With attention being concentrated on the "major"
elements during the earlier phases of developing the science of plant
nutrition, it was only natural to ignore traces of heavy metals, or at
least to defer consideration of them until more promising areas of
research had been explored. The most orderly and rapid development
of any science is likely to occur when investigators are selecting for
study those questions most amenable to approach at any given point in
time, provided, of course, that these problems are among those that
will contribute to filling out the framework of facts and theories that
will ultimately comprise the final form of the science. Hence
it could not be expected that early workers would gamble on the
chance of making significant contributions by investing time and
research facilities on the trace elements when more promising investi-
gations were staring them in the face, as it were. Moreover, the

efforts had been directed toward eliminating trace impurities in order to
supply a purer fertilizer salt which presumably would be more suitable.
The failure to achieve complete success in the latter operation allowed
for a residual trace of boron to remain in the nitrate. To this trace of
boron was later attributed superior results obtained in fertilizing certain
boron-requiring crops, as compared with results obtained with synthetic
nitrogen sources.
The small amounts of trace elements needed can be illustrated by
an example or two. The total ash constituents of vegetable matter
may comprise in the neighbourhood of five per cent of the dried plant
matter, more or less. These ash constituents account for much of the
major plant nutrient elements. Copper, one of the essential trace
metals, however, may occur in the dried plant matter at concentrations
below 0.001 per cent and yet be present in adequate supply to carry out
its necessary physiological functions. In Australia, less than one pound
of molybdenum per acre was sufficient to provide an adequate suply
of this essential metal in experiments with pastures. Though cobalt is
not known to be an essential factor in plant nutrition, it is required in
the nutrition of certain farm animals. Hence a satisfactory forage
grass must contain an adequate supply of cobalt, or else this element
must be fed in the form of a mineral supplement, along with such
minerals as salt, iron and copper salts, and bone meal. But the
requirement for cobalt is so slight that sheep receive sufficient if the
pasture grasses contain as little as 0.07 part per million of the dried

By the time that it had been realized that plants and soils contain
ever-present traces of heavy metals and other presumably non-essential
elements, the question naturally arose as to whether these traces had
any biological signficance, or whether they were merely innocent
bystanders, neither helping nor hindering normal, physiological
processes. The fact that certain poisons can act in exceedingly small
concentrations must have suggested that beneficial effects might also be
conceivable under similar circumstances. Numerous puzzling obser-
vations, unexplainable on the basis of the major elements, called for
explanations. Even in the past century, some investigations were made
that pointed the way to an appreciation of the importance of trace
elements, but these were not adequately followed up. Reluctance to
proceed is understandable on the basis of an observation of the author
to the effect that even present-day scientists may hesitate to study the
as yet "unexplored" trace elements, for fear that research adminis-
trators may regard their work as visionary and impractical! It is much
safer" to stick to tried and true avenues of research (even if they are
already overworked!) rather than to risk reputation and the hope of
future appropriations by carrying out researches that may prove
unfruitful of positive results. This is, of course, a very negative
attitude from the standpoint of the necessary ultimate working out of
all the details of the science, but it is understandable.

In addition to the known essential trace elements in plant nutrition,
plants also regularly, or frequently, contain traces of other elements
concerning which we have little or no clue as to their roles, if indeed
they have any. These include strontium, lithium, barium, titanium,
aluminium, cobalt, fluorine lead, nickel, rubidium, silver, tin,
vanadium, zirconium and chromium. All higher plants contain
calcium and any natural material containing calcium also contains
traces of strontium, an element with chemical properties similar to
calcium and which therefore tends to accompany calcium in nature.
Do these ever-present trace levels of strontium have any biological
potency? Are they essential to life? Are they harmful under certain
conditions? Even if unessential, are they stimulating, or do they
modify the behavior of plants? Do they, perhaps, in some way
compete with, or interfere with, the normal action of the essential
trace metals? These are questions that must be answered. Until
they are answered, the science of plant physiology will be
incomplete and we shall not be sure we really know what is

Strontium is an element not possessed of an especially versatile
chemical nature. But consider chromium, by no means so innocent
chemically. It exists in several oxidation states. It cccurs in acid and
basic combinations. It has unusually well-developed propensities for
forming complex molecules and ions. Physicists can tell us
that, in the conceptual building up of the periodic system of the
elements by the progressive addition of the electrons to an increasingly
complex nucleus, chromium represents an irregularity in the series, as
may be seen by reference to a table of the electronic configurations of
the atoms. This must confer special properties upon it. Such
chemical "versatility" may be compared with the simpler properties of,
for example, copper or zinc. If, as evidence indicates, these metals,
participatate in enzymatic processes, it must be because they possess.
special reactivity toward the specific organic compounds involved.
Can we safely assume, then, that copper or zinc ions can carry
out their necessary functions in the presence of traces of such a
versatile element as chromium without at least some modification of
the process, perhaps in the nature of an interference? This is specula-
tion, of course, but it is the sort of speculation which must be a
prerequisite to our planning of future research. It is, of course, true
that enzymatic reactions are phenomenally specific. Perhaps we needn't
worry ourselves about anything interfering with the specific reactions
of copper, but we shall never know the answer to the question of
whether copper requirements are modified by chromium levels until
the matter is investigated. The same can be said for many other
such relationships. Suffice it to say that we must assume that the
"incidental" trace elements may play various roles whether they are
essential or not.

minor elements with as much force as to the major elements. Ten or
fifteen pounds or zinc sulphate may suffice for an acre of corn on sandy
land, but this small quantity is fully as necessary as the much larger
quantity of commercial fertilizer that would ordinarily be supplied. The
commercial fertilizer contaning nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium,
might be drastically needed. Without it, a virtual crop failure might
result in the case of very sandy soils in an area of high rainfall. Yet
alone, without the zinc, the commercial fertilizer may produce only a
slight improvement in yield. Nor would the zinc sulphate alone
produce much of a result. Together, the fertilizer and the zinc
sulphate might well produce a several-fold increase in yield. In other
words, the crop cannot prosper unless all of its essential requirements
are adequately met, even those needed only in slight amounts. The
zinc is not a stimulant, but an essential nutrient element and, as such,
it cannot be replaced by any amount of other nutrient elements. This
is, of course, an important implication of the Law of Limiting Factors,
which otherwise tells us that the growth of living organisms will be
limited by a relative dificiency of any single essential environmental or
nutritional factor. The deficient factor is then known as the "limiting
factor" and it alone can control growth and limit production, even
though all other factors are favourable.
The Law of Limiting Factors tells us, in effect, that we cannot
conduct experiments with the major elements without at the same time.
controlling the minor elements. This is not always easy to do.
Failure to appreciate this point in the past has made it necessary to
re-examine the conclusions of older experiments and sometimes to plan
new research to clear up doubts. At least two types of difficulty have
confused earlier interpretations :—
(1) Where one or more trace elements have been deficient
in soils and fertilizer materials, the trace elements have
acted as limiting factors and have prevented the major
fertilizer elements from producing their full effects. An
example of this sort of thing is the previously described
experience in treating corn with fertilizer, but without
zinc. Thus, unless the experimentalist has made sure that
no trace element is a limiting factor, he cannot depend on
reaching sound conclusions respecting the effects of the
major elements.

(2) Since trace elements occur as impurities in compounds
of the major elements, their presence may confuse inter-
pretation in cases where one or more trace elements are
limiting factors. As mentioned above, effects that might
have been attributed to the nitrogen in Chile nitrate, were
later explained as partly due to an impurity of boron.
Obviously any conclusions as to the need for fertilizer
nitrogen would have been clouded by unsuspected benefits
ascribable to the trace of boron in the nitrogen source.
Practices based on these experiments would obviously
prove inapplicable if a change were subsequently made to
a purer nitrate. Moreover, money and time might have
been wasted in supplying more nitrogen than necessary,
just to get the benefit of a trace of boron, which could
have been supplied much more cheaply. An instance of
this sort of thing occurred in New Zealand, where,
formerly, iron ores and salts were fed to sheep to over-
come bush-sickness”. a nutritional anemia attributed at
that time to iron deficiency. The cure worked satisfac-
torily until a new source of iron ore was found which
was ineffective. It was then found that a trace of cobalt
in the iron sources had been largely responsible for the
beneficial effect. One observer made the comment that,
over a number of years, the equivalent of one entire
shipload of ferric ammonium citrate had been imported
into New Zealand just to get a few pounds of cobalt.

Along with progress and enlightenment we have gained our fair
share of confusion and misguided enthusiasm. Especially has this been
true in the domain of soil analysis. Naturally we would like to be able
to analyze soils for their trace elements, and, by referring to some
compendium of data on optimum levels, to decide whether individual
soils contain adequate amounts of trace elements, or are deficient.
Failing in this, we would at least like to be able to analyze plants and
decide questions of adequacy or deficiency. The only trouble is that we
don't happen to possesss the necessary compendium of data against
which to compare our analytical results. Trace element specialists
know this, but they are quite accustomed to putting up with quite a bit
of impatience on the part of usually well-intentioned enthusiasts who
want to be getting along with ambitious programs of soil treatment or
animal feeding on a regional basis. In a few instances, especially in
some local circumstances, critical levels are indeed known to a certain
degree of approximation. Even so, they may be variable, depending
on many environmental conditions. On a regional, or nation-wide
basis, soils and environmental conditions are so variable that, in the
present state of knowledge, it is much too soon to attempt to evaluate
soil deficiencies of trace elements in one central laboratory purely by
reference to analytical values and without intimate acquaintance with
local conditions.
Another way in which enthusiasm can take the wrong direction is
by way of the idea that we should supply trace elements just in case
they may be needed. This is particularly true when we are dealing
with elements that have never been proved essential in the specific
circumstances, if at all. Molybdenum has been shown to be required
by certain pasture plants and by some vegetables. On the other hand,
excessive molybdenum in certain soils has caused serious intestinal
disturbances with cattle, even when the dried forage contained as low
as ten parts per million of molybdenum. Yet carefull experiments
have shown that two pounds of sodium molybdate per acre

Burma is certainly to be expected. In a field near Heho, the writer

There is a pronounced "frenching"; or yellow mottling, which may
be indicative of a trace element deficiency. The same symptoms
are shown by this plant (or at least a very close relative of it) in

Rarely, excessive concentrations of a trace element will render a
soil non-productive. Such cases, however, may be indicative of under-
ground ore resources. As a matter of fact, the analysis of plants for
their content of trace metals has been used as a method of searching
for ores. Geobotanical prospecting, as the method is called, depends.
upon the ability of plants to absorb traces of metals, whether or not
they are used in metabolism by the plant. Geochemists are well
acquainted with the accumulation of trace elements in the topsoil,
the zone of plant feeding, trace metals may be released into solution
by the gradual dissolution of soil minerals or they may travel to this
zone in solution in waters ascending from below during drier periods.
In either case the trace metals are absorbed by plants, later to be
returned to the topsoil in decaying plant parts. They may be
re-absorbed by plants and thereafter carried through the process again.
and again, being trapped, as it were, in a "bio-geochemical" cycle at
the surface of the earth. Since the process of accumulation and

Plans are being formulated for a trial of the geobotanical
method of prospecting in Burma. It is hoped that this method
will give some useful clues to the location of ores whose outcrops would
otherwise be obscured by the mantle of soil. In this activity it is
planned to use the spectrograph as an analytical tool. This versatile
instrument will permit us to examine samples for a number of metals
simultaneously without recourse to tedious "wet" chemical procedures.
Since plant ashes produce fairly simple spectra, it should be possible to
use a medium quartz spectrograph, and hence to avoid the extra work.
of producing multiple spectra that would be required with a larger
instrument. To be sure, plant analysis is fraught with some difficulty
of interpretation, since the concentration level of a trace metal will
depend on the species sampled, on the particular part of the plant, and
on the season. Some plants are better accumulators of metals than
others. The soil is thus likely to be a more stable sampling medium,
but its high content of iron, with the resulting complex spectrum,
makes the use of a large spectrograph mandatory. Multiple exposures
would then be necessary for each sample, if a fairly comprehensive
analysis is required, as it should be. The most suitable conditions will
have to be worked out in practice. Geobotanical prospecting has been

Going still further from the strictly biological realm, we may take
note of some industrial applications of trace element studies. In the
catalytic cracking of petroleum oils, catalyst "poisons may impair
the efficiency of the catalyst. Such a "poison" is arsenic, which
occurs in exceedingly slight traces in some petroleums. The identifi-
cation and measurement of traces in oils often taxes the ingenuity of
analytical chemists.
Instability in storage is an unfortunate characteristic of many
products of organic origin such as foods, medicines and beverages.
Under tropical conditions, with high temperature and humidity, these
tendencies are likely to be intensified. They are known in some cases
to be catalyzed by traces of heavy metals, such as copper. It may well
be, then, that Burmese research workers may find it necessary to apply
the methods of trace element studies to future problems of this
nature.
A considerable volume of work has gone into investigations of trace
metals in biological tissues and excreta, from the standpoint of industrial
hygiene. Metals such as lead and beryllium have been determined in
trace quantities in such materials to furnish a guide to the protection of
industrial workers. Some thought may have to be given to the
problems in Burma, where operations at the cottage industry level
may occassionally be carried out without adequate health safeguards.
One may well enquire as to how slight traces can produce
significant effects, which in some instances in the biological world are
little short of dramatic. Perhaps we may indicate, in an incomplete
way, several categories into which trace element effects may fall:-
(1) Sometimes the trace element is intrinsically valuable and can
profitably be extracted from ores containing only traces
of it. Such is the case with gold and uranium. It is well
to remember, in this connection, that trace elements often
occur in the from of “stray” atoms tucked away in the
three-dimensional atomic (or/ionic) lattice of a crystalline
mineral where they really play the role of unnecessary
'guests" which were accommodated in the lattice
merely because they happened to fit into the geometrical
pattern established by the lattice structure of the
“host” mineral.
