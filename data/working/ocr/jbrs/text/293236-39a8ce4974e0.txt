EUROPEANS IN BURMA OF THE FIFTEENTH CENTURY.
1. One sign of the growth of oversea communications with
Burma during the 15th century is the arrival of European visitors.
It is noteworthy that the earliest European, after Marco Polo, to
leave an account of travels in the cast, Friar Odoric (1322-30), went
direct from Ceylon to Sumatra ; Burma, obviously, was in a back-
water. Ibn Batuta, the Moorish traveller (1325-54), may have touched
in at Arakan; he refers to "the country of the Barahnakar" where
the women were of surpassing beauty, but the men had "mouths
like those of dogs." Yule refers this to Arakan; others suggest
the Andamans. But, although sailing from Sunurkawan [Sonargaon
SE of Dacca], to Sumatra, he called nowhere else along the Burma
coast. He refers however to "martabans (that is, large jars) "2
showing that the port of Martaban and its products were already
well known to sailors in Eastern Waters. The first description that
we have of Burma is by Nicolo di Conti, (c. 1435); then there is a
brief reference to Pegu by a Russian merchant who traded in West
India (1468-74); before the close of the century the Genoese jeweller
Hieronomo de Santo Stefano came to Pegu in 1496. A few years
later is an account given by another Italian merchant, Ludovico de
Varthema (1503-08). Finally we have the reports on Burma given
by the earliest Portuguese writers, containing much information about
the country at the end of the 15th century.
2. Nicolo di Conti.3 This Venetian travelled widely in India
and the Far East in the first half of the 14th century. Having been
forced to accept Islam, when he returned to Europe he sought ab-
solution from the Pope and in this connection dictated from memory
an account of his travels. Doubtless in some points his memory
deceived him, and in many places the scribe miswrote the names of
places; but he gives us a lively picture of Burma, of which much
still holds true. He reached India by way of Ormuz and Cambay
and down the coast to Bizenegalia [Vizianagar],
"Their king is
more powerful than all the other kings of India," and thence to
Ceylon. From here he went to Sciamuthera (Sumatra) by way, it
seems, of the Andamans, but without coming near Burma.
Sumatra he first met the duriano, "The taste" he says, varies, like
that of cheese." Then, after a stormy passage of sixteen days, he
reached "the city of Ternassari, which is situated on the mouth of

he again sailed direct to south India, without calling in at Burma.

How far does this account justify us in believing that Varthema
personally visited Pegu? In discussing this, one must remember
that his journeys lasted more than five years, from the end of 1502
to the beginning of 1508, and the account seems not to have been
written until 1509-10. During these years he visited all the lands
lying along the route from Asia Minor to the Spice Islands, and
carried his travels in his memory without notes. One may therefore
excuse numerous mistakes. Of his general accuracy there can be
no question. His description of the Malays as "men of the sea,
olive-coloured with long hair, and "the visage broad, the eye round
and the nose compressed" is clearly a photograph; he mentions
camphor from Borneo, nutmeg from Banda and tells of "other
neighbouring islands where cloves grow" and gives a good picture
of the cultivation of nutmeg and cloves. There is then a presump-
tion of general accuracy in what he tells us. At the same time, it
must be admitted that in his description of Pegu there is little to
compel belief, and much that strains our credulity. It is strange
that he should find Christians there, and stranger still that he should
find Christians in the army: also it is difficult to explain why he
should say that they "have not many elephants." It is not, per-
haps, impossible that he saw "two women burnt alive voluntarily,
and his mention of it seems to suggest that in Pegu the practice,
confined presumably to Indian residents, was unusual: but it will
be necessary to discuss this matter with reference to Tenasserim.
Another difficulty is his reference to a war with Ava; so far as I am
aware no such war is recorded at this time in the local chronicles.
As regards the products, lac was then and for long afterwards a notable
product of this region, and so were brazil-wood and sandal-wood,
but there never seems to have been much cotton grown, though
possibly this and the silk which he mentions were imported from
up-country and made in Pegu. That he should comment on the
forests of Pegu certainly suggests personal experience; for at that
time timber was unlikely to be exported. The thick canes, pre-
sumably wa-bo, the elephant bamboo, are characteristic of Burma,
but I do not know if they are, or were, distinctive. One still sees
oars such as he describes; but now they are mostly if not solely
used on heavy paddy barges, ordinarily by Indian coolies.
would have thought that similar oars were used elsewhere. He is
certainly correct in picking on rubies as the chief merchandise, and
in giving Capellan, the mediaeval name of the Ruby Mines, as the
place of origin-one notes that he disclaims having been to Capellan.
Then, again, one must note his exact description of the situation of
Pegu. On the whole, then, it would seem safe to accept his

It is not so easy to accept his account of Tenasserim. But of
the two chief reasons which have been put forward for discrediting
it, one is really a reason for accepting it. It has been suggested that
he describes the site wrongly. In fact, however, he displays the
same nice accuracy as he shows with reference to Pegu; I have
never studied the lay-out of Tenasserim with a compass, but it lies.
on the left, or south bank of a river which in general at about that
point runs from east to west. The other reason is certainly of weight.
He goes from Pulicat to Tenasserim, and thence to Bengal, as if Tenas-
serim lay on the coast of India between the two; further he says that
the distance from Bengal to Pegu is 1,100 miles, and to Tenasserim
only 700. But he had no map or chart to show their relative position,
and after travelling many thousand miles he could easily drop or add
a few hundred on any particular voyage after a lapse of four or five
years; moreover the distance from Pulicat to Bengal is only about
600 or 700 miles, whereas he gives the distance by way of Tenas-
serim as 1,800 miles. Again, it is clear that Tarnassari was near
Malacca, and was frequented by junks; further, he took with him
from Tenasserim to Bengal, coral, a product of the Mergui Islands
and Malay Archipelago. There seems therefore no reasonable
doubt that by Tarnassari he meant Tenasserim in Burma.

subject to Siam. It should be noticed that he likens the manner

But it is an Indian rather than a Far Eastern town that he describes.
