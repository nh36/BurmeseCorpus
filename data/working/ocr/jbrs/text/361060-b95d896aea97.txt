THE CULTURAL INSTITUTE
By J. S. Furnivall
*
About two years ago, at a meeting of the Burma
Council of World Affairs, I commented on the fact that
Burma had no national library, no national museum, no
national art gallery and no national theatre. It must
be the only country with any pretensions to civilisation
in any part of the world with nothing of the kind.
Certainly it is far behind all the neighbouring countries
in these matters. This had indeed already engaged the
attention of the Government. From the first attainment
of nominal independence during the Japanese regime
there had been a project of a national museum. And
last year it showed its intention to remove the stigma
by creating a wholly new separate Ministry of Union
Culture.
The Ministry deals of course with all forms of
cultural activity throughout the whole Union of Burma.
But general experience shows that certain aspects of
cultural progress cannot suitably be treated merely on
departmental lines. The creation of a national library
and so on requires an appropriate organisatson as well
as special buildings. It will take some years to put up
a building or buildings that will provide full scope for all
these activities on a scale worthy of Burma and adding
lustre to Rangoon as the capital of Burma. Meanwhile
we must begin on a modest scale and make the best
of what we have. Here we were fortunate.
were fortunate. Many
years ago subscriptions were raised from the general
public all over Burma to commemmorate the jubilee
of Queen Victoria. Out of the funds so raised the
Jubilee Hall was erected and entrusted to the Rangoon
Corporation to be used for the general welfare of the
country. The Corporation recognized that an institu-
tion for cultural advancement would not only promote
the welfare of Burma in general but would add to the

Burmans have never had anything of the kind
and unfortunately only a few Burmans have been abroad
to see this kind of thing in other countries. Some
people think of a museum as a luxury, a collection of
curiosities of no great interest except to a few dull
people with a strange interest in dull subjects. And
they are apt to regard painting and sculpture, the drama,
music and dancing as merely frivolous. Only a compara-
tively few, the more far-sighted, can appreciate the value
of such an institution as a national asset of the first
importance, machinery essential to the welfare of Burma
and to its political, economic and social progress. This
is only natural. Not many people can readily grasp a
new idea. Consider, for example the invention of print-
ing. It was more than a couple of centuries before
people in Europe began to make much use of printed
books. Printing was introduced into Burma rather more
than a hundred years ago. But when I first came to
Burma there were still few printed books apart from
school text-books and religious treatises. Indeed, it was
not until Burma had regained its independence that
there was any effective progress in the use of printing
as an instrument of national enlightenment. Now the
Burma Translation Society and the Mass Education
movement are laying the foundations of a new Burma.
But their work is incomplete without something in the
nature of a university for the common man. That is
what the Cultural Institute (is intended to provide. In
western lands the national and communal libraries,

I myself took a degree in natural science at Cambridge,

Here in Burma nature and history make the provision
of similar facilities a matter of urgent and exceptional
importance for national welfare and progress. What are
the essential conditions of welfare and progress in
Burma? To answer that question it is almost sufficient
to glance at the map. Burma lies in a backwater. It is
shut up within natural boundaries, barred off from the
outer world by mountains and the sea. Within these
natural boundaries successive waves of migration have
left deposited many distinct racial elements.
basic facts of physical and human geography prefigure
the destiny of Burma and determine the problems which
its peoples and rulers have to solve. Until the various
tribal elements are fused together in a common nation,
Burma can never attain stable equilibrium. And until
Burmans can surmount the barriers protecting and con-
fining them, they will remain backward, unable to face
the outside world on equal terms.

These are no new probems. The same two factors
give a key to the whole course of Burmese history.
From the first unification of Burma by Anawratha
nearly a thousand years ago all the outstanding rulers
of Burma aimed at consolidating its unity within its
natural boundaries. But they shirked the problem of
facing the outer world. Burmans assimilated the Bud-
dhist civilisation brought from India by merchants
and missionaries a thousand years before Anawratha;
but with that they were content. There was no attempt
to maintain contact with the course of world progress
and, while other peoples were going ahead, Burmans,
stagnating in seclusion, fell behind. Auk-kya nauk-kya
is a new catchword but an old fact. Inevitably they
paid the penalty. They were unable to face the chal-
lenge of the modern world and Burma forfeited its
independence. The British Government likewise failed
to grasp the essential conditions of welfare and progress
in Burma. It concentrated on the material development

of Burma as a business concern and there was rapid
economic development. But instead of consolidating
national unity it multiplied divisions and, as has often

in the modern world are identical with the conditions
of welfare and progress in Burma. The members of
the various racial units, Burmese, Chins, Kachins,
Karens, Mons, Shans and all the minor peoples must
learn, without sacrificing their distinctive racial charac-
ters, to regard themselves as citizens of Burma. And,
beyond this, all citizens of Burma must be endowed
with all the privileges of citizenship in the modern
world. The endowment of Burmans with world citi-
zenship involves a double problem. We must give
every opportunity to the natural leaders of the people
to train themselves for leadership. And we must extend
the mental horizon of the common man so as to bing
the outer world within his range of vision. A solution
for these difficult problems is a pressing need. In some
ways they are peculiar to Burma, or at least to countries
with a similar mixture of peoples and a like past
Some kind of institution that will help to solve them
is indispensable, but we cannot expect to find an exact
model elsewhere. The Cultural Institute should contri-
bute to a solution, and its precise form must depend
upon its functions. Let us look at each of these in
turn.
One function is to maintain a National Library.
What are the special requirements of a national library
in Burma? In western lands of course most of the
books in any public library are in the national language.
In Burma the national library should of course contain
all the books, periodicals and newspapers of any serious
interest or permanent value that are written in Burmese
or any other local language or in Pali. But there are

Railway Board has its own service periodical and so also

If then the library is to serve as a medium for
opening up the modern world to Burmans it must con-
sist mainly of works in English. It should contain so
far as possible all the standard works in English that
readers in Burma are likely to require. and a selection
of outstanding world classics in other languages. Apart
from these the contents must be adapted to the special
needs of Burma. It will have to cater especially for two
main classes of readers. One class will consist of those
whose task and privilege it is to lead Burma into the
modern world, taking the place of the foreigners who
formerly stood beween Burmans and the modern world.
Most of these leaders will be men who have "completed
their studies" at the university. But have they really
completed their studies? This unfortunately was the old
tradition when administration was regarded as an art of
which one acquired the knack by practical experience.
Long ago Mr. English, I think when he was Commis-
sioner of Bassein, once said to me "We I.C.S. start with
our fair share of brains; but here in Burma we turn into
cabbages." If the brighter lads of Burma are content
to turn into cabbages, Burma will never be more than
a cabbage patch, and foreigners will take the crop to
market. At that time almost every one who took a
degree in the university in Burma, whether in arts or
science, entered some branch of the civil service where

by their ignorance of Burma. Foreign experts can do
something to construct the machinery of welfare but
Burma must, generate its own power to run the machin-
ery. Knowledge is power and the national library
should serve as a dynamo for generating power. Every
one who is capable of taking an interest in public
affairs and especially politicians and journalists who

But in the Library one cannot wholly escape the
limitations of language. That is why a national museum
is still more important than a library for the welfare and
progress of Burma. Just now I mentioned the valuable
work of the Burma Translation Society. One of its
most useful projects is a Burmese Encyclopedia. This

as a means of introducing Burmans to the world outside
Burma. It was in fact attempted long ago in the reign
of King Bagyidaw but apparently never completed and
so far as I am aware nothing of it survived. The need
for an Encyclopedia was suggested at the inaugural
meeting of the Burma Research Society, but at that
time the wings of the Society were clipped and nothing
happened. Now at last we are to have an encyclopedia
for all Burmans who can read Burmese. But what
about Bumans who cannot read or are not in the habit
of reading. A national museum should be an encylo-
pedia in object lessons, teaching the unlettered through
his eyes, and encouraging him to want to read.
Let me give an example. In the Jubilee Hall we
have a model of the projected Kalewa coal-mine. I hope
before long we shall have similar models of the oil fields
and the Namtu mines. Such models arouse the curiosity
even of the illiterate. It should encourage them to ask
where coal and oil and the other minerals of Burma are
found. This can be illustrated in a map showing the
geographical distribution of the mineral wealth of Burma.
And simple plans and diagrams could be devised to serve
as an introduction to the geology of Burma by showing
when and under what conditions they were deposited.

more than a thousand years before the birth of Gautama.

Under British rule Burmans fell still further behind

science and no less important - by showing how the
fortunes of Burma are linked up with the procession

Yet a national gallery in its

It is a matter of common experience that in
most countries the great ages of national history have
been marked by an outburst of artistic activity. And

Burma. But if Burman artists are to do justice to
their innate ability they must be given the opportunity
to study the world of art outside Burma. We cannot
afford to pay tens of thousands of pounds for original works
by the great masters of the past. But we can at a very
moderate expense obtain coloured reproductions that
are the next best thing. And Unesco has come to our
help by making it easier to choose and buy pictures.
Here I have a couple of catalogues from which I hope
the Cultural Institute will begin forthwith to set about
making a national collection. And here there is no
barrier of language. As is said in the introduction to
the catalogues "Art is a language which all the peoples
of the world are now learning to speak and which
they are eager to use in understanding one another."
Here then is another avenue along which Burmans
can enter the larger world. I do not wish to suggest
that our artists should slavishly imitate foreign art.
But by acquaintance with the work of foreign artists
they will learn to enrich the treasury of Burman art
and the national heritage of Burma.
Very similar considerations apply to the depart-
ment of the Cultural Institute dealing with drama,
music and dancing. In some ways these are more im-
portant than the art of painting, for they have a wider
popular appeal and can exercise a more direct educational
influence upon the public. They even flourished within
certain limits under foreign rule while the art of painting
almost disappeared and carving and modelling degener-
ated. My young friend Thein Pe Myint has published.
an interesting little book recounting the progress of
Burmese drama under the influence of the great Po
Sein. In these arts also recent years have seen a great
revival and promising new developments in the intro-
duction of the pyazat and the growth of the Burmese
cinema. But if the artists are to make the best use of
their talents they must derive new inspiration from
free contact with the outer world. And in these forms
of art especially State aid and State encouragement is
indispensable; the Burmese cinema in particular needs
help to enable it to face the competition of foreign
films. What can the Cultural Institute do to help them?

It can help them chiefly by providing a stage on
which they can show their skill. In Thailand there is
such a stage attached to the National Museum. In
Bangkok there are regular performances of drama and
ballet, and three times a week there are free concerts,
two of Siamese music and one of western music. In this
manner one can encourage artists to do their best and
also educate the public to new developments which left
to commercial enterprise might be strangled in their
early days for lack of immediate box-office appeal. I hope
that in a very short time we shall have a stage in the
grounds of the Jubilee Hall that will serve the purpose
of a National Theatre.
In more than one way this should be a really
national theatre. Many years ago in Myingyan District
I used to enjoy the performances of village troupes of
dancers thaing ka de. Under foreign rule these
village performances decayed. And at the same time
there was a rapid growth of crime. This was explained
as due in great part to the dullness of village life and
the lack of occupation during the off-seasons of agricu-
lture. The new plan of democratic local administra-
tion provides a remedy; it allows local authorities to
promote local welfare in any way approved by Govern-
ment. I hope that local authorities will take advantage
of their new freedom, under the inspiration of the Mini-
stry of Culture, to foster district and township competi-
tions between village companies of players. This would
brighten village life and should reduce crime by giving
employment to idle hands that otherwise might be used
for mischief. Then the Cultural Institute would play
its part by providing a stage for an annual dramatic
festival in which the best village companies from all
over Burma might display their skill. Such a festival
would naturally include presentations of folk dances
by all the peoples of the Union.
In my opening remarks I suggested that one
object of the Cultural Institute is to promote national
unity. The Museum would illustrate the common
origin and history of the racial groups, and it should
contain rooms for each major people, illustrating how
much they have in common and also their distinctive

racial contribution to the common national culture of
Burma. The annual festival of drama, music and dan-
cing would help to enphasize and consolidate this
unity.
Here then are a few suggestions as to what the
Cultural Institute might accomplish for the promotion
of welfare and progress in Burma. But the Institute
must be in Rangoon. What about the rest of Burma ?
At least in all the main centres of Burmese History
there should, as suggested by Professor Luce, be a
local museum (In England one can even find very
interesting and instructive village museums.) And cer-
tainly there should be at least one free public library
in each district. The organisation of these local acti-
vities would lie outside the scope of the Cultural
Institute; it would be left to local initiative and the
Ministry of Culture. But the objects of the Cultural
Institute include the training of professional librarians
and curators, and these would be available for appoi-
ntment to local institutions. Also the Cultural Insti-
tute could act as a centre for providing local institutes
with exhibits for their museum, pictures for their gallery
and books for their library. In the small island of
Hawaii there are some half dozen of these local insti-
tutes linked up with the central institute in Honolulu.
Much more do we need them in Burma with its large
area and diverse population. Thus even in the most
remote corner of the country we could bridge the gap
which in the past has separated Burmans from the
outer world. We must start in a modest way and try
to make the best use of the means available. But I
suggest that a Cultural Institute somewhat along the
above lines should function as a power house in the
creation of new Burma and should be a central feature
in national planning. Then, within quite a few years,
one would no longer hear the melancholy refrain auk-kya
nauk-kya, and Burma would take a place among the
foremost countries in this part of the world in contribu-
ting to the common wealth of world civilisation.
