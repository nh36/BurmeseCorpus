R. B. DENNIS
Reginald Burton. Dennis B.Sc. (Eng.) who died at
Rangoon on 11 October, 1951, was born in London on,
25 April, 1900. He was at the Whitgift Grammar School,
Croydon, and received his engineering education at the
City and Guilds College with practical training in various
firms and public corporations and in the Post Office
Engineering Dept. In 1923 he was appointed Assistant
Divisional Engineer in the Indian Posts and Telegraphs
Dept. From 1929 to 1933 he was Divisional Engineer in
Mandalay and Maymyo and then returned to India on
promotion. In 1938-39 he was Director of Telegraphs,
Bombay and in 1939 rose to be Deputy Chief Engineer,
Posts and Telegraphs, India. At the time of his retirement
in 1947 he was Deputy Director-General, Telegraphs in
the Posts and Telegraphs Directorate, New Delhi.
During his service in Burma he became keenly inter-
ested in the country and the people, and recognized
that here there was an almost virgin field for anthropo-
logical research. He was unmarried and had no close
ties requiring his permanent residence in England. He
accordingly decided to devote the leisure of his later
years to anthropological studies in Burma for which he
was peculiarly equipped by his scientific temperament
and training and his warm affection for Burmans and
sympathy with their way of life. In this work he was
encouraged by appointment as Honorary Correspondent
to the Anthropological Department of the British Museum
and to the Horniman Museum and Library. Among the
matters that engaged his attention were the customs
with regard to the robes of Buddhist monk and with
regard to the various styles of hair-dressing and hair
ornaments. The latter work brought him into contact with
the children, who found in him a sympathetic and
understanding playmate, despite the disparity of age.
It led him on to a study of children's games and songs,

and this in turn to a study of folk songs in Burma.
The
first fruits of his researches were made available to the
public in fascinating lectures which have been printed
in this Journal. The talk on children's games, illustrated
by a troupe of his small friends, was so generally wel-
come that he was more than once called on to repeat
it for the benefit of a new audience. On his untimely
and unexpected death in October, 1951 after a short
illness; he left a considerable volume of material which
is now being studied with a view to ascertaining how
much can be made available for publication.
All the members of our Society will regret that a
venture promising of so rich a harvest should have been
cut short so abruptly and, although he was of a retiring
disposition and made friends more easily with children
than with grown-ups, all those with whom he cast off his
reserve must deplore not only the set back to anthropo-
logical studies in Burma but the loss of a kindly friend.
