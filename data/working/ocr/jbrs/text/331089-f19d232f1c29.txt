Dr. Stewart has left his mark in Burma.
will be remembered chiefly for his contributions to the

He was born and brought up in Scotland and
liked to be known as an Aberdonian. At Aberden Uni-
versity, and probably earlier at his village school at
the neighbouring village of Strichen, he was well in-
structed in the ancient classical languages, Greek and
Latin. He graduated with first class honours in Clas-
sics in 1903 and won University prizes for Latin and
for verse composition. He kept up a long-standing
friendship with Professor Harrower, the Professor of
Greek, until the Professor's death. In after years he
continued to take pleasure in ancient Greek and Latin
literature, and to the end of his life he kept up the ha-
bit of composing verses in those languages-usually epi-
grams of a humourous turn to express his thoughts on
various occasions.
In 1904 he was successful in the open competi-
tion for the Indian Civil Service. For his year of pro-
bation he went to Christ Church, Oxford, and won an
Indian Civil Service scholarship. Successful candidates
were allowed to state eir preferences as to the pro-
vinces to which they might be assigned, though they
did not always get their first choice. Stewart chose
Burma and got his choice. He arrived in Burma to-
wards the end of 1905. 'Competition wallahs' were not
always of the bureaucratic type. A famous exception
was Sir George Grierson, who carried out the great
Linguistic Survey of India. Stewart resembled Grier-
son in that from the beginning of his official career
the study of the languages and literature of the
country where he served was more congenial to him
than the ordinary duties of the Indian Civil Service.
Yet he was reckoned a good officer. In the early years
of his service the work of junior Indian Civil Service
officers in Lower Burma, where he was posted, was ex-
tremely heavy in consequence of the rapid expansion of

Stewart was one of the earliest members of the
Burma Research Society and contributed many articles
to the Society's Journal. His articles deal with various
literary, linguistic and historical subjects and with Bur-
mese, Chin and Mon folk-lore. He took the leading
part in the enterprise of the Society in collecting ma
terials for a new Burmese-English Dictionary and from
1926 to 1931 supervised the work of recruiting readers,
supplying them with books to be read and collecting
the word-slips recorded by them. More than seventy
readers contributed. During the later years of his ser-
vice in Burma his reputation as an authority on Bur-
mese studies was well established. He was called upon
for advice when the Government began to pay more
attention to the language and literature of Burma, and
he was appointed by the University of Rangoon to be
an external examiner in Burmese. When he finally left
Burma in 1933 he was appointed by the University,
to which the Burmese Dictio ✓ enterprise had been

one of the two Editors of the Dictionary. Soon after

His life in these later years was industrious. He
found time to write articles for Chambers' Encyclopae-
dia, papers to be read before meetings of Societies, re-
views of books etc. During the war he acquired enough
knowledge of the Annamite language to act as censor
of Annamite correspondence, and in Bishop's Stortford
did good service to his neighbours as a Chief Air Raid
Warden.
Stewart's work on the new Burmese-English Dic-
tionary is his most memorable achievement. Only one
Part had been published at the time of his death-and
the whole work on the same scale will amount to
twenty or more suc arts. The collection of the ma-
terials, as has been inutioned, was carried out under
his supervision, and he designed the form of the work
and settled the many details of practice to which sub-
sequent work must conform. He decided to include ety-
mological notes in spite of the present lack of publish-
ed authorities and he secured the help of Mr. G.H.Luce
and U Wun for this purpose. He decided to make
some attempt at historical treatment of words by in-
dicating the dates of works quoted and arranging
amples generally in chronological order under each
meaning. He decided also not to exclude examples
where the meaning is obscure and to attempt transla-
tion and explanation of obscurities when the word is
from a work of repute. In these and other decisions
he has set the standard of a large-scale Dictionary,
which when it is completed will do good service to

building the two editors with three Burmese assistants
worked for some months in 1946 and 1947, and there
the work is now being continued by the surviving ori-
ginal editor and Dr. Hla Pe as de facto joint editor.
