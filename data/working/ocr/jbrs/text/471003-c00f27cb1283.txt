SPIRITISM IN BURMA*
Dr. Ba Han**
1. Primitive Man and His Environment
The primitive man found himself confronted by conflict and hostility. The primeval
forests, waters and hills were awesome, and the beasts of the field were menacing. Man's
life was hard and insecure. For his livelihood he had to clear the forests and plough the
land. The wild beasts had to be destroyed or at least kept at bay. In this struggle for
survival he was at some disadvantage. Compared with the animals, he is physically
weak. His arm is not too strong, and he is not very swift of foot. He does not possess a
well-developed hairy hide or large teeth or strong claws.
Yet man's spiritual side which is inseparable from his physical side gives him a
decided superiority over the animals. He has therefore been enabled to subdue his
environment and establish his supremacy.
2. Astronomical Forecasting and Astrological Prediction
Man gradually came to learn that the heavenly bodies influenced the change of the
seasons and the ebb and flow of the tides. And on the seasons and the tides he was
dependant for his cultivation. He had therefore to observe the sky to forecast the
astronomical appearances. This correlation between the happenings in the sky above
with the occurences on the earth below led man to invest the heavenly bodies with
certain aura of divinity. He saw in the pattern of the sky the pattern of Fate decreed by
the gods. Celestial events were believed to give a clue to the future. The reading of the
future in the heavens gave rise to sky divination. The connection between astronomy
and astrology thus began early in the history of man.
In process of time the boundary between astronomical forecasting and astrological
prediction became exceedingly thin and impalpable. We read of star-gazers, astrologers,
and monthly prognosticators in ancient Babylon.' The Babylonian astronomers have
left us continuous dated records on baked clay tablets in cuneiform script of celestial
events from about 747 B.C. They could predict eclipses and could tell beforehand when
the cresent of the new moon would be visible for the first time on the western horizon.*
In the days of Nebuchadnezzar, King of Babylon (Circa 707 B.C.), magicians, sorcerers,
enchanters, soothsayers and astrologers were consulted by the King.³

6. Buddhism and Nat Worship
Now both spiritism and astrology are scathingly reprobated by orthodox Buddhism.
Auguries, charms and astrological practices are branded as the base arts in the rules of
morality (sila) prescribed for the higher training of the Buddhist who would tread the
path of escape from existence.9 Burmans still cling to the belief that the Arthava Veda
which foretells future events such as the day of one's death has been destroyed for the
good of men. 10
Two eminent monks Shin Maha Thilawunthall and Shin Maha Rahtathara12 who
are also poets of the front rank have in unmistakable terms denounced nat worship as rank
heresy. And no Buddhist monk with a reputation to lose will state the contrary. More-

It may be said without any stretch of language that the Burman Buddhist is
weighted with a schizophrenic tendency. On the one hand, he accepts unquestioningly
the condemnation of spiritism as anathema by his religion. Yet on the other hand, he
resorts to spiritism without a twinge of conscience. His mind is thus split between two
unyielding contradictions. It is the old story of Dr. Jekyll with Mr. Hyde lurking behind.

Whether after death a Tathagata exists or not, whether he exists and does not exist,
whether he is neither existent nor non-existent.'14 For all that, a Burman finds it difficult,
if not impossible, to escape the feeling that there is in him something indefinable which he
knows to be his own and very self and which survives death. This indefinable something
is called leikpya (butterfly fairy). Orthodox Buddhists maintain that the leikpya is illusory
and that it is nothing more or less than jiva or the life principle.15
Be that as it may, popular belief has fastened on the leikpya certain characteristics.
They are these. The leikpya resides in the human body which it can leave temporarily.
without causing death. During sleep it wanders abroad and then returns to its tenement.
It may lose its way and fail to return home. In that eventuality, the body it has left
expires. Owing to the belief in the persistence of personal identity after death, some
Burmese people mark the body of a dying person with turmeric or soot to serve as
identification marks when he is reborn in the next existence. What is more, the leikpya
that has finally left its tenement is supposed to forget or hate the earthly relatives left
behind if it happens to go under the tree of forgetfulness or the tree of hate in the other
world.16
Witch-doctors say that sickness is due to the absence of the leikpya. They further
claim that they can capture the leikpya or recall it by suitable food offerings (Kaw ခေါ)
and mantras. Those who believe the pretensions of witch-doctors call in one of them
when a person is in a precarious condition or in a swoon to perform the leikpya Kaw
ceremony for inviting the leikpya to return to its abode. If the patient's condition
improves the leikpya is said to re-enter its owner (leikpya-win).16
It is also commonly believed that when two persons are wrapped up in each other,
as in the case of a child and its mother or of a husband and his wife, their butterfly
fairies are closely knit together. On the death of one, it is feared that the butterfly fairy
of the other will follow that of the departed. To prevent this calamity a witch-doctor is
asked to perform the leikpya-kwe ceremony. (Separation of the butterfly fairies). 16
