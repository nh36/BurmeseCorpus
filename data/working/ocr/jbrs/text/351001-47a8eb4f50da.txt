INTRODUCTORY
The evening is the principal time of the day for children's regular games,
particularly those which involve running about. School from nine in the morning
till three for the little ones and till four for the Second Standard upwards means an
early breakfast, supplemented during the twelve to one break, instead of the
morning meal at about ten o'clock. After school there is the bathe and the evening
meal at about five and the sun sets at about six in winter, about seven in summer.
Most cut door games, especially the older ones, are therefore traditionally played
by moonlight, beginning when the air begins to cool at dust and going on till eight
or nine at night, after which the children need no urging to bed.
There are however short holidays, the chief in October for the "Festival of
Lights" (the full moon of the month Thadingyut which is the end of Lent) and no
school on duty Days (s) the days of full moon, change and the eighth waxing
and waning) or on the Preparation day (5) before each but the long holidays,
early March to late May covers the hot weather, when children usually spend the
day, apart from an afternoon sleep for the younger ones, playing indoors or outside
in the shade.
Such play is dealt with in the first section of these notes. In the next six
sections are described regular games observed during the past three years mainly
on those Duty days which coincided with closed days of European run schools,
which do not observe Duty days, and with dry weather too hot to begin romping
about before sunset nor too dull to attempt to take photographs. The Eighth and
last section deals with indoor games which, when played at all, usually take place
after the evening meal, otherwise in the afternoon. and are mostly games for
grown-ups only.

I. CHILDREN'S INFORMAL PLAY
Children's play, other than regular games, is mainly of two kinds, making
things or models of things, and-imitating adult activities. As the children, being
both observant and ingenious, are, continually inventing fresh forms of such play,
a list would be endless but a few examples from play observed or taken part in will
serve to show the kinds of things they commonly do.
Many boys make their own paper kites (the season for which is between
Thingyan and the onset of the Monsoon), all make paper tails, pound glass and
make their kites strings abrasive with it, and some older boys make winding reels
for them. All make sticks for their hoops, which are cycle wheel rims or the like
taken from the scrap heap, and clay balls for pellets (see Game 18, note 2) for them
and for playing marbles (see under modern version of Game 17). At the beginning
of the rains they make, if about twelve years old or, ii younger, get an older boy(or
else father, especially for small daughter with no older brothers, for girls also play
with but do not make these to make or parents to buy (at one anna each) a bamboo
pop-gun, ɔɔεε (hbaung-hbaung, an onomatape). This is a length of green
bamboo about two inches in circumference cut just below one node and again just
below the next, giving a length of nine to twelve inches. It is again cut across, a
couple of inches or so above the node and a piece of split bamboo is pared down so
that when hammered, towards the node, into the hollow of the smaller length, the
handle, it will not come out again. The remainder of this piece, the piston, is pared
down till it is a sliding fit in the other length of bamboo tube, the barrel, and is cut
to about half an inch short of the length of this, about seven to eight inches.
Measurement is all by eye and the only tool used is the large, heavy, household
knife, the da (∞: pron. : from hda). Ammunition is the immature berry of the
Mya-ya tree (Gp Grewia microcos) which boys climb up and gather for
themselves. The berry is also known as εε: pop-gun fruit, and is crushed

piston (or right into the far end for first loading), then aim is taken, commonly at a

Though the final upward turn in cutting out the jacket is often omitted, leaving
the bottom edges straight, the jacket resembles the winged upper garment worn
with the old style htamein by stage princesses and with ceremonial dress more than
it does any jacket ordinarily worn now. Men's jackets button (with buttons and
loops of cord made of sewing thread) from the neck straight down the front. Those
worn by women and girls have the top button centrally at the throat whence the
right hand side of the jacket is cut straight down to the fifth and bottom button
centrally at the waist. The left side is cut diagonally downwards to the right from
the top to the second button, thence straight down to the third and fourth and
thence across and slightly downwards to the fifth button. The five buttons, always
detachable, are of glass, matched colored stone or, nowadays, plastic material and
So on.

There are many things which boys and girls make together. Using some pieces
of discarded roof thatching, sticks and bamboo they found lying about, in one case
a boy and a girl built themselves a hut about two by three by two feet high, in
another case three girls built a rather larger one, both when showery weather, in
which to see if it would keep the rain out, was likely. A discarded baked clay
model ox, old toy of a child of some wealthier house, gave two children the idea,
after they had patched up its broken Paris with mud, of setting up, with bits of split
bamboo, twigs, leaves, grass, wayside flowers and other oddments to hand, a
miniature cow shed with roof, walls and door, a rice field and a garden. Small
seeds dropping from a tree necessitated sweeping the garden, now and then with a
little bunch of grass stems, commenting the while how merry and untidy cattle are
in their habits and what a lot of work there is in keeping the place clean. They
mimic consciously and with gentle, often dry, humor. On finding the remains of
the handle bars of a bicycle, the same two, with an old piece of canvas, a couple of
bricks and same pieces of wood and of iron wire, constructed what they said was a
motor cycle and when the writer, whose own vehicle had doubtless been the
inspiration, was obliged to sit on it and "ride" it, he not only found the controls
generally in the right places but float the older of the two children then eleven and
a half years old, had a fair idea of what they were for. Cultivation of edible herbs,
flowers rice and fruit trees (in patches far too small and closely set for most things)
appeals strongly from the age of ten or twelve even to children who have lived
nowhere but in crowded town suburbs.

Structures made normally last only a few days and when the end comes,
whether, by collapse, by drawing upon the materials to make something else, by
the weight of some object unthinkingly shifted or the foot of some unobservant
grown-up, there are no tears though they are never knocked down for fun. But if at
male grown-up is the cause he is informed in detail, with tragic voice and gesture,
of the devastation his clumsiness has wrought in the land of make believe. Only a
male grown-up is expected to receive these histrionics with due patience and
sympathy.

and, assuming grown-ups' very best manners, sit down just on the mat, a boy with
legs crossed under him, a girl with legs to one side and feet behind her,

Finding a dead bird near the house is of course the occasion for as long a
funeral procession as can be arranged, headed by the oldest child present carrying
the corpse, more or less at arm's length, on a large leaf or piece of cardboard and
covered by a leaf and ending with actual burial a few inches in the earth.

their little movements, their chumminess and their happy smiles brightened the
face of every passer by. Then an elderly, nondescript bagman passing, stopped,
pulled out two nanas and gave one to each of the little ones.

A less pleasing form of imitation play arose in 1949-50. The eldest child of a
group (always, in the writers, experience, a girl) appointed herself leader (S),
had a mat spread out on the highest piece of ground avail-able, then led the rest in
single file, herself usually walking beside or to rear of the middle of the line, to a
solitary bush, tree trunk or post which all then belabored with the sticks they
carried. When enough damage had been done (mostly to the sticks she called them
off, marched them back in single file again to the mat where she sat down, told the
rest to pile arms, ordered one to fetch water for her, others to prepare her breakfast
and so on. After a time the whole performance would, be repeated on another bush
or some other object. It was never dear whether they were pretending to be
Government troops, insurgents or dacoits but other people disliked the game
intensely and firmly discouraged it.
Among forms of children's play other than making things, imitating adult
activities and regular games, tree climbing deserves special mention because both
boys and girls are very fond of it. Beginning on exposed roots as toddlers, by
twelve or so they can manage the upper branches of any medium size tree whose
lowest branch they can get at without shinning up the trunk. As no bravado or
being dared tempts them beyond their skill, falls are rare.

advantages of features and bright colors but they are too big to make houses for,
their clothes cannot be changed and they cannot sit down. The majority of children
have no ready made toys at all but there are now some locally made toys of
European style such as pistols, guns and tricycle, some of excellent quality, which
cause more trouble than all the indigenous games put together. Even Burmese
children's inventiveness and imagination has found little more to do with toy
firearms then to go round shooting everyone and brainless exercise of the legs on a
tricycle round and round a block of houses can he equally monotonous. That can
be varied where there are two boys with tricycles for they can have a collision,
quarrel over who should back away, try to hurt one another's knuckles with their
fists, one another's shin with their feet, try to overturn one another till one starts
crying and both are in thoroughly bad temper. Other phenomena which seem to
come inevitably with ready made toys are touchiness about ownership and
privilege of using, and the dog-in-manger attitude, all of which unlovely things are
entirely foreign to uninfluenced normal Burmese children's play of all kinds.
It appears however that tricycles did not bring these results at once for in 1900
it was stated, "the toy of the day is the tricycle, which parents will pinch
themselves to provide for their children, and which the happy possessor of will
make the common property of all their playmates" (MBF. 9). Parents to-day
confirm the existence of this attitude during their own childhood and are at a loss
to explain its comparative absence now. Children of Burmese Buddhist homes do
however frequently give away some of the simpler home made toys, such as paper
kites and rag dolls; to their younger playmates (quite irrespective of the relative
affluence of the parents), often indeed making such toys specially for that purpose.
Parents or elder brothers making toy boats, carts (and nowadays motor trucks) for
young children, or helping the children to make them, which was common in the
more leisured days of thirty years ago, still occurs, but not often.

The occasional 'problem child', immodest in act and speech, is abnormal also
in that he hits to hurt, bullies, teases unkindly and is disrespectful to adults. Other
children are slow to ostracize him but react strongly when-ever he misbehaves.
Even smaller children rebuke him vehemently, hit him or chase him away. A
wooden slipper may be used to hit him with or that or a stone thrown at him, things
not done otherwise. Parents beat him and, if he offends again soon after, shut him
in a room alone. Otherwise, corporal punishment is exceptional. The most
pronounced case well known to the writer was a boy of eight and a half at the time,
big and strong for his age. Once, when he snatched and threw away off the
verandah a nine inch disc which was hoop to a very small boy, there being no one
else to take action at the time, the writer seized the bully's hoop, which was no less

warm hug and "Uncle, do it again," but showed what was needed.
On meeting again a year later his first request was to be picked up and turned
over though that particular treatment had never been repeated. At nearly ten, he
still wets his bed impenetently, at school is near the top of his class one month,
bottom the next, cheeks anyone if he thinks he can with impunity, is always full of
energy, high spirits and mischief, but no longer behaves immodestly, and he wants,
and is ready to give, any amount of affection. An only child, he owes much to his
now increasing friend-ships with other boys of his own size and strength. The
close, continuous association of each Burmese child with all the neighboring
children of all ages, and to a lesser extent with those children's parents and
relatives, is a major factor in the child s upbringing. It is most obvious and
effective only where adult behavior is uniform as in the matter of personal modesty
and again in kindness to children (and indeed to all living things, especially smaller
and weaker than oneself) in both of which the conduct of every adult has in general
been exemplary. Children are hardly instructed or admonished in these things at
all.

A point of behavior is picked up for the moment from a child's own parents
just by childish mimicry and then fixed by including it in play, instilling it into
others by example and precept and helping still younger ones to copy it too during
play together, so that the traits become as characteristic of the new generation as of
the last.
Babies are thoroughly spoilt by parents and everyone including children. For
the most part, toddlers and younger continually howl to gain their ends but change
rapidly once they achieve confidence in their legs. It is then that the vivacity,
affection and generosity, so prominent in their natures a little later, first appear.
Quite commonly a child who if so much as glanced at will howl, merely to gain in
company, the undivided attention of its mother or whoever may be holding it at the
time, a few weeks later will be found fondling one's knees, smiling up then raising
its arms ready to be picked up and carried as the expression of its desire for closer
acquaintance. Thereafter, instead of howling it will hail its new friend with joy
whether the next meeting is soon or not till weeks or even a month or two later.
From as early as three years old children are considerate to younger and
smaller ones and the absence of tears even in the rougher games, is a striking
feature of all their play. A child lords it over younger brother or sister, but let no
one else presume too far. They are capable of intense anger, little body taut, speech
lost and eyes red if little brother or sister seems unkindly or unjustly treated. An
ordinary tiff is forgotten in minutes but this may take days to get over. In spite of
so much chivalry, the oldest child in any group at play always expects to win and
is not generally a good loser. As will be seen in the following sections, the
competitive element in many games is attenuated and in many more, including
most of what are probably the oldest, it is absent altogether. A purely competitive
game such as Me Thida (No. 26), which is mainly of chance, or Tiger (No. 27)
which is entirely of skill, they are liable to turn into an hilarious orgy of cheating
but in outdoor games of the competitive kind it seldom goes further than one
cheating being talked about afterwards and twitted about it next time, before play
commences.

One is apt to think of Games of Skill as necessarily competitive but Chinlon
(Game 16 is a well known example of a non-competitive game of skill. Shwe Yoe

struggle for supremacy between competitors, In the broader sense of a diversion or
spell of play, it certainly is a game. It is the only one of the seven games in this
section which is entirely non-competitive. There is a winning team in each of the
preceding section and all the Indoor Games (Section VIII) are competitive but
there is no winner or winning side in any of the other fifteen regular games in this
collections, nor in children's informal play (Section I). Thus competition is not in
general a feature of Burmese games and in those games in which there is a winner,
or a winning team, at the end of a round, play is seldom continued to determine the
winner of a number of rounds. A score of one all is just as satisfying as being the
winner in the best of three, because it is the spell of play itself which is enjoyed.

forms; of Game 17, in Game 22 and still more in the traditional form of game 18,

21. ZE-HKOP.
The game is played by children and by young people, particularly young
women, up to the age of 20 or 25 or so.
Players sit or squat down in a circle and have seven stones of not more than
about 3/8" diameter, preferably fairly smooth pebbles. It is said that each in turn
should toss all seven up, try to catch them all on the back of the hand, toss them
from there and catch them again in the palm and that the first to succeed plays first
in the game proper but actually one player starts by common consent without any
preliminary.
A player spreads out six of the stones on the ground with a space of one to two
inches between each and, using one hand only, throws the seventh up to about two
feet from the ground, picks up one of the six without touching any of the others,
catches the seventh, drops into the other hand the one picked up, tosses the other
again and catches it after picking up a second one and so on for all six. The next
move is to arrange the six stones in pairs, toss the seventh and pick up a pair before
catching it, drop the pair into the other hand and so for the other two pairs. Then
the same is done with one group of four and one of two, then with five and one.
then with all six and then one, two and three. Finally three stones are put aside, the
other three taken in the hand, the seventh tossed up, caught after putting the three
down, tossed again and caught after picking up the three. All this with three and
the seventh is done three times over.
If a player misses at any point, or his hand touches a stone not due to be picked
up at the time, he loses his turn and the one on his or her left plays. When a
player's turn comes round again he must start again from the beginning, no matter
what point he had reached before, and the first to complete the whole series wins
the game.

card games. On the occurrence of a death, all doors and windows of the house are
kept open day and night, and all lights burning all night, for seven days (counting

third day (i.e. the next day but one after the death). To prevent burglaries, there

relative staying one of these nights may on any account leave till the last day) up
throughout the night, particularly the younger then, and these keep themselves
awake by playing indoor games.
