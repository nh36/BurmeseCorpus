I. Introduction
The problems-political, social, and economic, in a newly indepen-
dent country are so interrelated and intertwined that it is difficult
to discuss one without digressing a little into the others. Thus the
discussion of the economic development problem of Burma without
touching on others, though however little it may be, will tend to leave
the reader bewildered amidst a maze of facts and seemingly unrelated
events the import of which he may neither be able to discern nor
evaluate. The need is felt more so by the fact that Burma, an
ex-colonial country, has emerged from the feudal economy of its kings
to a free competitive economy under a colonial regime and then to a
period of planned economy under her national government within a
short period of sixty years or so. The changes that took place, it must
be admitted, were of such a nature as to leave the people unprepared
for the new set of situations. This resulted in their being placed at a
disadvantage, at first primarily, in the economic field. To it must be
attributed part of the problems that attended Burma and that was
coming to a head just prior to the out break of the war. The
seriousness of the problems manifested themselves in the general
discontent and the instance wave of nationalism that followed.
But then the war and the independence struggle, which only
served to shelve the problems for a time, had created far more
serious problems than those which Burma had before.

II. Burma-a short account
Burma is situated between India and China. She shares a commom
frontier with India, China, Pakistan, Indo-China, and Thailand. Its
area is 262,000 square miles and enjoys a 2000 mile coastline. About
57% of the total area is forests. According to the latest census there
are 19 million inhabitants in Burma. The average density of population
is 65 per square mile; the high density area of population being in the
Irrawaddy delta and along the Irrawaddy valley. There are many racial
groups in the country and the majority racial group i. e. the Burmese
form 60% of the total followed by the Shans 14% Karens 7%, Kayahs
7% Kachin's 2.5% and the Chins 2.5. It is an agricultural country
with rice its principal crop having a total shown acreage of 12 million
acres. The annual yield of paddy in 1940-41 was 7.4 million tons. Of
this 3 million tons was exported annually. It was a source which provided
Burma with 44% (£18,000,000) of the total foreign exchange earnings.

The Cross Domestic Product of Burma just before the war was
estimated at K. 4945 millions. The Per Capita output or production
was K. 326 and Per Capita Consumption K. 206/-. But the figures
given above will not give an insight into the problems which existed in
Burma before the war. Suffice it to say that the main features of the
situation prevailing at time were :-
i. that there was an agricultural indebtedness, estimated at
£ 50,000,000 with nearly half the cultivated land in lower
Burma being in the hands of non-agriculturists largely
money lenders-Chettyars from Southern India.
ii. that there was no Burmese commercial class or rich
middle class worth talking about.
iii. that the Burmese people as a whole had little or no share
in the export or import trade of the country.
The above situations alone, or they to a large extent," contributed
to the intense nationalist feelings that eventualy brought about agitation
and struggle for independence outside the British Commonwealth.
War with all its destruction swept twice over the length and breadth
of Burma. As a result it was estimated about half the national wealth
was destroyed. Added to it came the insurrections which still persists
though on an abated scale. Burma in short on attainment of indepen-
dence inherited a shattered economy. The extent of the damage can
best be gauged by the fact that nearly half of all towns and practicaly
all the port facilities, railways, and inland water transport systems were
destroyed. Half of her paddy fields were on the verge of reverting to
jungles and her cattle and elephant population reduced to twofifths the
prewar figure. The newly independent country of Burma therefore was
confronted not only with the destruction that war brought in its wake

Now before I go into how these economic and social problems
were dealt or are being dealt with in my country I would like to pose a
question to the members of this seminar, perhaps for academic conside-
ration, as to what alternatives were open for a country facing a situations
such as those encountered by Burma? Was it possible for the Govern-

the country faced as it was with situations described above? Could the
Government of Burma abstained from nationalising land?. Was the
nationalisation of the Inland Water Transport System, Electricity Supply,
and Timber and Rice Marketing justified? Was such an action detri-
mental to the interests of the country?. If so how was Burma to raise
capital for her economic development program? (remembering the
fact that the war and the insurgency had made Rice the only source of
foreign exchange earnings; export of Oil Timber and Minerals being
made impossible by the prevailing situation). i.c. about 1950.
It was at this juncture that the Government of the Union of Burma
engaged the services of an American firm of consultants to draw up an
economic development program for Burma. The American firm in
question is none other than Knappen-Abbot-and Tippets Inc. popularly
known in Burma as K.T.A. By this time Burma had earned consider-
able foreign exchange because of the high prices that rice fetched in the
world market (about £80/- per ton). The Economic and Social
Development Plan which was ultimately drawn up for implementation
came to be known as the Pyidawtha Plan. (Pyidawtha literally trans-
lated into Burmese means Happy Pleasant Country).
III. The Pyidawtha Plan or the Economic and Social
Development Program of Burma
The Pyidawtha plan envisaged the investment of K. 500 million for
an eight year period commencing from 1952. The Plan was to bring
about an increase in the Gross Domestic Product to K. 7,000 million-
an increase of 63% over that of 1952 (estimated to be K. 3900 million)

The plan was to be implemented in two four year plans. They
were designed to allow for a maximim flexibility and amendment and
adjustment in the light of new information, additional studies,
experience and changing circumstances.
There were however two assumptions to the plan.
They were: i.
that the price of rice, the main source for foreign
exchange earnings, would not fall below £45/- per
ton by 1959-60 or at least not below £55/- before
1956.
ii. that the insurgency could be dealt with at earlier
date.
In fact the price of rice fell to £36/- per ton and the result was
that certain projects requiring heavy foreign exchange expenditure had
to be deferred temporarily. As to insurgency though it was to a certain
extent reduced complete restoration of law and order was still left to

be attained. Of these two factors the latter was the more serious.
Though the fall in the price of rice resulted in Burma's inability to
finance the projects out of its own resources they were in any case
executed with the help of the loans and credits which Burma managed
to obtain without any undue delay from the United States, India and

The first four year plan made considerable headway inspite of the
many difficulties experienced by the government. Government made
capital investments of just over K. 1,700 million during this period.
The Gross Product in 1956 was K. 4430 million and when compared to
that of 1952, which was K. 3636 million, showed a 21% increase. In
1957 the capital investment was K. 495 million and the Gross Domestic
Product showed as K. 4690 million. It registered a 5.8 % increase over
that of 1956. Still the figure was 5% below that of the prewar figure
of K. 4945 million.
The second four year plan which is in execution went through a
thorough review and reformulation in the light of past experiences and
situation prevailing at the time. Again acknowledgement must be made
of the help received from many countries especially the United
States of America for rendering Burma a loan of $25 million
under her aid program and a further $17 million under P. L. 480. The
second four year plan, which went through modification, aimed at a
real increase in the total output of the national economy of 28% from
1955-56 to 1959-60. A great emphasis was laid to restoration of law
and order. The resultant reduction in investment in the national
economy due to this emphasis was not expected to lessen production
and economic growth. Improvement in the state of law and order in
itself was expected to bring about substantial increases in agriculture,
timber and mineral production, and manufacturing and other indus-
tries. A very modest 5 to 6% yearly increase in the total national
production over the remaining four year period was the goal. In short
the second four year plan not only involves a modification of the original
plan in capital expenditure but also incorporates in it the experiences
encountered in the course of the first four year plan. It also makes
provision for better implementation.

It is a point of interest to note that different people react different-
ly to a set of situation. The type of setbacks experienced by Burma in
her economic and social development plan, it is presumed, is sufficient
to cause a change of government in any country. In Burma however
the people reacted differently. The Prime Minister was very frank in
admitting the setbacks and the people in general excused him for the
setbacks in the plan. It is felt that the following excerpts from a book
entitled "Burma" issued by the British government stationery office
sums up the attitude of the Burmese people to the entire scheme of
things. One of the excerpt reads "Even if success will not have been
as great, or indeed nearly great, as was at one time hoped, the fact that
Burma has adopted a comprehensive plan (much of which is of lasting
value) has given a direction and stimulus to her administrators………………………”
The other excerpt reads "Less intensively developed at the time of
their independence than were the peoples of India and Pakistan, and
with their modest administrative legacy gravely damaged by insurgency,
the Burmese are applying themselves with determination and in the air
of freedom to the tasks which they have set themselves."
