WESTERN ENTERPRISE AND ECONOMIC DEVELOPMENT
IN BURMA+
by
U KHIN MAUNG KYI*
The purpose of this paper is to
study the characteristics of the develop-
ment of Western enterprise in Burma
and to analyse the impact of Western
enterprise as an institutional system on
the economic growth of the country. It
is assumed that the Western capitalist
enterprise, organized on a large scale,
with its emphasis on rational economic
calculation, widely contrasts with the
traditional patterns of the peasant
economy. The introduction of Western
capitalist enterprise into Burma and its
interaction with the native economy and
society is an experience, the study of
which would shed some light on the
patterns of socio-economic development
in Burma.
Though the study of the development
of Western enterprise as an institutional
analysis is a relatively unexplored field,
searching examination of the colonial
economic structure in Burma has already
been made by some economists, both
native and Western.¹ They usually deli-
neate important structural
aspects of
colonial economy, such as the dependence
of the economy on the export of raw
materials, and question whether the
colonial economy, tied as a raw material
producer to the imperial power, was
viable enough to grow into a self-generat-
ing productive economy. They point out
that the policy of free trade with the
resultant elimination of the less competi-
tive Burmese and the "skimming" tactics
of foreign investment, that is, the invest-
ment in quick yielding businesses and the
withdrawal of capital when the
opportunities for quick returns were
exhausted, left the colonial economy
virtualty as undeveloped as it would have
been without a chance to capitalize on
its early rapid economic expansion. They
also examine the relative shares enjoyed
by the foreigners and the nationals in
the seeming prosperity of the colonial
era. Their conclusion that the natives
shared very little of the colonial
prosperity is well documented. However
enlightening the studies of capital structure,
its concentration and recovery, and the
resulting effects on the process of growth
may be, the story of colonial development.
cannot be complete without understand-
ing the institutional arrangements which
gave rise to opportunities for such
development. For behind the economic
categories such as profits and yields, and
production and exports lie the efforts of
men and their organizations, and the

Western Enterprise and Economic Development in Burma
study of the origin and growth of Western
enterprises and their ultimate power of
control will explain the processes of the
colonial society and its bearing on the
Burmese social conditions of that period.
In addition, Western capitalist enterprise
in its purest form is presumed to be an
institution based on economic calculations,
rational work organization, and the
employment of wage labour. This form
of organization places itself in stark
contrast to the traditional institution of
the family or kinship which emphasizes
ascriptive and diffuse obligations. Thus,
the intrusion of Western capitalist
enterprise into the traditional
Burmese
society itself might have created new
conditions, such as the emergence of
wage labour, urbanization, and technical
diffusion, which themselves unleashed
forces of change.
Since the long term economic progress
of a country could only be furthered by
the development of native entrepreneurial
activity and technical improvemrent, we
would like to offer as an alternative
criterion in evaluating the performance
of Western enterprise the question of
whether it had promoted technical progress
and generated the emergence of a local
entrepreneurial class. We would ask such
questions as what diffusion of technology
had occurred in Burmese society during
the colonial period and what were the
prospects of development of a native
entrepreneurial class. Why did the Burmese
entrepreneurial class fail to develop, as
was the case, apart from the usual
explanation of Burmese lack of business
acumen or the lack of capital? These
questions can be answered only when the
growth of Western enterprise is considered
in relation to its impact on the local
conditions. This paper will take up as
its central issue the evaluation of the
impact of Western enterprise on native
development, especially in the area of
entrepreneurial development and technical
diffusion.
In the first part of this paper we
shall present the origin and growth of
Western enterprises in Burma and discuss
the nature and characteristics of their
operations. The second part of the paper
is concerned with the conditions under
which and the ways in which certain
foreign business communities had come
to amass monopolistic control of busin-
ess operations in Burma. And in the
third part, we shall also evaluate the
prospects of the emergence of Burmese
entrepreneurial activity and the extent of
technical diffusion within the context
of the colonial economy.

Western Enterprise and Economic Development in Burma
We have seen that trade in those days
was helped by the contact and patronage
one could collect from higher circles in
government. Under the Burmese regime
Burmans naturally had the function of
acting as intermediaries between the
Western enterprise on the one hand and
the local customers or the court on the
other. Under foreign rule, Burmans became
less useful to Westera enterprise especially
when Indians and Chinese more sophisti-
cated in business methods could be
recruited as ready and willing compradores.
The story of Western enterprise in China
indicates that Western enterprise helped
promote the emergence of a native business
community which later even challenged
them. In Burma, with the flood of more
experienced and competitive Indian and
Chinese immigrants, Burmans could not
get a chance of working as intermediaries
between Western enterprise and local
customers, nor could they grasp at oppor-
tunities available in the lines of trade
in which European firms were not
interested.
The sluggishness of technical diffusion
due largely to the active policy of prefer.
ring foreigners over Burmans in industrial
establishments further retarded the growth
of local entrepreneurship. Burmans, having
been denied the experience of apprentice-
ship in Western enterprise were insulated
from the progress of technical and mana-
gerial knowledge. The rapid expansion,
consolidation, and dominance of a few
Western firms also prevented local attempts
to start enterprises on modern lines from
achieving success. As a concrete example
we would like to cite the case of the
Burma Spinning and Weaving Company.
There was a serious attempt by patriotic
Burmans to set up a national weaving
factory at Myingyan.52 The enterprise was
incorporated as a public company with
the capital of 3 million rupees in 1921.
The company's subsequent failure was
presumed to have been caused by Steel
Brothers and Company whose interest
ran counter to that of the new company.
Serious attempts to dislodge the Irrawaddy
Flotilla Company from its monopolistic
domain also met a similar fate.
All in all, we have concluded that
Western enterprise contributed little or
very insignificantly to the welfare of the
Burmese community: it either failed to
share the fruits of colonial development
with the Burmese community, or had
not generated in any effective way the
process of entrepreneurial development or
of technical diffusion. Some may look
upon this conclusion as rather strong or
biased. Yet we wonder how anyone could
have arrived at a conclusion different
from ours if one had taken up the job
of evaluating the results of colonial
venture with any reasonable degree of
impartiality and sincerity. It is a sad
story that under the then prevailing
circumstances, Burmans never had an
opportunity even to function as “compra-
dore", an intermediary between local
customers and Western enterprise, let
alone to compete with Western enterprise.
One has to admit that under the colonial
administration Burma has fared much
worse than almost any other country in
the British empire, even without taking
into consideration what she could have
accomplished by herself had her indepen-
dence not been interrupted.
