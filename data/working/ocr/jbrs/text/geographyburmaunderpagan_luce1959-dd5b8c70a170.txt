by
Professor G. H. Luce
AN UPCOUNTRY SCHOOLMASTER wrote to consult me some
years ago, about a series of historical maps of Burma which he
proposed to publish for use in schools. I do not know if his maps
have actually appeared. The work is certainly worth doing; but it will
not be easy to do it well. The Burmese Chronicles, with all their merits,
abound in anachronisms; and if we depend on them instead of inscrip-
tions and other contemporary records, we are bound to go wrong. The
inscriptions themselves are not geographical treatises, and do not
generally localise placenames, which were well enough known, no doubt,
at the time of writing. Moreover Burmese placenames - beautifully bota-
nical-are apt to repeat themselves; a Nyaungbin-gyi or Letpan-ywa
may occur in any district. Furthermore there is hardly more than a
single surviving placename - S'rikṣetra - which antedates, with any
certainty, the 10th century. Mr. Harvey's History, it is true, gives a
map of "Burma about 700 A.D.", but he rightly calls it "conjectural";
his second map, of "The Kingdom of Pagan", is only less so. The
one really historical map known to me is that of Huber, at p. 680 of
Vol. IX of the Bulletin de l'Ecole Française d'Extrême-Orient, intended
to illustrate the Mongol conquest of Burma; but it is fort he most part
limited to Chineses sources, and does not extend south beyond Pagan.
What then can be done towards the mapping of ancient Burma? The
first extent geographical description of the country is in Ptolemy, about
150 A.D. Gerini's method of reconstructing Ptolemy is not to be
recommended; the result is only to land us in a desert of imaginary

Nothing can be easier than to plot the map of Ptolemy and fill in the

greatly; capital Sigma and Beta, for example, tend to be confused; and
no two texts agree about the latitudes and longitudes. Let me only add
that all identifictions of modern Burmese place-names with those
given in Plolemy, must be viewed with grave suspicion.
Six or seven centuries pass before we can think again of making a
map
of Burma. Our sources now are Chinese. In 801-2 A.D. Nan-chao,
which controlled the north of Burma, was for a short period at peace
with China; and the Pyu of Burma, under her wing, sent an embassy
to Ch'ang-an which is fully recorded in the T'ang histories. Already
the T'ang Court, notably the minister Chia Tan, had "investigated the
geography of the frontier and road-mileage in great detail". Chia Tan's
main work, the Shih-tao-chih ("Record of the Ten Regions"), is lost,
but a series of seven itineraries compiled by him survives in the New
T'ang History. One of these goes from Tongking to India overland,
and where it crosses the north of Burma, alternative routes are given,
one going directly west from Yung ch'iang to Kämarūpa (Assam),
the longer one diverging to the south-west, to visit the Pyu capital.
These routes were traced with amazing skill and patience by Professor
Pelliot in his magistral treatise “Deux Itineraries" (B.E.F.E.O. 1904 ).
Another T'ang work, the Man -shu, ("Book of the Southern Barba-
rians"), compiled by Fan Ch'o about 863 A.D., is particularly rich in
information about the north of Burma, though the text is sadly corrupt.
Putting these three sources together-the Man-shu, Chia Tan's itinera-
ries, and the Pyu ambassador's account of their own country as
recorded in the T'ang Histories, one might perhaps attempt to compile
a second map of Burma; but it would be highly conjectural.
Names for Putao, the Hukong Valley, Manipur, the Upper Chindwin,
the Mali Hka, the 'Nmai Hka, and a number of places and tribes
along the frontier might be inserted with approximate certainty; but
we cannot yet place with assurance even the Pyu capital of this
period; the intermediate points are therefore resting in air; practically

nothing certain can be fixed in Lower Burma; the Chindwin is
described as flowing into the sea; so that in placing important towns
near its mouth one must choose between Monywa and Bassein!
One central fact emerges. The names are non-Burmese. One may
even doubt if they are Pyu, for often the Chinese (reconstructed) shews
final consonants which were not admitted, at least in writing, by the
Pyu. To my mind, one inference is clear. It is only from 832-5
A.D., when the Pyu, and some of the Mon, principalities of Burma
were overwhelmed by tribes subject to Nan-chao, that we must date
the coming of the Burmans. The T'ang authors do not specify them.
but they were very likely included in the "Mang" tribes, due south of
Yung-ch'ang-in Hsenwi perhaps of Hsipaw, who were so called because
they called their kings mang of mang-chao. They were conquered by
Nan-chao rather late, in 794 A.D., and we hear rather less of them
than of the tribes further north. The Mranmā tribes may have been the
southernmost, since the Chinese do not mention them. That they were
deeply influenced by Nan-chao is not to be doubted. All this,
together with other reasons which will appear in the course of this
paper, lead me to believe that the Burmans entered the plains of
Central Burma in the 9th and 10th centuries, from the east or north-
east rather than the north, driving a wedge beetween the older
populations, or pushing them westwards or absorbing them, and so
forming a part of the great western drive of the southernmost Tibeto-
Burman tribes which swept from China across Burma to Arakan.
The third historical map of Burma will be that of the kingdom of
Pagan, say 1000-1300 A.D.; the maps before you to-night are a pioneer
effort in this direction. The physical ground map-which seems to me
magnificently done - I owe entirely to the generous co-operation of
Professor Beasley and the Department of Geography and Geology at
University College. I feel sure that the Burma Research Society will
second me in proposing a very hearty vote of thanks to Professor
Beasley for all the immense trouble he has taken. At Professor
Beasley's suggestion we have written the names on the transparent linen
sheet covering the ground map. I am also greatly indebted to the
Rev. S. Wan Hui and Ma Mya Mu for most kindly, and most
beautifully writing in the Chinese and Burmese names.
I have hesitated a good deal about the method of writing them.
We have now left entirely behind us the 8th-9th century names which
the Chinese learnt from the Pyu; the names are now predominently
Burmese, and lineal to the modern style of naming. And yet, to

My main authority has been the Burmese inscriptions of the
period. With the exception of a few untraced, all the original inscrip-
tions known to me-some 300 in all-have been checked, and places
mentioned in them indexed. Old Mon inscriptions - mainly of Kyanzit-
tha's reign, 1084-1113 A.D.- have given some additional names. A few
are taken from the earliest Siamese inscription, that of Rāma Gamhen
of Sukhodai, dated 1292 A.D. Chinese names are taken from the text
translated by Huber, the Huang-yüan-cheng - mien - lu, and the Yüan-
shih or Dynastic History of the Mongols. The material gathered from
the Burmese inscriptions is abundant, but rarely precise. Names are
mentioned casually, in passing, as a mere label for land dedicated. Many
of them are lost; and even when a name survives, it is not infrequently
has a new application: thus the Plañmanā and Mraňkhlan of the inscrip-
tions are not the Pyinmana and Myingyan of the modern map. To
avoid mistakes, it would be necessary to index all the inscriptions right
down to modern times, and so keep continuous hold on the geography.
This I have not yet been able to do; and must confess that my placing,
e.g. of Pusim at modern Bassein, of Caliy at Sale, of Talup at Talokmyo,

of Sagaing, Minbu, Magwe and Prome have not, I think, been written.
One is led, I am afraid, to the conclusion that, geographically, the
general histories of Burma are inadequate. Burmese Chroniclers seem
to have gathered their material at haphazard, without much local
research, and where their sources were in conflict, decided on a priori,
often aesthetic, grounds which version to follow, and could rarely bring
themselves to miss a chance, either to point a moral, or to adorn a tale.
Which drives me to a further judgement that at the same time as we
study and index the rich corpus of inscriptions, we must begin again in
the districts to compile step by step, sittan by sittan, township by town -
ship, the living history of old Burma. Is it too late? Can much be
added to local information already gathered in thamaing and district
gazetteers? Is it still possible to follow in other districts the admirable
example of the late U Ba Thein's Ko Khayaing Thamaing? Many
persons in this room can answer these questions better than I.

names we know, must be situated. Yet Pagan itself was never a kharuin;
