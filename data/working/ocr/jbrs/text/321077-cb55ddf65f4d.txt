THE JUNK IN PALI?
In 1475 A. D. two ships were sent by the King
of Pegu, Burma, to the King of Ceylon on a Buddhist
mission. On their return the next year their adventures
and missionary work were recorded on stones in Pali and
Mon, parts of which can still be seen at the Kalyanisima
at Pegu. On the reverse face of the second stone of Pali
the following passages can be read:-
*Samuddagàminiyà Jonganàvày' orohápetvá, nadigàminiyo
nàvàyà' bhirùhåpetvá Tigumpanagare vasåpesi," "he had them
disembarked from their sea-going vessel and conveyed to
Tigumpanagara in river boats."*
"Tato Citradutena saddhim ekadasasu theresu Sihalaranno
dùtam àgamantesu yeya Kalambutitthe pasàn' olambakam
katvá, thapita janakayabhirucham jongámahànávam
Paràdhabhidhano viruddhamahavato ufthabitva, samudde
osidapesi." "The eleven theras, and Citradata, therefore,
waited for the emissary of the Sinhalese King, and
anchored their ship at the port of Kalambu. Meanwhile,
a violent wind, called 'Paradha', arose and sank in the sea
the large sea-going vessel, in which passengers had already
embarked."

JONGA () in the above passages is not met
with in Pali and Sanskrit Dictionaries, and according to
Pali scholars of Burma the word is un-Pali-like. The
editors of the Kalyani inscriptions are silent about its
peculiar nature and strange form. They simply translated
it as "the large sea-going vessel" and "6". The
Mon version of the record is, unfortunately, badly
damaged at the part where this word should occur,
so that it is impossible to know the then Mon equivalent
of it. The word has not yet been traceable in modern
Mon. However about 25 years after the Kalyani inscrip-
tions, i. e. in 1501, a Portuguese, Duarte Barbosa by
name, came out to the East and he left notes on his
Asian travels. About Pegu he writes:-
"There are in that place great ships of three or four
masts, which they call Juncos, which sail to Malaca (and
Samatra) and many other places.”+
Can we connect Jonga of Kalyani Pali Inscrip-
tions with Junco (Jungo, O. E. D.) of Barbosa, and the
Malay jong and the Javanese djong and modern Junk?
