THE BURMESE EMBASSY TO VIETNAM 1823-24*
B. R. Pearn**
Until recent years Burma and Vietnam had little contact of an official character,
but there is one instance on record of the despatch of a diplomatic mission from the King
of Burma to the Vietnamese Court. It appears that in 1822 an official of the Vietnamese
Government named Cao-dahn-lam, serving in what is now South Vietnam but was then
commonly called Cochin-China, suggested to the Governor of that part of the country
that wealth could be gained by purchasing esculent birds' nests in Burma and exporting
them to China. Attracted by this prospect Chao Kun, the Governor, sent this officer by
sea to Rangoon; he took this step on his own responsibility and without the sanction of
his Government. Cao-dahn-lam arrived safely in Burma and reached the capital, but his
lack of credentials from the Vietnamese Court aroused suspicion and he met a cool recep-
tion. In the end he succeeded in satisfying the Burmese authorities of his good faith,
and though nothing came of his commercial proposals the King, who was considering
making war against the Thai, decided to follow up this avenue of approach to Vietnam in
the hope of gaining an ally. Cao-dahn-lam was sent back to Vietnam, and with him went
a Burmese diplomatic mission, bearing presents and a royal letter in which it was suggested
that the two countries co-operate in conquering Thailand and partitioning that country.
The Burmese mission to Vietnam comprised two Burmese officers and a man named
Gibson. Gibson's antecedents are obscure. He is said to have been born in Madras,
the son of an Englishman and a Telugu mother. According to his own account, his
father was a commander 'in the country service', meaning the captain of a vessel engaged
in local trade as distinct from the English East India Company's ships trading to Europe.
He claimed, probably correctly, to have passed much time in Burma during his childhood
and to have been educated at Nabet, in Sagaing district, where there was a Roman
Catholic mission ministering to the descendants of European captives who had
been settled there. He had, he said, travelled widely, twice visiting Lisbon
and once going in a Danish ship from Tranquebar to Vietnam. He spoke several
languages, including his mother's tongue, Telugu, but does not seem to have been
acquainted with the Vietnamese language. His father's occupation as sea-captain, his own
travels, and his connexion with Burma, all suggest that he should be identified with
William Gibson, who appears in the list of non-official residents in Calcutta given in the
East India Directory for 1804 and subsequent years, where he is described as a mariner,
owning and commanding the vessel Ann, of 200 tons, built in Rangoon. He was appa-
rently well regarded by the King of Burma, and was employed by him to make maps.

When I was at Court, for the first time, in 1819, the year of his present
Majesty's accession to the throne, the late Mr Gibson, who afterwards went
on a mission to Cochin-China, was engaged by the King's orders in construct-
ing a map of the Burman dominions, together with the adjacent countries
of Hindustan, Siam and Cochin-China.1
The Burmese mission left Rangoon in January 1823, and reached the British
settlement of Penang the following month. There disaster befell them, for the ship in
which they travelled was destroyed by fire and they only just managed to save the letter
and presents which they were charged with. However, through the good offices of
Captain Henry Burney, later to be Resident in Burma, the Government of Penang made
a loan of 4,000 Spanish dollars, the Spanish or Mexican dollar being the general interna.
tional currency in use in South-East Asia at that time. Burney also arranged passages
on a Portuguese ship bound for Macao, so that the mission could go on its way. Gibson
asked Burney how he could show his gratitude, and Burney suggested that as he was
going to a country little known to Europeans, an account on his return of what he had seen
would be welcome. Gibson therefore undertook to keep a journal, recording the events
of his mission.2
On April 22, the mission left Penang, and after touching Malacca and Singapore
they arrived on June 1 off the mouth of the Mekong delta, where they anchored at Cap
St Jacques, known locally as Vung-tao. Thence they went by boat through the delta and
on the 8th reached Saigon, the capital of southern Vietnam or, as Gibson called it in
European manner, Cochin-China. The party were well received: four 'barges of
ceremony' escorted them during the later part of their trip through the delta, and at
Saigon they were met by seven elephants. They remained in Saigon from June 8, 1823,
to March 14, 1824, a stay of nine months.
During this lengthy sojourn, they did not lack for hospitable treatment. In
accordance with the custom of those days, they were dependent on the Vietnamese for
their sustenance. They received gifts of food and also of money: in all the Governor
gave them 1,262 kwan or about 840 Spanish dollars. They were entertained both
by the Governor and by his subordinates to feasts and military displays, and were
impressed by the splendour which the Governor maintained. Thus at one entertainment,
wrote Gibson,
The retinue of his Excellency the Governor-General was on this occasion
magnificent: it consisted of sixty elephants, horses, litters, and a thousand men
under arms and in regular uniforms. Everything glittered with gold, and was
conducted without noise or disorder.³

Their long residence in Saigon gave them the opportunity of learning something
of the political situation in that part of Vietnam, though as they hardly moved out of the
town prior to their final departure, their observation was necessarily limited. The
Mekong delta, formerly part of the Cambodian kingdom, had been occupied by the
Vietnamese during the preceding hundred years or so, but the population was for the
most part still Cambodian; and Gibson, like a number of other travellers of his time,
applies the name ‘Kamboja' to the territory. The Vietnamese, they found, had also a
strong position in the remnant of the Kingdom of Cambodia that still had some semblance
of independence; and Gibson refers in his journal to a Vietnamese officer as being
Governor of Cambodia. Relations between Vietnamese and Cambodians were evidently
not easy: there was rivalry between Vietnam and Thailand for control over Cambodia,
and though at this time the Vietnamese were in the ascendant, the situation was unstable.
The Cambodians were discontented and the Thai were ready to take advantage of their
restiveness. In conversation with the mission, the Cambodians expressed dislike of the
Thai, but Gibson suspected that this was a diplomatic dislike, assumed to please the
Vietnamese, some of whom were present at the time: his own opinion was that the Cam-
bodians were more oppressed by the Vietnamese than they had been by the Thai when the
latter were dominant. Gibson noted that Cambodians of high rank were allowed to wear,
and presumably were expected to wear, Vietnamese dress, while the lower orders, he wrote,
'must appear in their native habit, which is nearly that of Siam'.4
Owing, it would seem, to a general mistrust of foreigners and in particular of
foreigners who had any contact with the restive Cambodians, the Vietnamese authorities
took steps to prevent any intercourse between the emissaries and any Cambodian notabili-
ties. After an aunt of the King of Thailand, who was widow of a Thai prince, had made
contact with the mission, the Vietnamese officer in charge of the Burmese party, Ong Bo
by name, was reprimanded; a subordinate official attached to the mission was sentenced
to the cangue or wooden collar; and a Portuguese interpreter was whipped. After this, a
careful watch was kept on the mission and, so Gibson recorded, spies were set over their
movements.
An exception to the prevailing dislike of foreigners was, it seems, the Chinese, who
were allowed to enter freely. One entry in the journal records the arrival of four junks
from China, carrying 1,300 passengers; they had paid six Spanish dollars each for their
passage. On another occasion Gibson noted the arrival of a junk with 400 passengers.
The Chinese, he wrote, settled in various parts of the country, some going as far as the
capital of Cambodia. They settled especially along the rivers which were the principal
means of communication. They were poor when they arrived, Gibson observed, their
total possessions comprising a coarse mat and a small bundle of old and patched clothes.
Thousands of Chinese, he observed, were also going every year to Thailand and to the
towns in the Straits of Malacca, meaning Singapore, Malacca and Penang. However poor
the Chinese may have been on arrival, some evidently prospered, for Gibson and his

The only non-Asians whom Gibson mentions as being in Vietnam were a handful
of Frenchmen. When the Emperor Gia Long was struggling for the throne, he had the
aid of a French missionary, Bishop Pigneau de Behaine, who in 1787 negotiated a Treaty
between Gia Long and the French Government; the beginning of the French Revolution
in 1789 ended hope of aid from France, but Pigneau gathered a band of Frenchmen from
Pondicherry who served Gia Long well. After he attained final victory over his enemies
in 1802, Gia Long retained the few surviving Frenchmen in his service, though Pigneau
had already died in 1799; and there were still two in employment when the Burmese
mission was in Vietnam. These were a couple of naval officers, Philippe Vannier and
Jean Baptiste Chaigneau, both of whom had the rank of Mandarin. Gibson indicates
the presence of three other French laymen, but gives the name of only one, Pierre Diard
who was not employed by the Government but was a naturalist who had been working in
Vietnam since 1821. The position of the French mandarins was not very comfortable.
Gia Long had shown them gratitude but his successor, the Emperor Minh Mang, who
ascended the throne in 1820, was suspicious of all foreigners, and though he still employed
these two, they were far from happy. One of them told Gibson that they were contem-
plating leaving the country on account of the Emperor's unfriendly attitude; and indeed
Vannier and Chaigneau did go back to France in 1824. There were also some French
missionaries in Vietnam who were viewed with even less favour. The Emperor's dislike
of Europeans, recorded Gibson, was openly expressed, and the practice of the Christian
religion had been forbidden-though in this Gibson seems to have been premature, for
though such a ban was enforced later, it had not then been imposed. It is quite likely,
however, that in southern Vietnam the Governor, knowing the Emperor's views, had
taken measures to discourage Christianity.
:
The Frenchmen were, however, still useful to the authorities on occasion. The
embassy ran into difficulties in presenting the royal letter which they brought, for the
Vietnamese could not read Burmese and none of the embassy could put the letter into
Vietnamese. An attempt was made to employ the Thai language as a medium, but for
reasons not stated the translation into Thai proved unsatisfactory; so the French were
called in, and they translated the letter into French and Latin.
But though the Vietnamese authorities mistrusted Europeans, they were by no
means ill-informed about their affairs: indeed, the fact that they were well informed may
account for their mistrust. The Governor displayed a keen interest in the career of
Napoleon Bonaparte, whose only fault, he said, was his vast ambition which had led to
long wars and in the end had produced no good for the French nation. The Governor
also regarded the British as over ambitious and foresaw that they would extend their
influence into the Malay States of Perak and Kedah and over Junk Ceylon and 'Pulo,
Lada. Obviously, the ambitions of the Europeans were viewed with uneasiness in
Vietnam.

The Governor's reference to Pulo Lada—for the comma between 'Pulo' and 'Lada'
in the Journal is obviously intrusive-is interesting. Pulo Lada or 'Pepper Island' is in
the Langkawi group of islands, off the coast of Kedah north of Penang. Though these
islands have not figured in history to the extent that Penang has, they had a similar
potentiality and towards the end of the nineteenth century the German Government tried
to purchase them from Thailand, to whose Government Kedah was then subordinate.
That Chao Kun should not only have been aware of the existence of Pulo Lada but also
of its possible usefulness is evidence of the width of his information.
The mission's members found much to interest them. Apart from political matters,
Gibson recorded the rivalries and intrigues which went on amongst the Vietnamese
officials; he noted the construction of the great canal of Ha-tien. The party were interest-
ed in the celebration of the New Year or Tet festival at the end of January, in connexion
with which Gibson recorded that the people had no objection to eating dogs, cats, rats
and alligators. On one occasion they saw at the Governor's audience eight men,
obviously different in type from the Cambodians and Vietnamese, who were said to be
the original inhabitants of that part of the country, prior to the Vietnamese occupation.
According to Crawfurd, who later published Gibson's Journal, these were members of the
Loi' people, who professed the Hindu religion and lived under their own chief in the
country once known as Champa. From this it would seem that the people whom the
mission saw were in fact Cham.
This supposition is confirmed by the fact that Chapman,
in his report on his mission to Vietnam in 1778, says that he heard of a people, distinct
from the Vietnamese, who inhabited the province of Champa called 'Loys'. The Cham
were evidently reduced to a state of poverty, in contrast to their one-time greatness, for
Gibson describes them as being very poorly clad and says that the Governor gave each of
them a pair of trousers and a shirt.
But interesting as all this no doubt was, the members of the mission must have
found their long stay in Saigon very tedious, for they were not allowed to go to the capital
and the business which had brought them there made but slow progress. At the outset
of their visit, the Vietnamese officials expressed some scepticism about the advantages to
their country of the projected alliance against Thailand. Their first visitor was the
secretary to the Governor -- or, as Gibson sometimes calls him, the Governor-General.
The secretary wanted to know why the Burmans had themselves failed, after so many
attempts, to conquer the Thai, and how any benefit could ensue from an alliance between
two countries, Burma and Vietnam, which were too far apart to act in concert. The
mission took the line that a close connexion between the two countries was quite feasible:
Vietnam held the southern end of the River of Cambodia - i.e. the Mekong and Burma
held the northern end; if the Thai, who held the middle, were eliminated, contact would
be easy. In the north, too, the only obstacle to contact between Burma and the part of
Vietnam known as Tongking was 'the tribe of the Lenjen or Laolantao'. By the Lenjen
or Laolantao, Gibson evidently meant the ancient Kingdom of Lan Chang, the Kingdom
of the Lao: he seems to have been unaware that more than a hundred years earlier this
Kingdom had fallen apart into three States-Luang Prabang, Vientiane, and Champa-all
of which were now under Thai domination. He and his colleagues said that Lan

In the mission's first interview with him he said that there was likely to be war between
the British and the Thai over the affairs of the Malay State of Kedah. At this time the
Thai were endeavouring to establish a claim to suzerainty over Kedah and had in 1821
expelled its Sultan, who had taken refuge in Penang. The Sultan had close relations with
Burma and also hoped for aid from the British to whom Penang had been granted in
1786 in the expectation that in return Kedah would be protected from its enemies.
Though the British gave the Sultan no aid, the dislike felt by the officials in Penang to-
wards Thai expansion in the Malay region was well known, and relations between British
and Thai were seriously strained and rumours of war thus arose. It was a similar rumour
that not long afterwards caused Chao Anou, ruler of Vientiane, to try to throw off the
Thai yoke in the belief that the Thai would be preoccupied in resisting the British, an
attempt which led to his defeat in 1826 and his ultimate death in captivity in Bangkok.
The expectation of an Anglo-Thai war was thus widespread and the Governor of southern
Vietnam was affected by it. He no doubt reasoned that if the Thai were involved in war
with the British, then a Vietnamese attack on Thailand would be facilitated, and in that
case the proposed alliance with Burma would be worth considering. Gibson, however,
apparently not grasping this point, replied that he had heard nothing about the possibility
of an Anglo-Thai war.
The Governor harked back to this matter, saying that the British ‘had lost the trade
of the Eastern Countries through means of Malacca' meaning that the rendition to the
Dutch at the end of the French wars of the port of Malacca had deprived them of oppor-
tunities for trade-and that therefore they were likely to look to the Malay peninsula for
expansion. Would this lead to war with the Thai, he asked? But Gibson again held
out no prospect of such an event.
In conversation with the Governor's secretary Gibson reverted to the point which
he had referred to earlier, that the King of Burma was well established in the upper part
of the Mekong valley. If, therefore, a road were cut through Lan Chang a profitable
trade between Burma and Vietnam could be developed. He also mentioned the sea-power
which Burma could bring to bear against the coasts of Thailand. But the secretary was
not encouraging: the Governor, he said, was well aware of the strength of the Thai forces
but he knew nothing about the Burmese army and would need more information on this
point before committing himself. Gibson therefore suggested that a Vietnamese observer
be sent to Burma when the mission returned home.

Meanwhile the royal letter which the mission had brought had been sent, with the
translations, to the capital, Hué, and on August 4 orders arrived for the Governor to go
to the court. It was, however, necessary to provide for the efficient government of south-
ern Vietnam in his absence, and only at the end of October did the arrival of an acting
Governor from the capital make it possible for Chao Kun to leave Saigon. Before he
went he told the mission that their proposals would be considered without delay at Hué,
but that since the matter demanded careful consideration, the two countries having
previously had no relations, he was likely to be absent for some months. He left Saigon
on November 19.
As the mission had arrived on June 8, over five months had now passed and their
offer of an alliance had not even been taken into consideration at the Vietnamese court.
They now had the prospect of a further delay of months, and this must have distressed
them, for they were very much cut off from contact with Burma and with the outside
world generally. They had no news of events outside Vietnam, apart from a rumour that
the Thai, having perhaps got wind of the projected alliance, were busy strengthening the
defences of Bangkok, and apart from a report brought by an English ship of the death of
Lord Castlereagh, whom Gibson erroneously refers to in his Journal as Prime Minister,
instead of Foreign Secretary, of the United Kingdom. Even this news they did not
receive at first hand, for they were not allowed to see the ship's captain but were, Gibson,
wrote, 'as closely watched as if they were confined in a gaol'. However, through the
help of one of the Frenchmen they were able to send the captain a report for transmission
to Burma. Again later on they sent a report of their proceedings by a junk which was
sailing for Singapore. Apart from these two occasions the mission had no opportunity
of sending information to Burma. Thus they were very much isolated.
It was not till the middle of February, 1824, that any news reached them about the
progress of their business. On the 13th of that month they heard that orders had come
from Hué to arrange for their transport back to Burma, and on the 18th they learnt that
three officials and a secretary were to accompany them home, bearing a letter and presents
for the King. They still did not know what decision had been reached about the Treaty
till the 28th, when the French naturalist, Diard, arrived in Saigon. He told them that he
had been directed to accompany them on their homeward journey, and he conveyed to
them the royal decision.
The Foreign Minister, Diard said, had spoken in the Council against the proposed
alliance, but Chao Kun had been in favour of it, saying that it might help Vietnam to
regain the province of Bantaibang-i.e. Battambang, part of Cambodia which had passed
into Thai hands. The project was also favoured by the two French mandarins, Vannier
and Chaigneau (referred to by Gibson as 'Cheneaux'). But the prevailing sentiment in
the Council was adverse, and so the proposed alliance was rejected. Gibson failed to
ascertain clearly the reasons for this decision: he ascribed it to the contempt which the
Vietnamese felt for all other peoples except the Chinese. Whatever the reason, the
mission had failed in its object.

So on March 7, 1824, the mission formally took charge of the royal letter and
presents, and on the 14th of that month they embarked and dropped down the river.
After some delays due to adverse winds, they finally left Vietnamese waters on March 31.
Their troubles were, however, by no means over. They reached Singapore on
April 9, and there learnt of the outbreak of war between Burma and the East India
Company. This placed Gibson, as a British subject, in an embarrassing position. He
and his companions were sent to Penang, where he again saw Burney, whose advice and
assistance he once more sought. Burney suggested that his knowledge and experience
would be useful to Captain J. Canning, who was Political Commissioner with the British
forces in Rangoon, and Gibson, who was now destitute, willingly accepted this idea.
The Penang Government lent him 1073 dollars which, like the sum advanced a year
earlier, was not repaid. Gibson was then sent by sea to Rangoon where he was given
employment as an interpreter; but a few months later he died of cholera at Prome.5
The Burmese members of the mission made their way by junk from Penang to Tavoy, but
a few days after their arrival Tavoy was occupied by British forces and they became
prisoners of war. Their Vietnamese companions from Saigon were sent safely home.6
While in Singapore on his return journey, Gibson gave a copy of his Journal to
J. Crawfurd, Resident Councillor in that island. Crawfurd, who later conducted a mission
to Burma to negotiate a commercial treaty, had himself visited Vietnam in 1822, and, like
Gibson, had achieved nothing; he was naturally interested in Gibson's account. Gibson
also gave a copy to Burney in Penang. Crawfurd later published the Journal along with
his own account of his mission to Vietnam (Journal of an Embassy from the
Governor-General of India to the Courts of Siam and Cochin-China, London,
1828). As published, however, the Journal did not appear in its original form: according
to Crawfurd, Gibson's education was poor and the original abounded in errors of grammar
and spelling; Crawfurd therefore made an abstract of it, and it is this version which is
still extant.
The Burmese embassy to Vietnam led to no positive result. At that time, 1823,
Minh Mang had been on the throne of Vietnam only three years and he may have felt
that he was not yet sufficiently well established to engage in the hazards of war, especially
as there was much discontent in Tongking owing to a shortage of rice. Moreover, at that
period the Vietnamese had occupied almost the whole of Cambodia and they had no
reason to engage in a Thai war unless the Thai should attempt to replace them as protec-
tors of the Khmer Kingdom. But the basic reason for the mission's failure was probably
the unprecedented nature of the proposal which it brought. It is evident that the Viet-
namese knew little about Burma and her military potential, and therefore they were
necessarily cautious and reserved in their attitude. Only a prolonged series of diplomatic
exchanges could have brought about an alliance between two countries which were so far

the Anglo-Burmese war prevented the Vietnamese emissaries from fulfilling their

pressure to be in a position to trouble the Thai. Thus policy towards Thailand, which
was the only factor tending to bring Burma and Vietnam together, ceased on both sides
to be the major interest, and, for lack of common ground, the diplomatic intercourse of
the years 1823 and 1824 was not followed up. The mission from Burma to Vietnam
thus remains an isolated incident, interesting in itself but not of great consequence to
the peoples of either country.

We left Rangoon in the beginning of January 1823, in an European-built vessel,
and on the 26th February reached Penang, after touching on our way at Tavoy.
While lying at Penang, on the 24th March, a Siamese junk, on fire, ran foul of the
Mission ship, and burnt her. There was hardly time to save the dispatches, jewels, and
other presents for the Court of Cochin-China.
Having made application to the Governor of Prince of Wales's Island, a loan of
4000 dollars was given to me for a bill on the Myowun of Rangoon. The Mission then
took a passage in a Portuguese ship.
It finally sailed, on the 22nd of April, from Prince of Wales's Island, reached
Malacca on the 2nd of May, and Singapore on the 12th. On the 18th we left this last place,
and on the 1st of June reached the anchorage of Vungtao, or that of Cape St. James in
Kamboja.
On the 3rd the Mission reached the village of Canju. Four barges of ceremony

Feb. 18.-This was the seventeenth day of the moon, and the termination of the holidays.
A salute of three guns was fired from the rampart of the fort, which was followed by a
discharge of muskets and crackers from the houses of the town. The whole troops were
drawn out, and, with drums beating and colours flying, they marched with much ceremony
round the glacis of the fort. After this they proceeded to the river-side, where three
galleys were lying prepared, from which salutes were fired, and returned with a discharge
of muskets as before. The galleys then sailed about the river in procession, accompanied
by a great number of small boats, ornamented with little flags, banners, lanterns and
spears.
About seven o'clock in the morning, a royal order from the King was conveyed
from the house of Ong-Tan-Hiep to the Governor's in the fort with much ceremony, on
a gilt stage; six elephants followed in procession, and many of the principal Mandarins
attended. The new Governor appeared in a splendid military dress, having the emblem
of a lion on his robe. As far as concerned the interests of the Burman Mission, we
learned that three Mandarins and a Secretary, with seventy persons as an escort, were
directed to accompany us back to Ava. The names of these Mandarins were, Ong-Kin,
Ong-Kian, Bie Voung, and the Secretary Ong-Tri-Bohé. Ong-Kin was by descent a
Chinese; his father was the chief of a gang of Chinese pirates who assisted the late King
in reconquering his country. He entered into the King's service at Pulo Condore, and
was created commodore of the pirate fleet, which he had brought from the coast of China.
These people, when the war was over, received a settlement on the left bank of the river,
where they or their descendants still exist to the number of three or four hundred,
receiving regular pay and rations from the Government, and being ready for service when
called upon.
Feb. 19.-To-day we paid a visit to the Governor, and were informed that a vessel was
preparing to convey us back to our native country.
Feb. 22. We received accounts, that the persons constituting the deputation which was
to return with us had received an acccession of rank, and that a person of superior dignity
was expected from the capital with a letter and presents to his Burman Majesty.
Feb. 25. A fire broke out to-day in the market place, close to the Secretary's dwelling;
the Governor-General himself appeared on the spot to assist in extinguishing it. In
consequence of the exertions that were made, and the vicinity of the place to the river,
two houses only were destroyed.
Feb. 26.-Coe-Doe-Lam arrived from the capital, and by him the Mission had certain
intelligence that the Governor-General would not return to the southern provinces before
the month of May, and until he had assisted in the celebration of the nuptials of his
nephew Cadoa with the sister of his Majesty and daughter of the late King. He stated
that his Excellency, on his arrival, had recommended the opening of the public granaries;
and that rice, in consequence, had fallen in price to half a quan a basket. The scarcity
had occasioned a revolt in Tonquin, and the rebels there would not lay down their arms
until they had a personal conference with the Governor.

Feb. 27. Another fire broke out close to the house occupied by the Mission, which by
great activity was soon extinguished.
Feb. 28.-Monsieur Diard arrived from the capital, and the members of the Mission
were informed that the presents for his Burman Majesty were coming overland. Monsieur
Diard was appointed by the Cochin Chinese Court to accompany the Burman Mission,
and showed the Ambassadors the mandate of the King to that effect, under the seal of the
Mandarin of Strangers. He also stated that he had been expressly called to Court, and
he related the whole circumstances of the transaction respecting our Mission, as it had
taken place. He stated that the Mandarin of Strangers had made a speech in the Great
Council against the Burman alliance, asserting that it would alarm the Siamese, and make
an unfavourable change in their sentiments towards the Cochin Chinese nation. The
King demanded to know whether his counsellors were afraid to enter into this new
alliance; and observed, that one thing was quite certain, - that the Burmans were the
avowed enemies of the Siamese. His Excellency the Governor-General Tai-Kun and the
two French Mandarins, Vanier and Cheneaux, spoke in favour of the Burman alliance.
They said, that the Burmans were the inveterate enemies of the Siamese, and that through
them the Cochin Chinese might again become possessed of the fruitful Kambojan province
of Bantaibang, and so a free commerce, favourable to both sides, might be established.
The results of this debate were adverse to a connexion with the Burmese, although I am
unable to explain the cause of so unfavourable a determination in the mind of the Cochin
Chinese King. Upon the whole, however, I am inclined to ascribe this conduct to the
extravagant conceit of the Cochin Chinese nation; who firmly believe themselves, and the
Chinese from whom they are descended, to be the only civilised people in the world,
and all other nations savage and barbarous. As to the Siamese, the King of
Cochin China thinks he could conquer them in an instant if he desired it. There is not
a person of sense about the Court or Government except the Governor-General Tai-Kun,
who often smiles at the absurdities of the rest, and has even hinted to the King the extra-
vagance of his pretensions, - since he is, in fact, no more than a tributary of the Emperor
of China.
March 4.-The war-junk intended to convey us to our own country was this day
launched.
March 6.-The Mission received to-day five hundred and sixteen quans, and one hundred
and forty-one baskets of rice, reckoned to be three months' stock of provisions for the
voyage. We were requested to repair next morning to the fort, for the purpose of viewing
the letter and presents for his Majesty of Ava.
March 7.-At daylight the members of the Mission repaired to the fort on foot, ushered
by a Mandarin of the civil order. We missed upon this occasion our old guardian,
Ong-Bo; and making inquiry, found he had been dismissed from his office. In the front
part of the hall of audience, we found the letter to the King of Ava, laid out on a table,
and the presents arranged to the left of it. Within the hall, four tapers were burning.

The Mandarins of the military order were in one line on the right hand, and the civilians
in another on the left. All were standing up, and in their dresses of ceremony. Shortly
after, the Governor made his appearance, and took his place on the right-hand side; next
to him stood an elderly Mandarin, said to be the General-in-Chief of the Army of Lower
Cochin China; the rest followed according to their ranks. At the head of the left or
civil side was Ong-bo-baing, the Treasurer; Ong-kim, the First Judge, came next; then
the comptroller. The Secretary was only fourth in rank.
The music began to play, and heralds on both sides having given the signal, the
whole of the Mandarins advanced to the centre of the hall, and made five prostrations to
the throne, as if the King had been sitting on it. The Mandarins who were to accompany
the Burman Mission then made their prostrations. The members of the Burman Mission
were then ordered to advance and bow five times in a similar manner, which they did.
The King's orders respecting the Mission were then explained to them, the presents to his
Burman Majesty were enumerated, and the gifts conferred upon each member of the
Mission, individually, were stated. After this ceremony, we were conducted to the house
of the Governor, where we had a long conference respecting the affairs of the Mission,
and particularly concerning what related to our return. We now retired, accompanied by
the King's letter and presents, which were conveyed with much state, and deposited in the
hall of the house where we resided. The gifts intended for the individuals of the Mission
were then distributed according to their ranks.
March 10.-To-day we proceeded to the fort and delivered the presents of his Burman
Majesty. The Mandarins received them with great respect, in a standing posture.
March 12. The members of the Burman Mission, according to custom, repaired this
day to the palace to return thanks for the gifts which the King had condescended to
confer upon
them. We appeared, on this occasion, in dresses bestowed upon us by his
Cochin Chinese Majesty, and the courtiers and ourselves performed the same ceremonies
and prostrations as on the 7th. After this was over, the Governor entertained us at his
house, and amused us with Cochin Chinese plays. We then finally took leave.
April 1.-On the 12th and 13th of March the presents and baggage were out on board.
On the night of the 13th and the morning of the 14th two fires broke put. In the evening
of the last day we embarked, and dropped down the river with the ebb-tide.
On the 15th and 16th the crew were engaged in cutting firewood for the voyage.
On the 17th we reached the village of Kanju. On the 18th and 19th the crew were
occupied in watering. On the 20th we again dropped down. On the 21st we anchored
off Kauro. Here we continued till the 24th; the Cochin Chinese insisting, that although
the wind was fair, the period was, according to their astrology, unlucky. On this last day
a foul wind came on, and at night we weighed with the land breeze, but anchored again
off Cape St James; the Cochin Chinese officers alleging that it was necessary to make a
report of their progress to the Government. On the 26th, a dispatch-boat arrived from
Saigun to know what had become of us. While we lay here, three junks for Singapore
