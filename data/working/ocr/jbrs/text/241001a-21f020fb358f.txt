THE SHWE DAGON PAGODA.
CHAPTER I Evidence of the Pali.
Part 1
The story of the Shwe Dagon Pagoda, unlike most legends connec-
ted with pagodas, can be connected with evidence adduced from books
compiled outside of Burma. For Taphussa and Bhallika, the reputed
founders of the Pagoda are mentioned in more than one book of the
Pali scriptures, which were compiled in India. Thus in Buddhavamsa",
a book of the Khuddaka Nikaya, the 5th division of the Sutta Pitaka, it
is said that "Taphuşsa and Bhallika were the chief attendants of the
teacher Dipankara."
In the Anguttara Nikaya of the Sutta Pitaka occurs the passage:
"Monks, supreme among my devout disciples who first took the refuge
are the merchants Tapussa and Bhallika.
11
In a later portion of the same book we read "Monks, Tapussa
the householder, having attained to perfection in the Tathāgata and beheld
deathlessness and realized deathlessness walks about endowed with
six qualities. Which are the six ?-absolute faith in the Buddha, ab-
solute faith in the Law, absolute faith in the clergy, noble virtue, noble
knowledge, noble emancipation. Endowed with these six qualities,
O monks, Tapussa the householder walks about, having attained to
perfection in the Tathagata and beheld deathlessness and realized death-
lessness." The same is repeated of Bhallika.
And a discoursed of the same book tells us that while the Buddha
was dwelling with the Mallas "Tapussa the householder approached the
Venerable Ananda and having approached him and saluted him said
thus Reverend Ananda, we householders are partakers of sensual
pleasure, enjoy sensual pleasure, delight in sensual pleasure, revel in
sensual pleasure. And to us householders who partake of sensual pleasure,
enjoy sensual pleasure, delight in sensual pleasure, revel in sensual
pleasure, renunciation appears like a precipice. But I have heard that in
this discipline of the Law the mind of many a young monk aspires to,
settles on, rests on, attains freedom in emancipation regarding it as
peace. Such, Sir, is the difference regarding emancipation between the
young monks of the discipline of the Law and the mass of the people"".
This outburst of Tapussa's forms the basis of a sermon by the Buddha.
A verse sung by Bhalliya (= Bhallika) is included in the Theragathae,
another book of the Sutta Pitaka:
"Whoso hath chased away the death-king and his host,
E'en as a mighty flood the causeway of frail reeds,
Victor is he, selftamed. Fear cometh never more.
His is the goal supreme and utter steadfastness."

How Taphussa and Bhallika became disciples of Gotama the Buddha
is told in the Rajayatanakatha of the Mahavagga of the Vinaya Pitaka":
"Then the Blessed One, at the end of those seven days, arose from that
concentration of mind and went from the foot of the Mucalinda tree to
the Rājāyatana tree. Having reached it, he sat down cross-legged at the
foot of the Rajayatana tree uninterruptedly for seven days enjoying the
bliss of emancipation. At that time the merchants Tapussa and
Bhallika were travelling on the road from Ukkala to that place. Then a
deity, who had been a blood-relation of the merchants Tapussa and
Bhallika spoke to them, 'Sirs, at the foot of the Rājāyatana tree dwells
the Blessed One who has just become the Enlightened Buddha. Go and
wait on him with rice-cakes and honey-food. This will be to your
advantage and happiness for a long time to come'. Then the merchants
Tapussa and Bhallika took rice-cakes and honey-food and went to where
the Blessed One was. Having approached him and saluted him they
stood on one side. Having stood on one side, the merchants Tapussa
and Bhallika addressed the Blessed One, 'Lord, may the Blessed One
accept from us these rice-cakes and honey-food that it may be to our ad-
vantage and happiness for a long time to come.' Then the Blessed One
thought "The Tathāgatas do not accept (food) with their hands. Now
with what shall I accept the rice-cakes and honey food?' Then the four
Mahārājā Gods knowing in their minds what had occurred to the Blessed
One offered him four stone bowls from the four quarters of the globe
saying Lord, may the Blessed One accept with these the rice-cakes and
honey-food.' And the Blessed One accepted the newly created stone
bowls and received the rice cakes and honey-food and these he ate.
Then the merchants Tapussa and Bhallika, when they knew that the
Blessed One had removed his hand from the bowl, bowed down with
their heads at the feet of the Blessed One and addressed him Lord, we
take our refuge in the Blessed One and in the Law. May the Blessed
One receive us as disciples who, from this day forth, have taken refuge as
long as our life lasts'. They were the first in the world to become
disciples by the twofold formula."
So far there is nothing in this Mahivagga account or the other
Pali extracts, which we can connect with the Shwe Dagon Pagoda.

It is not until we come to the Pali commentaries written some
eight hundred years later in the fifth century A.D. that we get some
light thrown on the subject. In his commentary on the Mahavagga
passage Buddhaghosa, while merely describing Ukkala as the district
(janapada) of Ukkala, tells us "that the two merchants Taphussa and
Bhallika were brothers.... that the deity (who had been their mother)
made all the carts of the merchants unable to move, so that they saying
' what is the matter' made offerings to the spirits of the road and the
deity revealed herself at the time when they made the offering....that
the bowl which the Buddha had at the time of his exertion disappeared
when Sujata came to offer milk-rice Hence the thought 'I have no
bowl. The Tathāgatas of the past did not accept food with their hands
etc.'....that hitherto the food offered by Sujātā had served the Blessed
One as a continuity of nutritive essence, so that during all that time he did
not experience hunger or thirst or physical weakness. But now out of a
desire to accept food, he thought 'The Tathāgatas do not accept etc.'....
that the four Mahārāja Gods first offered sapphire bowls, which the
Blessed One refused. Then they offered these four stone bowls of the
colour of beans, which the Blessed One accepted in order to preserve
their faith, not out of greediness. And having accepted them he resolved
that the four bowls would become one and that the four gods would
enjoy equal merit. Thus he accepted the bowls after resolving them
into one bowl....that after informing him of their discipleship, the
merchants said to the Blessed One 'Lord, from today forth whom shall
we salute and revere ?' Then the Blessed One stroked his head and
hairs clung to his hand. These he gave them saying 'keep them.' Having
got the hair-relics they were pleased and delighted as though they were
anointed with ambrosia and having saluted the Blessed One went away.'

A third Pali commentator, Buddhadatta, who also wrote about the
same time as Buddhaghosa, refers to the story in his commentary on
the Buddhavamsa: "And so 7 weeks were completed. During those days
the Blessed One spent his time in the bliss of rapt meditation and of
its fruition and at the expiration of the 7 weeks he felt a desire to wash
his face. Then Sakka, King of the Gods, brought and gave him a fruit
of the Myrobalan tree, as well as a tooth-cleanser made of the snake
creeper and water to wash the face. Then the Blessed One chewed the
tooth-cleanser, washed his face with water from the Anotatta Lake and sat
down at the foot of the Rājāyatana tree. At that time he accepted the
rice-cakes and honey-food of the merchants Taphussa and Bhallika in
the newly-created stone bowl offered by the 4 Regents of the world and
having eaten them came back again and sat down at the foot of the
Ajapāla Banyan tree.”
$3
Thus we find from the Pali commentaries quoted above that all the
three commentators Buddhaghosa, Buddhadatta and Dhammapāla who
wrote more or less at the same time in the 5th century A. D. give docu-
mentary evidence of the interview of the merchants with the Buddha, that
Buddhaghosa and Dhammapala mention the gift of hairs to them by the
Buddha, and further that while Dhammapala merely says that they were
born at Pokkharavati it is Buddhaghosa who definitely gives us the
important information that the two merchants deposited the hair-relics
in a shrine at the gate of Asitañjana, their native town.
The story of the two merchants is mentioned in still other and later
Pali works which however do not give any additional information. Thus
the Nidanakatha of the Jātaka commentary, which is generally ascribed
to an unknown author later than Buddhaghosa gives an account which
looks like an expansion of that given above by Buddhadatta in his com-
mentary on the Buddhavamsa:
'And so seven weeks were completed. During those days there
was no washing of the face, nor caring for the body, nor partaking of food,
but he spent his time in the bliss of rapt meditation, of the path and of
its fruition. Then as he sat there on the 49th day at the end of the 7
weeks he felt a desire to wash his face. And Sakka, King of the Gods,
brought and gave him a fruit of the Myrobalan tree. The Teacher ate it
and felt comfortable. Then Sakka gave him a tooth cleanser made of the
snake-creeper and water to wash the face. The Master chewed the tooth-
cleanser, washed his face with water from the Anotatta Lake and sat
down at the foot of the Rājāyatana tree. At that time the two merchants,

stopped their carts and moved their hearts to offer food to the Master.

To sum up: The Pali books of the Canon tell us that the two bro-
thers were the first disciples of the Buddha. The Mahavagga of the
Vinaya Pitaka, the only Canonical book which describes their inter-
view with the Buddha, mentions them as travelling from Ukkala, presum-
ably their native country, but says nothing of their receiving the hairs
from the Buddha. We find the first mention of the gift of hairs in the
commentaries of the 5th century A.D. by Buddhaghosa and Dhamma-
pala, who call the brothers' native town Asitanjana or Pokkharavati.
Buddhaghosa is the first to tell us that the brothers deposited the hairs
in a shrine at the gate of Asitañjana, their native town. He gives no
hint that he is thinking of Lower Burma. The story of the two bro-
thers as told by Buddhaghosa is re-told by later books without addi-
tional information, if in greater detail.

CHAPTER II.—Evidence of the Inscriptions.
Forchhammer in his Notes on the Early History and Geography of
British Purma, 1883, has given us a description of the inscriptions. (p.6).
"On the Eastern slope of the hill upon which the Shwe Dagon Pagoda
stands, and about 50 feet below the present platform, are three large
stone inscriptions. Three years ago they were buried under ground:
by removing some shrubs and bushes I discovered the tops of these
stones just peeping an inch or two out of the ground; they stand upright
and about 2 feet apart. I had the earth removed all around and between
the inscriptions, and at a depth of about 12 feet I reached the sockets of the
stones embedded in a layer of bricks, which presented a smooth surface
to the full extent of the ground exposed, namely, an area of 15 feet by
8 feet the earth removed consisted of pieces of old bricks, gravel, clay,
and fragments of pottery; it nowhere exhibited traces of geological forma-
tion.....The inscriptions were engraved by order of the Talaing King
Dhammaceti in the year of the Burmese era 847 (A.D. 1485).... Though
the pagoda is said to have been founded before Gotama had reached Pari-
nirvana, yet these inscriptions are the most ancient remains that have
as yet been discovered on or about this sanctuary. Like all Talaing ins-
criptions, they face the north and stand on what I believe to be the ancient
original platform of the stupa, visited and repaired by Dhammaceti....
The stone slabs measure 12 feet above the socket, 3 feet 10 inches in
width and 10 inches in thickness; both sides are covered with inscrip-
tions in Talaing with a Burmese translation so arranged that the side of
the stones facing the north contains the Talaing text and the side facing
the south the Burmese nissaya. Large pieces of the stones have been
broken off and can not be found; the inscriptions are therefore incom-
plete; the letters are square (but not the "Square Pali" form) and each
occupies the space of half an inch; each line contains from 70 to 80
letters and each side from 36 to 45 lines. " ]
The three stones stand on the eastern slope of the Pagoda in a row
from north to south, with the writing on their north and south faces.

Forchhammer's measurements are not correct. Thus the stones are not
about 2 feet apart. Starting from the north and naming the stones
North, Middle and South, we find that the North and Middle stones are
4 feet 1 inch apart. the Middle and South Stones are 4 feet apart. And
they are not 10 inches in thickness-the North stone is 1 foot 8 inches,
the Middle stone 1 foot 3 inches, the South stone 1 foot 1½ inches thick.
Neither are they 3 feet 10 inches in width-the North stone is 4 feet,
the Middle stone 4 feet 4 inches, the South stone 4 feet wide. The
layer of brick in which they are embedded is a pit of brickwork but it
has not an area of 15 feet by 8 feet. It extends 15 feet 10 inches from
north to south, 9 feet 9 inches from east to west. And the slope being
from west to east, the depth of the pit is 8 feet 4 inches on the west
side, 6 feet 3 inches on the north and south sides, and 5 feet 6 inches
on the east side. But the most glaring mistake lies in the statement that
"both sides are covered with inscriptions in Talaing with a Burmese
translation so arranged that the side of the stones facing the north con-
tains the Talaing text and the side facing the south the Burmese nissāya."
Actually, both the north and south faces of the North stone contain
the Pali text, both the north and south faces of the Middle stone contain
the Talaing text, both the north and south faces of the South stone con-
tain the Burmese text. And of each of the stones the north face is the
obverse and the south face the reverse..
The North stone containing the Pali text is so much defaced that
beyond the words Kakavan natissa in line 6 and Bhalliyathero in line 10
of the reverse face, nothing of sense can be deciphered. These two
words and the correspondence between the contents of the Talaing and
the Burmese texts leave no doubt that the Pali text also dealt with the
same subject. Nearly a quarter of the stone in the upper part on the east
side has been broken off.

Dr. C. O. Blagden, who is publishing it (No. XV) along with other Mon
