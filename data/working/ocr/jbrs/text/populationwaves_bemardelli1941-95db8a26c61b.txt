1. In thinking of population growth usually the notion is applied
consciously or unconsciously — that such growth takes place along a smooth
curve: either in the form of MALTHUS' geometric progression, the
interest law, or according to VERHULST-PEARL'S logistic equation, or
along such curves as have been worked out recently by Dr. KUCZYNSKI
and his collaborators in order to trace the decline of the European race.
Practical statisticians, no doubt, realise that populations actually show con-
siderable deviations from such models, but they have tended on the whole
to consider such "irregularities" as "random variations" which are best
smoothed out.
Having worked on a variety of problems which all involve assumptions
about the manner in which the numbers of a population change, it appears
to me now rather doubtful that the preference given to smooth curves is
justified even as a first approximation. Smoothing always means introduc-
ing a definite hypothesis about the nature of the causes that determine the
value of the variable under consideration; in eliminating the irregular oscil-
lations typical for a population graph, we assume, in fact, that there are
two sets of causes operating: a fundamental, basic set of causes which, left
alone, would produce the picture of our smooth curve, and a second set -
more irregular and haphazard in occurrence and intensity -- which accounts
for the superimposed aberrations.
The justification of the procedure of smoothing thus depends essentially
on the validity of the hypothesis that the basic causes will manifest them-
selves smoothly. If it can be shown that this is not the case, that the basic
causes themselves are bound, in fact, to generate oscillations, then the
operation of smoothing will lose its rationale. Its application will merely
blur the picture, and possibly even block the way for a correct understanding
of the causal effects which we wish to elucidate.
2. The oscillations of which I am talking can easily be demonstrated
in any Census Report. The following table, taken from the Burma Census
1931, gives the age-distribution of 10,000 persons of each sex for the
indigenous population of this country ¹. The figures have been compiled
for the indigenous inhabitants only, because they reveal best the pure effects
of population growth, and are not, in any way, affected by migration.

As migrants all over the world, the Indians follow the lure of prosperity:

Indian labourers come in large numbers whenever they are wanted, attracted

a somewhat slow process which extended over years. Many Indians, we
may presume, who had come on advances before 1929 found it difficult to

from 1929 to 1932, a distinct lag can be observed between the population
figures and the values of trade, a lag which indicates the degree to which

I. For any given set of fertility and mortality conditions there exists
always one characteristic age distribution (1000:500:167 in the above
example) which does not give rise to disequilibrium waves; but whenever
the population deviates from this equilibrium structure, waves will be set up
with amplitudes that depend entirely on the degree of these deviations.
II. It is not necessary that the population should be stationary, as in
the above example; waves will also occur, if the Net Reproduction Rate
R> 1, indicating a growing population, or if R < 1, so that the population
eventually dies out.

4. The assertion that a population may oscillate even under absolutely
constant conditions will encounter, no doubt, a good deal of mental inertia.
There is in our mind a strong tendency - one might call it a natural preju-
dice to associate stable conditions with stable effects. Thus Prof.
PEARL was very surprised when he discovered in breeding experiments
with the fruit fly Drosophila that, even while keeping "the food conditions
always optimal both as to quantity and quality", he obtained nevertheless
the striking result.... that, during both the growth period, and thereafter,
there (were) violent oscillations of the population in size. . . In fact these
waves "- he reports¹, “are perhaps the most characteristic feature of popu-
lation experiments of this particular type. It has not so far been possible to
devise any method of holding these populations in a steady state at the level
of the asymptote, when there is at all times an abundance of fresh food. The
population simply waves up and down about an average size ". 2
"
,,
However, the feeling of surprise will quickly vanish, once one has got
a clear grasp of the idea that a population system constitutes a kind of a
natural pendulum. Readers will find the analogy most helpful: a physical
pendulum also has one characteristic equilibrium position (when it is verti-
cally hanging downwards) in which it does not produce any oscillations;
but when it is disturbed in its position, it will begin producing waves pro-
portionate to the degree of the disturbance.

A case in point is the celebrated pig-cycle of the Economists. This
curse of the pig industry" has caused a great number of inquiries, and
even Royal Commissions have sat on pigs in order to suggest remedies 1.
The explanations given, however, do not seem to be satisfactory. Attempts
to dissolve the variations of the pig population into regular harmonics have
failed 2, and explanations in terms of a simple lag in the adjustment of
farmers to changing prices have not been satisfactory either ³. More
complicated "epi-cyclic" models have to resort to imputing into farmers
an alertness and reagibility to prices, and a tendency for making speculative
errors, which exceeds by far the nervous mentality of excitable stock brokers.
Such theories, it would appear, rather exaggerate the influence of prices on
human behaviour.
The farmers decisions, we may presume, are very little concerned with
the pig population as a whole: what interests him is merely the number of
pigs which he is going to keep, and the date suitable for their slaughter.
With considerable inertia he will stick to the practice of his forefathers,
and only from time to time will he introduce innovations, and make altera-
tions of his plans, if market conditions have taken an entirely unexpected
turn. Thus the pig is left sufficient latitude to do what it has to do by law
of Nature: namely to react with most violent oscillations to the artificial
disequilibrium conditions under which it is kept. It is not the farmer who
is primary responsible for these waves, his contribution would seem to consist
rather in making these waves more erratic, and more irregular than they
would occur with pigs in the wild state.

Although the fundamental idea of these theories is prima facie plausible,
they had to be abandoned for lack of empirical evidence. Moreover we
know to-day that the economic system itself is in the nature of a pendulum,
and that it would endogenously react in an oscillatory fashion, even if all

have learned to consider the world as a continuous flow of goods and
services, as a collection of resources which behave somewhat in the way of
heavenly bodies, continuously emitting and absorbing light. All these re-
sources have to be maintained, they absorb a certain input at every unit
period which is the absolute condition of their continuing to radiate another
stream of services, their output. Now the amount of services which can
be reproduced during a small time-interval dt is obviously dependent on the
pre-existing resources as well as on the length dt of the time, during which
these resources were left to operate. Analysing this relationship a bit more
closely which easily can be done by means of the so-called Marginal Pro-
ductivity Theory - one is led to a linear system of differential equations,
very much similar to those given here for the reproduction of a population.
It is thus seen that production even in its most primitive form, disregarding
all monetary influences, constitutes already a vibrator system which, owing
to the inertia of human relations, can give rise to oscillations. * (In reality
this matter is unfortunately enormously complicated by the interference of
monetary factors). But though it has thus become evident that a satisfactory
explanation of trade-cycles must account for the endogenous reactions of the
economic system, this does not mean that the exogenous attempts were utterly
unsound. It is perfectly compatible with an endogenous model that some of
the external factors, viz. the population, vary themselves quasi-cyclically.

There are, on the other hand, in the economic development of the last
150 years distinct periods of 25-30 years duration, known as the long
waves," which display a well defined regularity to which attention has only
comparatively recently been directed.³ Phases whose general course reveals
an upward trend seem to be regularly succeeded by periods whose economic
data prove that the general tendency has been downward. Apart from
