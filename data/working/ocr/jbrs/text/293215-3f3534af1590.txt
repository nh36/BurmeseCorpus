DENMARK'S INTEREST IN BURMA AND THE
NICOBAR ISLANDS.
Spanish and Portuguese exploits in the Orient immediately
following Vasco de Gama's discovery of the all-water route to the
Indies are well known. Common knowledge also are the activities
of the quasi-governmental East India Companies of Britain and
Holland. Indeed, the activities of these great powers have over-
shadowed completely the attempts made by several minor powers
to gain a share in the fertile field of Oriental trade. It is the purpose
of this paper to collect certain facts relative to Denmark's commer-
cial and colonial interests in Burma and the Nicobar Islands.1
Repercussions following the granting of the charter to the British
East India Company in 1601 soon were evident throughout western
Europe. The next year saw the formation of the Dutch East India
Company. French merchants, determined to reap a share in the
profits of Oriental trade, organized the French East India Company
in 1604, but no French ships were dispatched until 1611 and France
acquired no eastern colonies until the middle of the century.
Denmark took advantage of her unique geographical position
for foreign trade by organizing the Danish East India Company in
1616.2 At the beginning of the seventeenth century that stout
little country, having the oldest monarchy and the oldest flag in
Europe, was in possession of considerable naval and military power
and vastly more territory than that over which she now is sovereign.
It was not until 1620 that Danish expansion brought her into
contact with countries in proximity to Burma. Christian IV, King
of Denmark from 1588 to 1648, was a ruler of unusual vigour, and his
personal interest in foreign lands was reflected in Danish expansion
in Greenland, the Virgin Islands, in the African slave trade, in India
and other sections of southern and eastern Asia. His Majesty granted
the Danish East India Company the exclusive right to trade between
Denmark and the Orient for twelve years. His son was placed at
the head of the venture which was organized along the lines of the
Dutch East India Company.3

Because of their experience in the East, Dutch merchants and
skippers were taken into the Danish service 4. A leading Dutch
merchant, Marselio de Boshouer, was received by Christian IV at
his castle in Fredericksborg in November, 1617, at which time he
received a commission to visit Ceylon and conclude a treaty of trade
and alliance between that country and Denmark. Under the treaty
signed subsequently Denmark undertook to maintain a garrison of
300 fully armed Danish soldiers in Ceylon.
Having sailed from Copenhagen on March 30, 1618, de Bos-
houer's expedition met an unhappy end; its leader died at sea while
en route to Ceylon. The four ships carrying the troops sighted
land at Karikal, then in Portuguese hands. Only forty Danes sur-
vived the perils of the sea and the battle with the Portuguese, but
these survivors were given, by the Rajah of Tanjour, a small parcel
of land at Tranquebar as a refuge. From this modest beginning
sprang the Danish colonies in Asia. Tranquebar was ceded to
Denmark by treaty in 1620 and remained in Danish hands, with
intervals of British control, until sold to Britain in 1845.5 Other
Danes coming out East made a treaty with the Sultan of Socotra
and established a trading post at Mocha. The King of Ceylon
ceded the port of Trincomalee to Denmark upon assurance that the
Danes would construct there a fort against the Portuguese. An
extensive commerce sprang up between that port and Copenhagen,
and soon the Danish flag was seen flying in the Muluccas, Kedah,
Macassar, the Persian Gulf, Atchin, Manila, and China.
Despite these auspicious beginnings the Danish record in the
East is not a happy one. There were never many Danes in the
East, and it was never easy to recruit men in Copenhagen for service
in the tropics. The Danish colonies were not profitable as a state
enterprise; during the 18th century the India establishments returned
a constant deficit. It is believed that some Danish merchants and
investors made a profit from the India trade, but the point is an
obscure one. At any rate, the sale of Denmark's holdings in India
for the sum of 1,250,000 Rix-dollars (approximately Rupees 15 lakhs)
was considered good business.

While it is true that Danish interest in Burma proper was only
transitory, that country did make persistent efforts to establish colonies
in the Nicobar Islands. It may be said with some reason that the
Nicobars never have been, and are not today, within the sphere of
Burmese affairs. Yet there has been for centuries much traffic
between the islands and the Martaban and Tenasserim coasts. Burma
always has shared the Nicobar trade with Penang and with Atchin
(Kota Radja) on the northern tip of Sumatra. In the harbour of
Moulmein are anchored a number of picturesque old sailing vessels,
some of which are the last surviving patriarchs of the Nicobar trade.
Marco Polo's brief and erroneous mention of the Nicobars
introduced them to Europeans. 13 Portuguese and Dutch ships
made occasional stops in the islands for wood and water during the
era of exploration. The first Englishman known to have visited the
Nicobars for any length of time was the navigator and gentleman
pirate, William Dampier, who according to his own account and at
his own request was put ashore with two other Englishmen, a Portu-
guese, and several Malays after having captured a Danish ship engaged
in the Guinea trade. Dampier's purpose was to search for ambergris.
The deadly Nicobar fever, of which the later Danish colonists made
such frequent mention, was fatal to several of Dampier's companions.
He escaped with a few survivors who managed to navigate a canoe
to Atchin.
Ships of the Danish East India Company began taking an in-
terest in the Nicobars after the visit to the islands of the Dansborg
in 1709. Country boats from Tranquebar made frequent trips to
the islands for cargoes of coconuts, betel nuts and timber. The
Tranquebar Government sent the Grev Laurvig to spend the winter
of 1723 in the Nicobars, where a profitable cargo of the usual pro-
ducts was taken aboard. East India Company ships from the Syriam
factory and from the Negrais colony made frequent visits to the
Nicobars, the islands having been first surveyed by Capt. Weldon
in 1687.

Denmark decided upon an expansion policy in its Indian pos-
sessions following the arrival in Tranquebar of the men-of-war
Bornholm and Nellebladet in 1752. The vessels brought a considerable
number of men and arms, and additional cash for extension in Malabar,
Bengal, and the Nicobars. Two years later Governor Krog was
ordered to equip an expedition for the purpose of annexing one or
more of the larger Nicobar islands where colonies were to be est-
ablished. In pursuance of these orders, September 8, 1755 saw a
party of eighty-three men including engineers, artillerymen, fifty-five
Danish soldiers and a number of sepoys embark on the Danish East
India Company ships King of Denmark and Ebenezer. This first
expedition, finding the winds contrary, sailed on to Atchin, and it
was not until the 12th November that they anchored in a bay on the
northern side of Great Nicobar a natural harbour which the Com-
mander, Lieutenant Tanck, declared as "safe as the Copenhagen
canal."
Construction of a residence for the officers and quarters for the
troops was begun immediately. By the end of December eight
cannon were placed behind stockades which had been constructed
as harbour defence works. The new colony was formally dedicated
and annexed to Denmark on New Year's Day, 1756. On the 15th
January, Tanck completed a detailed report on the condition of New
Denmark, and three days later two ships returned to Tranquebar.
Although the colony was established during the fair season the in-
famous Nicobar fever began to take its toll of the new residents.
When the East India Company ship Sea Horse (remembered for
connection with the Negrais colony) completed a reconnaissance of
the islands in March, 1756, its officers reported only four of the
colonists in normal health.
The Nicobar colony suffered from the first because of a division
of authority, the Danish Government being represented by Baron
Tanner and the East India Company by Lieutenant Tanck. The
precise authority of each officer and his relation to the other is some-
what obscure. At any rate, there was considerable friction and
when Baron Tanner withdrew his men to Tranquebar an improve-
ment was seen. The ship Ebenezer meanwhile made another trip
to Atchin and later returned to Tranquebar.
Just as the Ebenezer was ready to sail from Tranquebar with
reinforcements, the Sea Horse arrived in that port with adverse news
from the Nicobars. An auxiliary expedition was sent out at once
with instructions to Lieutenant Tanck, who was already dead, to
transfer the colony to Nancowry harbour, at the same time giving
permission to abandon the colony if that course seemed advisable.
When Lieutenant Faye arrived with a relief expedition of forty
men and two women on the Ebenezer he found barely one-third of

The Volquartz expedition, which consisted of sixty-three men
including the Danish missionary Poltzenhagen, found alive only
thirty-five men out of 125 that Denmark had sent to the Nicobars
within six months. Consequently it was decided at once to shift
the colony to Nancowry harbour. However, upon arrival at Nan-
cowry, the discovery that about ten years previously the French
East India Company had maintained a settlement at Nancowry led
Volquartz to move to Kamorte lest troublesome questions of
sovereignty might arise. The Danes found favourable trading
conditions at Kamorte, but the fever was just as virulent as on the
southern island. The Ebenezer was dispatched to Atchin for medi-
cines and fresh European stores while Volquartz's ship the Copenhagen
returned to Tranquebar for a load of 40,000 brick and 20,000 roof
tiles for the Nicobars and Atchin.
By raising the Danish flag to a salute of cannon the Kamorte
colony was christened New Seeland on October 18, 1756, nine months
after the first colony was established at Great Nicobar. Health
conditions grew no better; ten of the new colonists died in October;
thirteen in November. By the end of December, one year after the
Danes reached the Nicobars in force, only twenty-four out of 188
men remained alive.
Volquartz having died, a new commander arrived in June, 1757,
with reinforcements. He removed the colony, together with the
remains of the New Denmark colony, to Nancowry harbour. The
new commander, Jens Tweed, died the 29th of June. Dangers
thickened about the new colonists. Just as the Ebenezer was ready
to sail for Atchin in August, 1757, some 400 Nicobarese armed with
spears threatened an attack. Most of the Danes avoided the danger
by boarding the Ebenezer which took them to Sumatra, leaving one
sergeant and five soldiers to guard the Company's property. In
order to escape from their misery two men jumped overboard on the
journey; two deserted, and three died in Atchin. On the return
trip the vessel was wrecked on Great Nicobar. Lund, in charge of
the remnants of the colony, lost all control over the survivors most of
whom perished in the jungles. A few managed to escape to Atchin
in country boats. Among 300 men sent to the Nicobars within
eighteen months Lund was, in addition to Baron Tanner and six
soldiers, the sole survivor to reach Tranquebar, and he came in an
English ship by way of Malacca.
Thus the first Danish Nicobar enterprise ended in disasters,
most of which may be attributed to malaria, some to inadequate

During the ten years 1758-1768 none in Tranquebar had the
courage to attempt another Nicobar adventure. In 1760 fourteen
Moravians reached Tranquebar, and they were promptly invited by
the Danish Government to transfer their activities to the Nicobars. 14
Not until 1768 did they procure a schooner, which they named the
Nicobar, to carry six of the missionaries, six white soldiers, six sepoys,
provisions for a year, and sawed timber for a blockhouse to Nancowry
harbour. Although two Moravians and two Danes died within the
first year, the health of the first Moravian colonists generally was
good. The Tranquebar Government decided to support the new
colonists and exempted them from all taxes for twenty years.
On the 16th September, 1769, the Tranquebar Government sent
out the Nazareth under command of Captain Joseph Falck, a Dane
who had been for some years in the Russian and Indian services.
Shortly after Falck took command as provisional head of the Nicobar
enterprise, twelve of his men deserted in a Malayan ship. By
January, 1770 Falck and his two assistants were buried in the
Moravian cemetery. The same year the Nazareth was lost at sea
while en route from Tranquebar to Kedah.
A number of negro slaves were sent from the Danish African
colonies to the Nicobars where they proved most useful, the Tamil
gardeners having died. The Moravians made considerable headway
with the planting of coffee, cotton, and other crops. On Easter
Sunday, 1771, six sepoys deserted. During the same year the Danes
recorded the visit of the English ship Diligent which made a survey
of the islands and their harbours.

Interest in the Nicobars now appeared from an unexpected
quarter. The Austrian East India Company entered the field rather
late, but in 1776 sent its first ship into the Orient. The frigate
Guiseppe and Maria Tereza anchored in Nancowry June 6, 1778,
after having already established Austrian "factories "in Mozambique
and Goa. The Austrian captain announced his intention of taking
possession of the islands of Trinkut, Kalshal, and Kamorte in the
name of the Emperor Joseph. When the Moravians protested on
the grounds that the islands were a Danish possession, the captain
endeavoured to persuade the Moravians to enter the Austrian service.
The Moravians declined, whereupon the Austrians erected a small
fort on the opposite side of the magnificent Nancowry harbour and
raised the imperial flag on July 12, 1778.

Meantime the Moravians had become discouraged by the paucity
of their results, by the sinking of their schooner by a French privateer
off Junk Ceylon Island, by the refusal of the Danish government to
permit them to occupy the Austrian fort, and by the ravages of Nicobar
fever. When the Danish vessel Gaasen visited the Nicobars in
1787 only two Moravians and two negroes purchased from Stahl
remained alive, and they were quite willing to abandon the islands.
Thus ended eighteen years of Moravian effort in the Nicobars.
Denmark did not relinquish sovereignty over the islands at
this time but sent Licut. Ohmeyer, a corporal, three Danish soldiers,
and four negroes to keep the Dannebrog flying in Nancowry harbour.
From 1786 to 1810 Europeans were rather more interested in Napoleon
than in the Nicobar Islands. During much of this time the Danes
maintained a watch of one or two Europeans and a few sepoys on the
isolated islands. Provisions were uncertain and death took a heavy
count among the Danish sergeants who were replaced at irregular
intervals.

During the Napoleonic wars the Danish colonies in India twice
were taken under British control. In 1805 the Amboyna, a British
privateer, arrived in Nancowry harbour, ousted the sentry, and
plundered the godowns and residences. The guards were left on
the islands until 1809 when a British ship removed them to Tran-
quebar. The British did not trouble to occupy Nancowry and when
the Danish commander Dietzen requested to be left behind with a
negro sepoy Joan, no one objected. The fate of Dietzen is unknown,
but the Rosen expedition of 1831 found Joan living under suspicion
of having had a leading part in the piracies which became very trouble-
some about that time.
Some of the debris of the Napoleonic wars was cleared up by
the Treaty of Kiel as signed January 14, 1814. This restored to
Denmark "all the possessions and colonics which have been con-
quered by the British arms in this present war, except the Island of
Heligoland." No direct mention was made of the Asiatic colonies,
and an additional article, which amplified the definitive treaty by
adding that England would restore to Denmark all the latter's colonies
in the continent and seas of Asia," was signed at Liege on the
7th of April, 1814.
When the news of the conclusion of peace reached Tranquebar,
Governor Bille prepared to send a new guard to Nancowry, but the
plan was dropped at his death. Although ships from Tranquebar
made frequent trips to the Nicobars and captains sailing under the
Danish flag enjoyed a preference in the islands, no flag was raised
and no troops were stationed there from 1809 until 1830.
During the first half of the past century Denmark's interest in
the islands took on new life due to the growth of the China trade and
the cramped condition of her Indian colonies. Some Danes remem-
bered the excellence of the Nancowry harbour and spoke and wrote
of creating there a "Danish Singapore." All interested in the Orient
were watching the growth of Penang and Singapore during that
period, and it was pointed out that Nancowry harbour was far superior
to both and that it lay in the direct path of vessels from Europe to
China. A board was appointed in Tranquebar to collect information
concerning the Nicobars from the Danish archives and from skippers
who had visited the islands. At the same time the Danish missionary
Rosen, then in British service, proposed a colonial venture along the
lines of the British ruling companies. In 1830 Rosen came down
from Trikulur to present his plans in person to the Tranquebar
governor. Governor Christensen became a supporter of the new
enterprise and he and Rosen purchased the sixty-four ton schooner
Cimbria, gathered a crew of thirty-one men in addition to the crew,
and sailed together for the Nicobars on July 23, 1831.

Christensen stayed on the islands for two months. He selected

The new colony of Frederickshoj was dedicated on September 22,

duties with unusual vigour ; foreign ships were required to register

harbour dues. Governor Christensen published notices in Indian
newspapers to the effect that the islands were again in Danish hands
and that ship supplies were available at Nancowry. Two British
naval vessels, the Comet and the Magician, subsequently visited the
islands but found only inadequate facilities.
Governor Christensen showed his personal interest in the Nico-
bars by making a second visit on the Cimbria in 1832. This visit
proved fatal as the governor died on shipboard while returning to
Tranquebar (his body arrived in a cask of spirits under the care of
his servant).
Rosen made an attempt to visit the northern islands but was
driven by storms first to Atchin and then to Junk Ceylon Island off
the coast of Siamese Malaysia. The Danish record tells of two
large Burmese vessels, probably from Moulmein, which were wrecked
on the islands in 1832. Mention is made of the fact that when the
colony was moved to the eastern side of Trinkut Island "all the
Burmese became ill." Thereupon the entire colony was shifted
back to Nancowry the 12th of February, 1833, and shortly thereafter
to a new site called Frederickshaven.
Returning from Tranquebar with men and supplies on March
4, 1833, the Cimbria was sent at once to Penang for more supplies
and Chinese labourers. Nicobar fever broke out on the Cimbria.
Captain Forbes and Due, the newly arrived Danish assistant, died
in Penang where the mate was obliged to pawn the ship's papers for
Rs. 1,000 in order to feed and hospitalize the survivors. Eventually
the ship returned to the islands. Rosen decided to move the brick
houses of the Moravians from Tripjet to Frederickshaven, but disease,
discouragement, and scarcity of food prevented the completion of
the project. The colony was in such dire straits that the Danish
King's birthday was celebrated January 28, 1834 only by the firing
of a salute; the customary celebrations were not held since refresh-
ments were limited to yams.

in March bringing a most useful young Eurasian who was in search
of employment in the colony. A'Cunha soon got the Nicobarese to

Denmark had become thoroughly tired of the expense of main-
taining the Nicobar enterprise. By Royal Decree of February 21,
1837, the islands were abandoned to the Nicobarese. The decree
ordered that further expenditures on account of the Nicobars should
cease for the Royal Government and that no expenditures were allowed
for the preservation of Danish sovereignty on the islands. The
canes, certificates, and flags were recalled from the village headmen.
Doubts which the British entertained with respect to Danish
sovereignty after 1837 were not, therefore, unwarranted. Thus
ended the third major attempt at colonizing the island group.
Although Denmark sent no ships or men to the Nicobars from
1835 to 1845 they were not entirely neglected by other Europeans.
During this interval an Italian priest from Burma spent more than
two years on Car Nicobar before returning to Rangoon to die of
fever. He was followed by two French missionaries, Chabord and
Plaissant, who managed to build a large brick house before opposition
from the Nicobarese caused them to remove to Teressa Island.
At this point that interesting gentleman, Captain Crisp of Ran-
goon, appeared on the Nicobar scene. It was he who removed the
Frenchmen to Teressa. He took that island in possession and made
application for acknowledgment there to the Commissioner in
Moulmein. 18 At the same time the Tranquebar government was
informed of his action. In 1848 Crisp again appeared in the Nicobars,
this time at Car Nicobar where the inhabitants were induced, by
rumours that the Danes were returning in large numbers, to authorize
Crisp to offer the islands to the Calcutta government. The offer
was rejected in Calcutta since questions of sovereignty were involved.

Subsequent to the withdrawal of the Danish guard in 1837,
piracy became common in the waters of the Andaman Sea. A few
Burmese and Siamese ships disappeared completely, while in 1839
the captain and most of the crew of the British ship Pilot were killed.
Five survivors of this attack were picked up in an open boat by a
ship in the China run. In 1843 three foreign ships were attacked
without reason near the central islands. The naval vessel Phlegethon
was sent to punish these attacks in 1845. Later in the same year the
captain of a British ship was killed in an attack near Camorte; the
crew was saved only by the ingenuity and bravery of the serang.
Public indignation in India, Burma, and Britain demanded that if
Denmark wished to retain title to the islands she must suppress
piracy in the region. 19 Newspapers in India agitated against Den-
mark's ownership and an expedition was planned from Moulmein.
Just at this time the Danish governor from Tranquebar, Peter Hansen,
was in Calcutta to negotiate the sale of Denmark's Indian holdings,
but with instructions to insist upon Denmark's ownership of the
Nicobars.
During the Calcutta negotiations the Danish Consul-General
D. C. Mackey proposed to Governor Hansen that Denmark pay half
the expense of sending another expedition to the Nicobars to investi-
gate their alleged coal fields and to experiment with cotton growing.
Mackey, a leading Calcutta merchant, offered to pay half the expense.
Without waiting for instructions from Copenhagen, the schooner
L'Espiegle was chartered and fitted out under the orders of Captain
Lewis. H. C. Busch, who formerly had been Danish Resident at
the Fredericksnagore colony in Bengal, was chosen commander of
the expedition. A stranded Danish student named Lowert was ap-
pointed chief assistant. On March 31, 1845 the L'Espiegle reached
Car Nicobar where the usual flags and certificates were given out.
The vessel proceeded to Nancowry and on the 5th of May took pos-
session of Condul by firing a salute off the island and awarding a
flag to the "Rajah of Condul." It was discovered that the coal
fields on Great Nicobar were of little value. The Phlegethon and
the L'Espiegle were in the Nicobars at the same time, the former on a
mission which really disregarded Danish claims of sovereignty. On
May 20 the expedition was back in Calcutta at a cost of only 4,000
Rix-dollars to the Danish government.

completed, and the sloop-of-war Galathea was on its way out bringing

Upon its return to Pulo Milu the little Nancowry found the
colony in distress; the usual list of deaths mounted steadily. An
carthquake occurred, and there was unrest among the Nicobarese.
The Danish government made a complete reversal of its Nicobar
policy, and on December 8, 1847 decided definitely to abandon the
islands for good. The sloop-of-war Valkyrien came out from Copen-
hagen to remove the survivors of the Pulo Milu colony, which was
reached on June 10, 1848. The same evening the Dannebrog was

last commander of the Nicobar colony, Von Teylingen by name,
decided to settle in Tranquebar where he died August 3, 1873.
When the Valkyrien touched at Madras on its outward journey,
Captain Sechers, in accordance with his instructions, notified the
governments in Calcutta, Batavia, and Pondicherry of the imminent
abandonment of the Danish colony. At the same time he announced
the continuance of Danish sovereignty in the islands.

and French capitalists. French proposals for the development of
the Nicobars were most persistent, and there was some apprehension
in British circles lest the French occupy the islands. Germany was

Denmark's difficulties following the Schleswig war of 1864 made
it difficult for her to deal with piracy which was common in the

in Copenhagen, expressed the British desire that something be done
to put down this troublesome piracy. At the same time it was pointed
out that the islands were worthless and could not be purchased.
Denmark thereupon decided to abandon its claims to ownership of
the Nicobars and thus avoid responsibility for disturbances in those
distant waters.
The government of India, with full Danish approval, assumed
control over the Nicobars and united them to the Andamans for
administrative purposes in 1869. In 1871 some 200 prisoners were
transferred from Port Blair to the Nicobars. Roepstorff, a Dane
who had spent several years in the Andamans, was placed in charge
of the work project for the prisoners. He was murdered by one of
them on October 24, 1883 and it is believed no Dane has remained
in the Nicobars for any length of time since that day.
Among the causes for Danish failure in the Nicobars should be
listed the fickleness of the home government at Copenhagen and the
fact that Ross's epochal research into the treatment and prevention
of malaria was not made until the beginning of the present century.
Little has been done with the Nicobars even with the aid of our
present knowledge of tropical medicine; no European has cared to
live on the islands for any length of time. So far as the modern
world is concerned the Nicobars remain much as they were in the
time of Marco Polo.
