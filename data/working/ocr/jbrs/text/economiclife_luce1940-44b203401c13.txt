Scholars are generally agreed that the ancient home of Tibeto- Burman
speaking peoples was somewhere in the north-west of China, say Kansu, between
the Gobi Desert and north-east Tibet. In late Neolithic times, about 3000 B.C.,
Kansu appears to have reached a high degree of civilization possibly superior to
that of the rest of China. The finest painted pottery - unequalled in the whole
Neolithic world - has been found here ¹: and it may well have been Tibeto-
Burman rather than Chinese. The use of bronze itself seems to have spread to,
China via Kansu, though Kansu bronzes cannot compare with Chang Chinese.
The earliest Chinese written records. dating from the latter half of the 2nd
millennium B.C., and found at An-yang below the east edge of the Shansi plateau,
frequently mention a people called the Ch'iang, to the west of the Chinese. The
Ch'iang were shepherds or goatherds, whereas the Chinese were agriculturists
whose livestock was cattle and pigs rather than sheep. Where sheep have cropped,
cattle cannot graze. It was natural therefore that their owners should quarrel:
indeed the writings shew the Chinese constantly raiding and capturing the Ch'iang,
and even using them on a large scale for human sacrifice. ³ The Ch'iang found a
refuge for a time in W. Kansu, and later near the sources of the Yellow River and
the Koko Nor in north-east Tibet. But even here they were pursued by the Chinese
rulers of Ts'in in the 1st millennium B.C., and many of the Ch'iang tribes fled south
through the mountains and the gorges", reappearing many centuries later in Tibet
or Burma.

Such, roughly, ladies and gentlemen, is the framework of your ancient
history. And whatever the truth about the details may be, certain, main conclusions
seem to me inevitable. However high your Kansu culture may have been in
Neolithic times, you must have largely lost it in your long trek south through the
deserts, the mountains and the jungles. With the expansion of China the Ch'iang
had either to be absorbed, or to become nomads in the wilds. It was a hard choice,
between liberty and civilization. Your ancestors chose liberty; and they must have
gallantly maintained it. But the cost was heavy. It cost them more than 2000 years
of progress. If the Ch'iang in 3000 B.C. were equals of the Chinese in civilization,
the Burmans in 700 A.D. were not nearly as advanced as the Chinese in 1300 B.C.

They had changed completely, and more than once, their whole manner of
life. From sheep and pasture they had turned to desert and the kiang; thence
perhaps to icy plateaux, the musk-deer and the yak; thence to jungle gorges,
snakes, bears tigers, elephants, rhinoceros, etc., far more numerous and spreading
then much further north than now. Hardest of all, perhaps, they had the fond the
means to combat or avoid the many dangers of living in the tropics, develop some
immunity to sores and fevers, and face the last ordeal of the plunge down to the
plains.
Their Neolithic bee-hive pits half-sunk in the Kansu loess, they had had to
exchange for yurts of skins, for mountain caves, for leaf-shelters in trees, and
finally for pile propped dwellings thatched with grass Even today the great
majority of Burma's people, who once lived underground, have lived so long above
it, that they scarce dare yet descend to terra firma.

For food, they probably knew in north-west China both panicked and
sorghum millet, perhaps also rice. Rice, we know, was grown in N. China since
Neolithic times. But once started, only the eastern wing of the migration, nearest
the plains of China, could continue to enjoy such luxuries. And many, by the time
they issued from the gorges, must have lost all knowledge of agriculture; and if
they still ate grain, it was the large, hard, pearly" tears of Job," Coix ° growing
wild in the hill-jungles, and used a like for food and ornament. Only the luckiest
could have kept some items of their Kansu diet - the hardy vegetables of the north
that have found their way into the tropics, the radish, mustard, leek, onion, and
garlic, and a few sub-tropical gourds and melons, the 'stone' pumpkin, the bottle-
gourd, the sweet melon and the cucumber. Even leguminous plants, the peas and
beans in all their infinite variety, would seem to have been lost to them, and

Few domestic animals could have followed them throughout their journey
except the Dog, perhaps the Pig, the Goat and barnyard Fowl, and those unwanted
guests of man whose names betray an ancient origin - the Fly, the Flea and the
Louse. The Pig, the dearest friend of every home in Neolithic China, was perhaps
man's direst enemy in Burma: witness several legends of Burmans and Karens.
When finally the wanderers issued from the gorges, no wonder if, as Chinese
records tell us, they still retained some curious relics of their chequered past,
delighting still in milk and cream, storing the dung of animals for fuel, using
elephants for ploughing, and breeding yaks (or zo ?) on the Upper Chindwin,
barely 2000 ft. above sea-level."
The humble ground of Pagan civilization must be noted if one would
gauge the full height of its achievement. When I consider how they started - a
crude unlettered tribe of simple Hillman such as on may see today in a remote
bazaar in the Shan States, and when I then contemplate the grandeur of the
Patothamya,, the Ananda and the Thatbyinnyu, Pagna history seems to me a
miracle.
How was it that the Burmans did it?- I cannot tell you. But I can tell you a
few things that helped them.

Central Burma. The Burmans had had no time to get acclimatized; they have left
no trace, unless it is the Danu, of their passage through the North Shan States.
They must have crossed them almost at the gallop. And the changes to the plains
was a doubly difficult one. In Burma elevation and rainfall are the two chief
determinants of climate- elevation down to the frost line about 3000 ft., rainfall
below that. The Burmans had to make suddenly the double change, from cold
highland to burning lowland, form wet jungle to drought and desert.

It was the conquest of the coast by Anoratha, soon after 1050 A.D., that
brought the Burmans directly in contact with the last and greatest of the civilizing
influences that helped them – India, including Ceylon. Indirectly, of course, Indian
influences had reached them earlier 32; but Sanskrit and Brahmanism have affected
Burmese much less than Mon; it was the later Pali which over-whelmed the Pagan
Burman, supplying him, ready made, with a complete mental outfit. His
intellectual debt to India is generally recognized, and I need say no more about it.
What is less well known, is the extent of his economic debt. That debt, of which I
shall have much to say in the rest of this paper, had been steadily mounting from
the 1st century A.D., when Indians first in large numbers began to come by sea and
settle in Lower Burma, centuries before the Burmans entered in the north.
Having thus sketched the coming of the Burmans and their spreading over
Burma, I should now, I think, speak in detail of their economy as revealed in the
Pagan inscriptions. These are nearly always concerned with religious dedications;
we cannot expect everything to find its way into them. Hunting or fishing, for
example more important, probably, in the life of the ancient Burman than in that
of the modern - will hardly be mentioned in a Buddhist inscription.

Has there been a change of climate in Central Burma since the Pagan
period? It is often assumed that there has been, on the authority of an interesting
article in Vol. III (PP. 40-46) of the Journal of the Burma Research Society
"Climate in Burmese History" by the late Mr. J. C. Mackenzie. I am not competent
to discuss Mackenzie's theories about climatic fluctuation in general. His chief
evidence for desiccation in Burma is five passages in inscriptions mentioning
"paddy land or garden land in places where nowadays there is either no such land
or at any rate nothing like the area said to have been dedicated." In all these
instances, he says "there is no way to account for the change in the nature of the
lands except by the hypothesis of a change of climate." Unfortunately, he takes his
renderings of these inscriptions from U Tun Nyein's very rough translation, instead
of the original. I have consulted the latter, and now regard the evidence as far less
conclusive. All five are Pagan inscriptions, but only two belong to the Pagan
period; the rest date from 100 to 150 years after the fall of Pagan. In the two old

evidence on this subject. I have collected records of over 50 transactions in land

10. There are several minor evidences that the proto-Burmans entered Burma
from the east or north. Their word for Elephant is common to most of the Burma
Group of languages, Chinese, Tai, Karen and even Mon, Palaung and Wa; but no
Kachin, Chin, Sak or other language to the west of Burma has it. Nor have the
Chin Hills the Peacock, which the proto-Burman loved and domesticated; nor had
they the Horse, which he bred on a big scale and used in his conquest of the plains.
Major evidence lies (a) in the present position the outliers of the Burma Group of
languages, Maru, Lashi, Atsi, Maingtha and Hpun, stretched in echelon along the
road to Burma, between the Nmai Hka, the Irrawaddy and the Salween; (b) in the
Burmese language itself, which in vocabulary is almost to Chinese as to Tibetan,
and closer to Lolo than to Chin; and (c) in the positive evidence of the Man-shu of
Fan Ch'o (c. 865 A.D.), which describes the tribes at the tail-end of the migration:
see especially f. 18r°-21v°.

97. BOUNDARY POSTS: Accurate recording and demarcation of boundaries
must obviously have become increasingly important to the Burmans with ever
increasing pressure on the occupied land. Many so called Pagan inscriptions record
boundaries even from the earlier half of the dynasty, but most of these (e.g. List
455-18, 46a 19-23, 486-20, 548-1
486-20 548-14 341a2-3, 360a2-17, b2-26, 3739-12, 3884-16,22-26, etc.) are not
original. At first indications of land dedicated were made in rough and general
terms. In very large dedications, such as those to the north of the Yaw recorded in
Natonmya's Khemavara inscription (Plate 317-19, 569s), certain main features,
villages or mountain-ranges, are given in all four or eight quarters surrounding the
area. Smaller land dedications begin gradually to be indicated with reference to
minor features of the landscape, especially big trees (Plate 22012-20, 2244-36, 3516-9
etc.: see note 67 supra), pagodas, paths tanks, etc (Plate 2189-12), or to owners of
lands adjacent (Plate 256 16-20, 25720-2 etc.). The actual planting of boundary posts
was at first often the consequence of land disputes and litigation (Plate 117a6,
122b9, 371a 15-17), resulting in accurate measurement with the ta (Plate 125a³),
formal "treading of the boundaries" (apuin akhra nan) by ministers or local
notables (Plate 20319, 296 15-27, 42317), and planting of main and other posts (tuin
rhan, tuin mran, tuin, Plate 220 14,16 etc.). These posts were usually of wood (non,
non krat, khapon, rha, unhay; Plate 22014,20, 42432 etc), but sometimes of stone

will be abundance of elephants (cin), horses (kseh), water-buffaloes (pren), cattle

which the Sanskrit word may have been taken originally, is common in Jataka
