ANANDA BRICK MONASTERY INSCRIPTIONS OF PAGAN
Tin Lwin
:
Close to the famous Ananda Temple² at Pagan there is a religious edifice
seemingly deserted and unoccupied, but well looked after by the Archaeology
Department of Burma. It is much smaller, less known and of much later date:
It is a pucca-building known as a brick monastery (iṭṭhaka vihāra in Pali and ok
kyaung in Burmese). It however has no original name, but because it stands in
the neighbourhood of the Temple it has come to be known as Ananda Brick
Monastery. In this monastery are some inscriptions which may accordingly be
called Ananda Brick Monastery Inscriptions.**
There are four versions of the inscriptions: two in Pali and two in Burmese.
The Burmese versions seem to be translations of the Pali although there are slight.
differences here and there in details. These inscriptions are found on the outer
faces of the northern and southern walls of the middle hall of the monastery.
Their exact locations are as follows: (1) the first Pali version on the eastern part
of the southern wall, (2) the first Burmese version on the western part of the
southern wall, (3) the second Pali version on the eastern part of the northern wall
and (4) the second Burmese version on the western part of the northern wall. For
convenience these may be marked Pl, B1, P2 and B2 respectively. There is also a
shorter version in Burmese on the inner face of the southern wall. Though it is
in the form of an abstract it adds the names of masons and more information
about the monk to whom the lower storey of the monastery was offered: This
version should be marked A.
Each of the first four inscriptions is mainly in two parts: firstly, the narra-
tion as to how the monastery came into existence and secondly, the prayers of the

In the Pali versions the narration part is in prose and the prayer in verses.
While the prose of one version slightly differs from the other in form or in
composition, the verses remain the same in both the versions. The Burmese
versions however are in prose throughout.
There are spelling and grammatical errors in the Pali versions. Spelling
errors include wrong lengthening of short vowel (kappiya for kappiya, cirakālaṁ for
cirakālaṁ, etc. in P1 and loki ya for lokiya in P1 and P2) and vice versa (gacchati
ti for gacchati ti, kammakarānaṁ for kammakārānaṁ, gambhira for gambhira, etc.
in P1), wrong doubling of single consonant (ṭṭhapite for thapite in P1) and vice
versa (Miteya for Miteyya, paniddhisu for panniddhisu in Pl and mutama for
muttama in P2). As regards grammatical errors there are wrong omission of case-
ending (kahāpaṇa for kahāpaṇņehi in P1) and wrong use of gender or case (imaṁ
iṭṭhakavihāram patiṭṭhāpitam for ayam iṭṭhakavihāro patiṭṭhāpito in P1 & P2).
Use of b for p is also found (pabbomi for pappomi). Wrong or unnessary punc-
tuation at many places makes the inscriptions rather clumsy. The word kaliyugas
used as the name of the Burmese Era is peculiar, too.
The Burmese versions are faulty mainly in spelling. For instance there are
use of n for m or m (khap sin for khap sim in B1, mwam man for mwam mam in
B2) and use of y for r (chan khyań for chań khran) in B1 & B2). Wrong omission
of the visarga and the dot marking the creaky tone can also be noticed.
faults in all the versions are perhaps negligible.

The inscriptions are significant in that it reveals the history of the monastery.
From the narration part we know that it was built in A. D. 1775 and that it took
ten long years to finish it totally. The donor was Uttanarājā, Royal Archivist of
Pagan. The building is of two storeys with certain architectural features. The
donation was made in 1779 when only the brick structure was completed. The lower
storey was offered to one Sudhammālaṁkāra,? a leading Mahāthera of Pagan, and
the upper storey to those unknown monks who might happen to arrive there from
other places at any time. Thus the gift was puggalika (personal) as well as sanghika
(common). Apart from these facts, it is interesting to note the materials used in
building the monastery. The use of lime, molasses and buffalo leather for making
cement according to traditional method is of special interest. Facts and figures
concerning the materials and workers should also be valued as they throw some
light on certain economic aspects of the period.
Last but not least, the significance of these inscriptions also lies in the fact
that they belong to a monastery which is not only an old architectural piece, but

a storehouse of Burmese paintings which adorn the walls and the ceilings of the
monastery. Unfortunately nothing is mentioned in the inscriptions about these
paintings, but it is not difficult to hold that they were done towards the end of the
18th century. The paintings contain floral designs and animal motifs. What is
more important is that they depict contemporary scenes of the people almost from
all walks of life. These paintings are all the more precious in view of the fact
that Burmese paintings of post-Pagan centuries, especially those of the years
covering the Konbaung Period (1753-1885) are now very rare. The inscriptions
provide a firm ground on which the paintings can well be studied.
