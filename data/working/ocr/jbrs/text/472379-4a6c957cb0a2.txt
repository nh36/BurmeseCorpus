CRITIQUE OF A NEW PREFACE TO J. S. FURNIVALL'S
An Introduction to the Political Economy of Burma
When more than a quarter of a century
ago J. S. Furnivall, then a retired ICS
official, showed a simple text book of
economics which he had been written with
reference to life in Burma, to Professor
Jevons of Rangoon University, the latter
suggested that a book on the life of Burma
with reference to economic theory would
be more helpful.
Professor Jevons made
arrangements for Mr. Furnivall to give a
course of lectures at the University and out
of these lectures came the book entitled
An Introduction to the Political Economy of
Burma which has now become a classic in
the economic literature on Burma.
And now ten years after Burma's
independence, Mr. Furnivall has written a
new preface to a reprint of his book at the
request of the People's Literature Com-
mittee. In this new preface the author has
taken 'the opportunity of attempting to
clarify some of the major problems inherent
in the situation of Burma at the dawn of
independence'. He did this because 'un-
fortunately ten years ago too many Burmans
regarded independence as a sufficient guaran-
tee of welfare' and 'consequently some now
fell disappointed and despondent'. The new
preface has therefore considerable signific-
ance and deserves a critical review as much
as the book itself which however is so wel-
known and so widely read that it does not
at this stage require any more review.
The preface includes an able outline of
the social and economic evolution of
modern Burma. Seclusion from the outside
world characterised the country under the
Burmese kings and contributed to the crea-
tion of a distinctive civilization, pleasant in
many features but resembling that of
mediaeval Europe in its failure to develop
political and economic might. Confronted
with the West demanding admission into
the country to exploit rich natural resources,
Burma found herself incapable of self-
defence and forfeited her independence.
The victorious British power resorted
to indirect rule in the remote tribal hills
because it was the cheapest method of
administration. In Burma proper however,
the British ruled directly in order to provide
greater security for purposes of commerce
and industry. Burmans suffered the conse-
quences of their former self-sufficiency.
They found themselves excluded from trade
and industry and could not compete with
experienced technicians and cheap and
docile labour imported from India. A plural
society developed consisting of several
racial groups living side by side but
separately. Due to the impact of economic
forces and the replacement of custom by
the rule of law, social life disintegrated and
the vacuum was filled by crime and anarchy.
General administrative service was staffed
by Europeans at the top and by Burmans in
the subordinate ranks, while the require-
ments and other professions were filled by
imported personnel from India. 'Another
important part of the machinery of British
rule was the armed force consisting of a

But once the props failed the entire
unstable edifice gave way. The Second
World War swept away the political struc-
ture erected on principles valid solely for
business concern, that was Burma. 'The
War destroyed practically all the capital

economic apparatus that has escaped des-

rule was wiped out but Burmans gained
some experience in the handling of the
administrative machinery. Japanese imper-

against any foreign rule.
At the end of the War the British
Government came back with a blue paper
setting the clock back in respect of Bur-
man self-government. It was in fact a
foolish attempt to reconstruct prewar
Burma as a business concern managed
by foreign capital and enterprise, but
without the prewar quasi-democratic
trappings. This policy consolidated and
further inflamed national sentiment.” Aung

with no access to Indian troops anymore,

the intriguing communists from the AFPFL,
committing the remaining leadership to
the principles of democracy and socialism.

Then followed Aung San's assassination
and then independence in January 1948.
After this masterly survey of the
social and economic evolution of modern
Burma, Mr. Furnivall goes on to discuss
the problems the newly independent nation
faced, under five heads: political, economic,
social, administrative and military.
The main political problem, internally,
was the problem of national unity. There
was 'the still imperfect sympathy between
racial groups whose primary allegiance
was to their own ethnic and tribal unit
rather than to Burma as a nation.' This
is partially borne out by the Karen and
Mon insurrections. But it can also be
argued on the other hand that disaffection
among these minority groups was only
partial and even there it took pleace only
because the more far-sighted leaders found
themselves unable to control their more
impetuous followers. And it would not
have taken place but for the opportune
circumstances created by earlier distrubances
which had roots in the other 'cleft along
ideological lines between those who accepted
and those who repudiated communism."
The leftist disunity proved to be a greater
menace to security and peace in the
country than the racial disunity.
'Domestic political problems were
further complicated by the external situa-
tion, as the national leaders, with no
experience of international affairs, except
to a very limited extent in the different
conditions of the Japanese occupation, had
to pick their way delicately along the
perilous border-line between the rival
power blocs of East and West.' Reading
this, one is reminded of what another
weleknown writer Tibor Mende, had to say
about the same subject in his South East
Asia Between the Two Worlds. It was

Mr. Furnivall dealt at some length
with economic problems which he said
were far more difficult and complex. It
may be so but it should be remembered
that the political problems cast such a
thick shadow over the economic problems
as to enhance the difficulty and complexity
of their solution. They were therefore in
this sense also problems in political eco-
nomy rather than economics. Mr. Furni-
vall cites as the main economic problems
the problems of expanding production, of
a balance between production, of devising
machinery for the enhancement of social
welfare, and of inducing the people to
take advantage of their new opportunities
of welfare. 'Closely linked up with these
were the problems of adapting the admi-
nistrative machinery to the performance
of these new functions and making it
it strong enough to perform."

Mr. Furnival rightly gave priority to
the expansion of production as the most
direct way of increasing welfare.
developed countries, redistribution of pro-
perty and income may deserve as much
attention as increased production. But in
underdeveloped countries like Burma, where
property and income are notoriously scanty
and low, producing a bigger cake is the
most effective way of increasing individual
shares of it.
The second problem of striking a
balance between production and comsump-
tion is particulary difficult in a democracy
where a government which looks too far
ahead for the sake of the next genera-
tion is likely to be dumped in elections
381
by the present generation. The dilemma
is further strengthened for a newly inde-
pendent government from which people
expect bold new programmes for rapid
development as well as hasty amelioration
of their living standards. This desire to
have the cake and eat it too is indeed
a universal weakness of long standing.
Mr. Furnivall discussed the shortage of
skills in Burma at considerable length. Here
a Burman reader if only out of national
pride may differ with the author in the
degree of intensity of the problem. To quote
Mr. Furnivall, ‘the fundamental obstacle to
industrial expansion was not the lack of
capital but the lack of human resources-
of man-power'. Considering the destruction
of half of capital assets during the war, the
reluctance of foreign capital to enter the
country, and the low level of incomes and
savings among the nationals, it would indeed
be unrealistic to ignore the shortage of
capital as a fundamental obstacle to indus-
trial expansion which is ordinarily much
more capital-intensive than traditional agri-
culture.
The author rightly pointed out 'that
there were limits to the profitable employ-
ment of foreign experts, and to the benefit
which would accrue from the training of
Burmans in foreign land'. There is of course
another alternative of having Burmans
trained at home by foreign experts, but the
natural desire of foreign experts to renew
their contracts by prolonging the period of
tutelage (so reminiscent of similar tactics in
the political and administrative fields in the
pre-independence days) provides an obstacle.
Altogether, however, too much seems to
have been made about the difficulties of
acquisition of technical skills. One has only
to look at the rapid industrialization of

Coming now to the problem of adminis-
trative machinery, the author opines that
an active policy for the enhancement of
welfare as well as the measures required in
connection with international relations and
economic progress, must certainly demand
its considerable enlargement'. This seems to
have been done as far as numerical strength
is concerned, for government employees
now number almost 250,000 persons or
more than one in every hundred people in
Burma. But apparently what Mr. Furnivall
referred to was quality rather than quantity
because he illustrated by figures the de-
pletion of the superior administrative Civil
Service, the "steel frame” of British rule,
and higher cadres of other services. But
one wonders sometimes whether the 'steel
frame,' which was primarily trained for
routine administrative duties such as main-
tenance of law and order, revenue collection
etc., even if it remained intact, could have
sufficiently coped with problems of nation
building entailing national and long-term
planning. Short of a comple overhaul of
the governmental machinery, it is difficult
to see how these monumental problems of
national reconstruction under socialist prin-
ciples could have been tackled by bureau-
cratic methods and procedure evolved and
adapted for purposes of routine administra-
tion under laissez faire principles. However,
the inevitability of the growth of official
powers vis-a-vis the expansion of govern-
mental role in the economic life could have
been foreseen and such disincentives as
denunciation by politicians of officials as
one-time agents of foreign power, down-
grading of their remuneration, excessive

scrutiny of acts of commission rather than
those of omission etc., might well have been
avoided.
Mr. Furnivall also remarked on the
serious consequences of depletion in the
auxiliary services. This seems to have been
fully appreciated by the Gevernment, judg-
ing by the efforts to train up large number
of engineers, doctors and other profes-
sionals at home and abroad, although
nothing seems to have been done about the
structure of demand for such professional
talent. What one fails to find in Mr.
Furnivall's treatment of the subject is the
anachronism of the governmental system
which was used by the British for sub-
ordinating the executive and specialist
departments which for their sheer size could
not be staffed entirely by Britishers to the
few British administrators.
The author's observation on the Bur-
man mistrust of capital and his distinction
between two processes involved in the
acquisition of foreign enterprises i.e.
nationalization proper, the replacement of
foreign operatives by nationals, and social-
isation, the transfer of ownership to the
State, are all very relevant.
Himself coming from the West, Mr.
Furnivall is too much aware of the differ-
ences of background and circumstances
between Burma and the West to fall into
the error, that many of we Burman
economists
are prone, to apply western
economic concepts blindly to the conditions
here. Also because economic problems
are always intertwined with political, social
and other aspects of national life, Mr.
Furnivall as a social thinker is eminently
qualified to discourse on the political
economy of Burma.

a tone of sober optimism, ‘Confidence may
