Some Thoughts on the United Nations
Development Decades*
SAW WILLIAM Paw**
Mr. Chairman, Ladies and Gentlemen:
As it is customary for the outgoing
President of the Society to read a paper
at the Annual General Meeting of the
Society, I have prepared a short paper
entitled "Some thoughts on the U.N.
Development Decades" for this particular
cccasion.
Let me at the very outset, state my
reasons for the choice of this topic: First,
the U.N.Development Decades embrace
the coordinated thoughts and efforts of
all the nations of the world and as such
it deserves close study and attention.
Secondly, the main aim of the U.N.
Development Decades is directed towards
the independent planning and implementa
tion of the programmes and projects which
are considered best suited to the develop-
ment of the individual developing country
of the world. And finally, the U.N.
Development Decades create what may
be termed as "development consciousness"
among those who are anxious for
the economic and social development
of their respective countries and also
provide an international slogan or banner
under which the community of the con-
cerned can group or mobilize their efforts
and contributions.
First U.N. Development Decade:
The General Assembly of the United
Nations at its 1084th plenary meeting held
on the 19th December, 1961 by its resolu-
tion 1710 (XVI) designated the 1960s as
"the United Nations Development Decade,
in which Member States and their peoples
will intensify their efforts to mobilize and
to sustain support for the measures required
on the part of both devoloped and develop-
ing countries to accelerate progress towards
self-sustaining growth of the economy of
the individual nations and their social
development". The main aim, in more
specific term, was "to attain in each under-
developed country a substantial increase in
the rate of growth, with each country set-
ting its own target, taking as the objective
a minimum annual rate growth of aggre-
gate national income of 5 per cent at the
end of the Decade."
A brief and concise analysis of the
economic conditions of the world during
the 1960s is recorded in the United Nations
publication under the title of "Towards
Accelerated Development" - Proposals for
the Second United Nations Development
Decade. This publication was prepared
by the United Nations Committee of
Development Planning under the Chair-
manship of Professor Jan Tinbergen, Nether-
land School of Economics. The analysis
reads as follows:
"Although the annual rates of growth
recorded in developing countries during
the 1960s have in general been modest,
they have often marked a break from the
stagnation and inertia characteristic of the
decades preceding the the Second World
War. Broadly speaking the productive
capacity of developing countries has
increased significantly; their investment
and saving levels have recorded gains, and,
what is perhaps most important, their
ability to undertake wider and more com-
plex activities has been steadily on the

evenly shared among developing countries.
There has been a marked disparity among
the rates of growth recorded in different
developing countries or regions, and the
underlying heterogeneity of economic and
social conditions has generally remained
unchanged. On the average, developing
countries achieved during 1960-67 an
annual rate of increase of about 4.6 per

but only about 2 per cent in their per

the same period, the comparable rate of
increase in per capital gross domestic
product for developing countries in Africa
is estimated to have been around 1.5 per
cent; in Asia 2.2 per cent and in Latin
America 1.8 per cent. In many countries,
and even in some continents, the average
rate of increase in both total and per
capita gross domestic product in this period
has actually declined in comparison to the
rate achieved in the 1950s. There are
indeed not many countries which have
made truly rapid progress, apart from
those which have benefited from excep-
tionally favourable external conditions
(notably petroleum - producing countries
and countries which have gained from war
circumstances). By contrast, there are
many countries where the rate of progress
has been very moderate. Particularly the
performance of many countries with a
low per capita income and large popula-
tion has been totally inadequate and these
constitute the core of the problem. For
these countries, the basic preconditions,
both economic and social, of accelerated
progress are still absent. And in quite a
few places, the most valuable energy is
being vitiated by political crises, instead
I of being used for economic and social
progress.
During the same period, developed
countries continued to expand their econo-
mies at high rates. Between 1960 and 1967
the developed market economies imcreased
annually their total gross domestic product
by 5.1 per cent and their per capita gross

A summary of the analysis may be
given as follows:
1. During the first Development Decade,
the developing countries as a whole
achieved some reasonable degree of
development, the majority of them
showing some signs of positive efforts
to emerge from the previous stagnant
positions.
2. However, the growth rates for some
of the developing countries in Africa,
Asia and Latin America were very
low, the per capita increase in the
gross domestic product of some coun-
tries recorded less than zero per cent.

3. At the same time, during the period,
most of the western developed nations
and the eastern European socialist
countries achieved envious economic
gains.
4. Financial aid and technical assistance
were provided by the developed coun-
tries through bi-lateral agreements
worked out between the aid-giving
and aid-receiving nations and also
through international assistance_agen-

short of the needs of the developing
nations.
5. In the area of trade, there were some
encouraging indications of progress

yet on the whole, the share of deve-
loping countries had shrunk from 31%
in 1950 to 18% in 1968.
6. On the other hand, some of the deve-
loping nations wasted a lot of their
valuable resonrces in political effort
instead of using them for economic
and social progress..
7. In short, during the first Develop-
ment Decade, some developing coun-
tries did achieve some progress while
the majority of these countries still
struggled through the many problems
connected with the achievement of
political independence and also a
desire to maintain what they consider
as a sensible degree of economic in-
dependence.
Partners in Development:
The keen interest in development in
the late 1960s by the World Bank led
to the formation of a Commission on In-
ternational Development headed by the
late Lester B. Pearson, former Prime Mini-

Nobel Peace Prize; the other 7 members²
of the Commission were prominent inter-
national personnel. The report of the
Commission was published in the fall of
1969 under the title of "Partners in Deve-
lopment” in which the authors frankly
pointed out the need for concerted and
co-operative effort to reduce the widening
gap between the developed and developing
countries. They pointed out the need for
a proper understanding of development by
the donors and recipients alike so as to
eliminate the growing misconception which
tended to creep in between them in the
process of working out details connected

Second U.N. Development Decade:
Having realized that the actual achieve-
ment of the 1st Development Decade would
be less than the anticipated expectations, the
Economic and Social Council of the
United Nations felt the need for a closer
review and attention to the developing
efforts of the nations and as a result, the
Committee for Development Planning was
instituted. This Committee which was made
up of 18 Experts from Member countries,
appointed in their personal capacities by
the Economic and Social Council on the
nomination of the Secretary-General of
the United Nations, submitted its proposals
to the General Assembly commencing in
decision with regard to the designation of
September 1970, for consideration and
1970s as the Second United Nations
Development Decade. In its report, the
Committee stated that it should be possible
for the developing countries as a whole
to achieve during the Decade of the 1970s
an average annual rate of expansoin some
where in the range of 6-7 per cent in
total gross product and of 3.5-4.5 per
cent per capita. It also recommended
the need for vigorous and co-ordinated
action to be taken by all concerned. The
average annual increase of population over
the decade is assumed at 2.5 per cent.
The General Assembly of the United
Nations at its 1883 plenary meeting held
on 24th October, 970 adopted the resolution
2626 (XXV) to designate the 1970s as the
Second United Nations Development Decade.
The basic aim of the Second U.N. Develop-
ment Decade is in essence similar to
that of the First U.N. Development Decade,
except that it emphasizes on "accelerated
development". It may, at this point, be
appropriate to reproduce some of the
concluding remarks made by the Committee
of Development Planning, which reads as
follows:
"It is crucial for the world community
to accelerate the economic and social
progress of developing countries during
the 1970s. The progress must include not

Prebisch Report:
There is yet another interesting Re-
port on Latin America, prepared by Dr.
Raul Prebisch³ for the Inter-American
Development Bank and published in 1971
under the title "Change and Development-
Latin America's Great Task". This re-
port which has come to be known as the
Prebisch Report was warmly welcomed for
some similarities in emphasis with that of
the Pearson Report on the one hand and
at the same time the report had distinct
recommendations for the developing coun-
tries of the Latin America on the other.
For example, both the Reports placed
strong emphasis on ‘reciprocal responsi-
bilities' and the need for 'convergent
measures' for the countries concerned in
development efforts. However, there was
a difference in the projected growth rates
– 8% in the Prebisch Report and 6% in
the Pearson Report. Then there was also
the difference in the priority given to the
sectors for development. In Latin Ame-
rica the emphasis was to be given to
'Employment' and 'Industrialization' where-
as the Pearson Report tried to pin point
the importance of giving priority to
‘Agriculture' and 'Population Control'.
Whatever differences might be noted in

What is the Situation?
We have come close to the midway
point of the Second Development Decade.
What type of experiences had the world
in general, and the developing nations, in
particular, gone through?
The first year of the Second Develop-
ment Decade, that is 1971, faced some of
the worst world crises, notably the U.S.
Dollar Crisis arising out of the cumula-
tive effect of continuous adverse balance
of payments and the steady conversion of
dollars into gold, and the formal ending
of convertibility in August, 1971, followed
by the devaluation of the dollar by the
Smithsonian Agreement of December 1971
- all of which had serious repercussion
on the International Monetary System
which had its root in the Bretton Woods
Agreement of 1944. The search for an
acceptable solution to the International
Monetary Problem is still in progress.
Right up to the present moment there is
yet no universal agreement to the type of
International Monetary System to be
adopted. These events, undoubtedly, affect
both the planning and implementation
programmes of the developing countries.
Then there was the pollution problem
and the advocation of zero economic
growth as a solution to the conservation
of world resources, which went counter
to the path desired by the developing
countries. There was also the Middle
East War of October 1973, followed by
the 'Energy Crisis' as a result of actions
taken by the oil producers; the effect of
which is still being felt in both developed
and developing countries.
Then there was the change in climatic
conditions which resulted in the destruc-
tive storms, the disastrous floods, and the
droughts which destroyed life and proper-

ties and hindered the progress of
development efforts in developing count-
ries.
There is still the problem of 'Popula-
tion Explosion', 'Waste', 'Unemployment',
‘Inflation' and many others which are
bound to affect development efforts of
developing countries, adversely.
Hopeful Signs:
Yet during these development decades,
we pin our hopes from the many facets
of contributions in the form of 'Green
Revolution', ‘Managerial Revolution' 'Ad-
vanced Technologies' and many areas of
research involving detail studies of resour-
ces on land, in the water, and in the air
so that through the application of the
findings of these researches, human lives.
can be directed towards more meaningful
existence and at the same time, abundantly
enriched.
Each country, I am sure, in its own
way, is planning and implementing its
development plans. Mobilization of re-
sources within the country and from
abroad is continuing being carried out.
Developed countries form themselves into
groupings or clubs for special efforts to
assist certain developing countries. The
International Financial Organizations,
either on a world-wide basis or on a
regional basis attempt to make all-out
efforts to help in the growth efforts of
the developing countries. Even among
the developing countries themselves, assis-
tance in one form or the other is being
extended for mutual benefits. Non-govern-
mental bodies such as the charitable and
religious organizations also join in to
make the Second U.N. Development
Decade a success.
However, in recent conference of
the United Nations Industrial Develop-
ment Organization (UNIDO) held in Lima,
some of the spokesmen of the Third World
have pin-pointed the reasons for their
stagnation to capitalism and colonialism.
They have urged the Western nations to give
more assistance to the developing nations,

While the developing countries are
looking forward to growth and develop-
ment during the decades, and perhaps
also during the decades to come, there are
some who view the future with pessisism.
Among those are very eminent Scientists,
Scholars, and Technicians, who predicted
that industrial growth efforts with the
accompanying population growth will
result in resource depletion and in the
near future the limits to growth on this
planet will be reached. These findings
which are recorded in the publications,
namely (1) 'A Blue print for Survivaľ
signed by some 30 eminent British scien-
tists, and (2) 'The Limits to Growth'
brought out by a team of scientists at the
Massachusetts Institute of Technology,
USA warned the human race to slow down
in industrial growth and also the growth
of population. They advocated the im-
plementation of both zero population
as
growth and zero economic growth,
solution to the eventual destruction of the
human race should the present growth
trends in world population, industrializatin,
pollution, food production and resource
depletion continue unchanged.
Can this idea be accepted by the
developing countries, which are just on
the threshold of launching towards more
rate of
industrialization and reasonal
population growth as further step towards
improvement in the social standing of
their peoples? The retreat from the posi-
tion of positive achievement to zero is
indeed unthinkable, for although much
credit should be given to these prophets
of doom for reminding the human society
to be careful in the management of the
resour es, I believe in the ability of our
planet to accommodate our requirements,
which must of necessity change from time
to time to be in line with the existing
conditions.
In conclusion, let me quote the opti-
mistic opinion of Mr. Paul C. Hoffman
who served for 13 years as the Admini-
strator of the UN Development Programme
and its predecessor, the UN Special Fund:
"On the bassis of many discussions
with my fellow members from the develop-
ing countries, I came to believe strongly
that the underlying cause of poverty in
the low-income nations was not a lack of
resources. Rather it was a failure to realize
the potentials of the natural wealth and
human talents at the disposal of these
countries. It seemed to me, therefore,
that there was urgent need for a
large-scale programme specifically designed
to help the developing nations make
progressively better use of their vast latent
power to speed their own progress."
