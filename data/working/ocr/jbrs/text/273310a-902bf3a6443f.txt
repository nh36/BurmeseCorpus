EASTWARD: THE STORY OF ADONIRAM JUDSON.-By Stacy
R. Warburton (New York City, Round Table Press, $2.50).
Dr Warburton's authoritative life of Judson supplies a long felt
want. As ne points out in his preface, for a long time there has been no
standard biography of the pioneer of the American Baptist Mission in
print, for it is over fifty years since Edward Judson published his
Life. Dr. Warburton has gone in considerable detail into the early,
pre-Burma, days of Judson's career, and gives a very useful picture of
the environment in which he grew up and of the circumstances which
shaped his choice of a career. While there is perhaps little that is
altogether new in the book, a good deal of interesting detail is included,
and, what is more important, it is very useful to have in print so
careful a study of one of the great figures of Burma's history. There
are a few statements in the book which might be corrected in a second
edition. There were not two Roman Catholic churches within the actual
city of Rangoon (p. 63); there appear to have been one church within
the stockade and two outside. It is a pity to repeat the legend that the
original Baptist Mission house in Rangoon was near the execution
ground (p. 64); the English Baptists lived originally within the stockade
while the execution ground was outside. The new Mission House was not

unfinished when Judson arrived in Rangoon (p. 64); it was already
completed. What is the authority for the statement that Nestorian
Christians were reported to be in Pegu in the sixth century (p. 64)?
It may be so, but is there any evidence? This is a matter of some
importance; it is true that the Nestorian Christians were in China at
about that time, but one cannot conclude from that circumstance that
they were also in Pegu. Chinese connections were at that period with
Upper Burma, through which the route to India lay, and not with
Lower Burma. Their presence in Pegu cannot be presumed; and is
there any actual evidence for the suggestion? It is not by any means
correct to state that there were no printed books in Burmese when
Judson commenced learning the language (p. 65). The Romans had
printed Burmese books from 1776 onwards, and a certain amount had
already been printed by the English Baptists at Serampore. I doubt
whether the Mission House compound at any time fronted on the
Pagoda Road (p. 76) ; it lay nearer to the Wungyi's Road than to the
great Pagoda Road, and even so it does not appear to have been
actually contiguous with the former. Laird, who was one of Judson's
companions during his imprisonment, was not arrested and brought to
Ava when war threatened (p. 91); he had been arrested and sent to Ava
in irons before the probability of war appeared, and his arrest was not
due to political circumstances, as Dr. Warburton seems to imply, but to
a dispute into which he had entered with the officials and certain Euro-
pean merchants of Rangoon (see Bengal Secret and Political Consulta-
tions, 5th April 1827, No. 46). Dr. Warburton rightly mentions (p.65)
the assistance which Judson derived from the work on the Burmese
language already carried out by Felix Carey, to whom some of the
previous biographers of Judson have been neither generous nor just ;
but he is surely somewhat extravagant in his praise of Judson's know-
ledge of the language. He is himself aware of the danger of exaggera-
tion in this regard (pp. 155, 156), but yet he states that Judson
"became a master of the Burmese, its rich vocabulary, its peculiar
idioms, its niceties of phrase, its literary and colloquial forms Before
his return to America he had become much more at home in Burmese
than in English.....He dug deep into the Burmese until he knew that as
well as any Burman scholar". Such a claim cannot be substantiated ;
while Judson's knowledge of the language was remarkable, to claim
that he was as familiar with it as any Burman scholar will not do at all.
How many Europeans have there ever been who could make such a
claim? If Dr. Warburton had consulted (as every writer on Burma
should consult) the files of this Journal, he would have been had the
advantage of reading an article in Volume I, which might have modera-
ted his eulogium. It is no service to Judson's memory to advance

valuable work which he did in fact do. Dr. Warburton's translitera-
tions of names are somewhat inconsistent; the form Maung Ing is a
little odd. Maloun is a place-name hardly recognisable to-day in that
form. The use of the term Peguan for Mon or Talaing is archaic.

Burmese for flower. But these difficulties nearly all arise from the
circumstance that Dr. Warburton had to write his book without having
had any personal acquaintance with Burma or the Burmese language;
and when that is borne in mind the only ground for surprise is that the
errors are so few. Dr. Warburton has produced a very useful piece of
work on which he may be sincerely congratulated.
