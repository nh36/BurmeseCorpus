A. Brief Survey of Evolution of Planning Mechanism in Burma
1. Economic planning was written into the Constitution of Independent Burma.
Section 41 of the Union Constitution provides that “the economic life of the Union shall
be planned with the aim of increasing the public wealth, of improving the material
conditions of the people and raising their cultural level, of consolidating the independence
of the Union and strengthening its defensive capacity". In fact, even prior to Indepen-
dence, some foundation for planning process and machinery had been laid. A National
Rehabilitation Conference to discuss the problems of rehabilitation of the Burmese
economy to its pre-war level was called in mid 1947. Simultaneously in the governmental
machinery proper, a National Planning Board was created, to be serviced by the National
Planning Department set up earlier in 1946 for the primary task of rehabilitating the towns
and cities of Burma destroyed during the War.
2. The Member for Planning of the Governor's Executive Council became chairman
of the Board while the Members for Agriculture and Rural Economy, and for Industry
and Labour sat on the Board as members. Also included in the membership of the Board
were the Planning Adviser, three non-official members and the Secretary of the National
Planning Department, the last mentioned person also acting as Secretary to the Board.
The functions of the Board were (1) to survey the economic resources of the country and
to draw an overall plan, (2) to prepare an integrated Development Plan for Burma, (3) to
decide questions of priority between different schemes, (4) to convert the development
plans of each department or sub-committee into a territorial plan for each region or
district and (5) to supervise the preparation and creation of the development plans of each
department or sub-committee. The National Planning Board held one meeting after which
the State assassinations rendered it impossible to convene more meetings or to do any
effective work. It was also found that under the circumstances the Ministers did not have
the time to devote the necessary attention to the work of the National Planning Board
which would of necessity have to undertake much detailed investigation and give
continuous consideration to matters placed before it. It was therefore decided to set up a
small expert body and entrust it with the sole task of evolving a plan or plans for the
economic development of the country.

3. Thus the Economic Planning Board came into being in late 1947 or early 1948.
Headed by the Minister for National Planning, the Board's membership consisted of
Planning and Finance Secretaries, the Planning and Economic Advisers and two non-
official members. At the executive level, they were assisted by three outstanding economic
officials. The Economic Planning Board was specifically charged with the formulation of
a restricted Two Year Plan of Economic Development. This they did within a few months
and announced the Plan on April 1, 1948.
4. With the completion of the task of drawing up the Two Year Plan and in view
of the national importance of the planning function, the Prime Minister took charge of the
portfolio of the Planning Ministry and assumed the presidency of the Board which
was renamed the National Planning Board. In May 1948 the National Planning Board
was re-organized with the aim of creating a board-based, representative body capable
of reflecting public opinion generally and of enlisting the support of all sections
of the people for its plans. It was assigned the duty to survey the resources
of the country, to evolve plans for its economic development and to co-ordinate,
supervise and superintend the activities of Ministries in the execution of plans. The
Board was headed by the Prime Minister as President, and consisted of the Ministers
for Finance and Revenue, Agriculture and Forests, Commerce, Supply and Transport,
Mines and Industry; the Planning and Economic Advisers; the Secretary, Ministry of
National Planning; a representative each of the four racial minorities and four non-officials
appointed by the Governmnnt. Two factors militated against the effective working of the
expanded National Planning Board. One was the unwieldy size of the Board and the
other was the disturbed condition of the country. The latter particularly rendered
impossible the implementation of an overall economic plan and the Board had to content
itself with economic projects and decisions of an essentially ad hoc nature. The Board
soon became defunct and remained merely as an accomodation centre for statistical,
economic and industrial research workers.
5. In order to take over some of the functions of the defunct National Planning
Board, an Economic Council was constituted under the chairmanship of the Minister of
Industry and Commerce and consisting of the Minister of Co-operatives, a Minorities
Minister, the Planning Adviser and five non-official members prominent in the field of
industry and finance. The most important functions and duties of the Council were to
formulate and submit to Government plans and advice and recommendations in economic
matters and to submit plans and programmes for the nationalization of industries. Its
meetings, which were held twice a month on the average were usually presided over by the
Prime Minister. Ministers and official representatives of the Ministries and Departments
concerned with the subject under consideration were also invited to these meetings. The
fluidity and diversity of the composition of the Council made it a singularly appropriate
clearing house for economic policies and plans. Furthermore, since the Prime Minister
and his major colleagues were usually present at the meetings, the recommendations of the
Council were almost invariably accepted subsequently by the Cabinet.

planning machinery. After the resignation of the Deputy Secretary in the Ministry of
National Planning who was de facto chief planner in the Government, the Planning

Secretary who was concurrently holding the post of Finance Secretary, resumed effective
control of the planning machinery. The combination of treasury and planning functions
in one hand at the official level no doubt expedited the growth of the planning machinery
and process. In the first half of 1951, the Planning Ministry received a group of economists
from the University of Oxford who in a relatively short time did some ground-work for
planning in the way of national income estimation and the publication of the first
Economic Survey of Burma and of Conference Papers on Current Economic Problems of
Burma, 1951.
7. Economic planning entered a further stage when the Government engaged in
late 1951 with ECA aid a group of American engineers and economists to conduct a
two-year economic and engineering survey of Burma. On the recommendation of the
ECA, an engineering firm from New York was engaged, the Knappen Tippetts Abbet
(known in short as KTA and later severally as KTAM and TAMS) which in turn engaged
as their mining consultants, the Pierce Management, Inc. of Scranton, Pennsylvania, and
as their economic consultants the Robert R. Nathan Associates Inc. of Washington D.C.
8. On the eve of a comprehensive development programme the Government decided
to re-organize the planning organization in Burma. The Ministry of National Planning
was re-organized (1) to provide the various services pre-requisite to planning and (2) to
perform the central planning function. For the former a Directorate of Statistics and
Economic Survey (now known as the Central Statistical and Economics Department) was
created out of the statistical and economic staff of the National Planning Board. The
Census Department was also placed under the control of the Planning Ministry until
recently. For the latter function, a Social Planning Commission and an Economic
Planning Commission each comprising four full-time members and two ex-officio members
were to be established to initiate, stimulate and co-ordinate planning in their respective
field. (While the Social Planning Commission later with the assistance of a United
Nations Mission produced a Social Development Plan for Burma, the Economic Planning
Commission was never fully staffed and the economic planning function was in effect
undertaken by the American economists and engineers and their national counterparts).
9. At the same time, an Economic and Social Board, chairmanned by the Prime
Minister and placed under the administrative control of the Ministry of the Prime Minister
was created in supersession of the Economic Council (1) to supervise the execution of
development projects by the various Ministries and Departments and (2) to give advice on
economic and social policy for the Union Government. To assure co-ordination of
activities, the Minister of National Planning served as Deputy Chairman of the Board
while the chairmen of the Social and Economic Planning Commissions, the Economic
and Social Advisory Council and the Union Bank were its other members.
10. The Board has two major functions, namely, (1) the supervision of development
projects, involving the three functions of (a) progress reporting on development projects,
(b) inspection of development projects and (c) adaptation of priorities in the execution of
development projects; and (2) the co-ordination of broad economic and social policy of the

Government. For the former the Board was to be assisted by a supervisory staff and for
the latter by an Economic and Social Advisory Council including ministers, officials,
representatives of the central bank, chairmen of the planning commissions and individuals
prominent in the fields of industry, commerce and finance. Actually the representation on

the Government. A senior minister served as Minister of National Planning while the
Economic and Social Board was chairmanned by the Prime Minister. Other members of
the Board were the key economic Ministers and the Chairman of the Union Bank. Later
the entire Cabinet was invited to meetings. High officials concerned with the agenda items
almost always attended the meetings.
11. The planning machinery continued in this form until carly 1956 when the Prime
Minister took over the planning portfolio which became vacant. Another change was in
March 1957, when the planning portfolio and the chairmanship of the Economic and
Social Board were retained by the outgoing Prime Minister who now became Deputy
Premier for Defence and Planning. In October 1957, however, the Prime Minister resumed
the control of these two economic agencies. In the meantime at the official level, after the
death of the Executive Secretary of the Economic and Social Board, the Planning Secretary
had been concurrently holding the chief executive position of the two agencies. In
February 1958, the Prime Minister ordered the amalgamation of the Ministry of National
Planning and the Economic and Social Board into a Prime Minister's Office for National
Planning.
12. The Prime Minister's Office for National Planning was composed of a National
Planning Commission and a service organization, the latter to constitute a de facto minis-
try of the Prime Minister. The office was to exercise the combined functions of the
Planning Ministry and Economic and Social Board, namely, the functions of a central
planning authority, the highest supervisory and co-ordinating body in the field of technical
and economic development and the conduct of operations as well as the highest advisory
body to the cabinet on broad issues of development policy. The National Planning Com-
mission consisted of eight senior ministers, one junior minister and three key economic
officials.
13. In the exercise of its functions, the National Planning Commission was to be
assisted by a Committee of Advisers (successor to the former Committee of Experts) com-
posed of 14 high economic officials and 2 university professors. Functions of the Com.
mittee of Advisers were to process technical papers submitted to the Commission, suggest
and recommend on appropriate business of the Commission, and generally advise and
assist the Commission in the discharge of its duties. The Committee has the power to
invite to its deliberations persons prominent in the fields related to its work.
14. The Standing Committee for Supervision and Co-ordination was to continue to
exist and function in the same manner as it did under former Economic and Social Board.
The Standing Committee was comprised of 6 key economic officials.

15. The duties and functions of the Standing Committee were to inspect and oversee
the working of government economic agencies particularly the Boards and Corporations
from the point of view of rectitude and efficiency.
16. The Administration offices of the former Planning Ministry and the Economic
and Social Board were merged. The offices of the former Planning Ministry dealing with
the Economic Aid Committee, United Nations, Colombo Plan, Ford Foundation and the
Special Projects Implementation Board were to continue to function as a Foreign Aid Plan
Liaison Centre. Pyidawtha Projects were to continue to be administered as under the former
Planning Ministry, while the Sub-Committee on Foreign Exchange Control in the former
Economic and Social Board would continue to function under its director as a Sub-
Committee of the National Planning Commission.
17. The Social Planning Commission was to continue to exist and function in the
same manner as it did under the former Ministry of National Planning. (It was abolished
later in early 1958). The Economic Planning Commission in the former Ministry of
National Planning was abolished. Also under the Ministry was Central Statistical and
Economics Department, which also included a Census Office. The CSED administers
the Central Statistical Authority, acts as a source of economic intelligence and services
Government economic committees within and without the Planning Ministry.
18. Another Government committee which may also be considered a part of the
planning machinery was the Budget Committee which was appointed in early 1957 to do
the budget examination work for the Minister of Finance and Revenue. The Committee
was chairmanned by Chief Secretary and consisted of 2 advisers and 8 high economic
officials. The Central Statistical and Economics Department and the Economic Consultant
group served as staff to the Committee.
19. When the Caretaker Government came into power, the National Planning port-
folio was retained by the Prime Minister but the National Planning Commission was held
in abeyance since the Cabinet, being of a much smaller size, could function as Planning
Commission by itself. The official Advisory Committee was replaced by a Budgetary
Allocation Committee composed of four high military officials and four high civil officials.
The functions of the new advisory committee were to assist the Cabinet to (a) anticipate,
watch and suggest means of dealing with important questions involving economic policy,
especially those which owing to their generality or novelty extend beyond the sphere of
any department, (b) give connected forethought to economic, social and technical matters
including production, trade and prices, (c) explore and determine realistic levels of domes-
tic and foreign financial resources and expenditures, (d) consolidate, reconcile, adapt or
supplement the programmes and projects of the Government, (e) observe the progress of
the implementation of the Government programmes and institute measures to overcome
difficulties, (f) foster and encourage appropriate measures for the conservation of human
and material resources of the Union. The CSED served as staff to the Committee. The
Committee also discharged the function of the Standing Committee for Supervision and
Co-ordination and the Budget Committee. The latter was serviced jointly by the CSED
and the recently established Budget Division of the Ministry of Finance and Revenue.

20. Just before announcing the Second Four Year Plan for the Union of Burma, the
Union Party Government re-organized the National Planning Commission, in July 1961,
which had been revived earlier when it assumed power a year ago, to give a wider repre-
sentation to the racial minorities on the Commission so as to secure planning on a Union-
wide basis. At the same time it added a small but high-powered Development Projects
and Operations Supervision Commission charged with the detailed supervision of the
implementation of the development plan.
21. After the Revolutionary Government came into power in March 1962, a Cabinet
Sub-committee known as the National Planning and Budgetary Allocation Committee was
formed. A member of the Committee, the Minister for Trade and Industry, in turn chair-
mans an Executive Committee composed of a number of high economic officials of the
Government.
B.
Some Generalizations on Location, Size and Composition of Planning Machinery
22. It will be noticed from the foregoing that the planning portfolio in the Burmese
Government had been held for most of the time by the Prime Minister himself. Even at
times when the Prime Minister was too pre-occupied with other matters to hold the port-
folio himself, it was almost invariably entrusted to a senior and trusted member of his
Cabinet. This was even more true of the chairmanship of the supervisory and co-ordina-
ting body in the economic field whenever it existed separately from the planning body.
Planning, and even more so, implementation of the plan assumes team-work and discipline
which can be enforced only by the captain or at least a very senior member of the team.
Being a central co-ordinating ministry like the Ministry of Finance, having no specific
executive responsibility and yet claiming most fields of governmental activity as its own
area of jurisdiction, a planning department is almost always resented by executive depart-
ments. Only the authority of the Prime Minister can counteract or over-rule this natural
resentment.
23. With regard to the size of the planning commission, it has varied from as few
as 6 or 7 to as many as 16. Generally, the number has been large under political
governments and has tended to be small under non-political governments or when it has
been charged with specific functions of limited scope of duration. The actual number of
persons attending the meetings was much larger since officials concerned were almost
always invited to the discussion of relevant items on the agenda. Since economic discussion
usually requires the use of statistical data and does not lend itself to emotional oratory,
smaller numbers have been found more effective than large meetings which tend either to
develop into a forum for the eloquent or else break up into a number of small discussion
circles.
24. An analysis of the composition of successive planning bodies in the Burmese
Government reveals two important points. One is that the practice of non-official repre-
sentation on the planning commission was dropped after a few early years. The other is
that the representation of racial minorities on the planning body has been sporadic, based

on the assumption that the States will do their own planning. It was only as late as 1961,
when it was actually too late, that their representation was formalized with the aim of
planning on a Union-wide basis. On the official advisory committees which did most of
the groundwork for the higher bodies to which they report, representation was dominated
by secretaries to the government. While attempts to bring in advice from the academic
world had always originated from the ministerial quarters they have usually been met with
reluctant acceptance by the officials.
C. Relations with the Cabinet
25. Because successive Burmese planning commissions have almost always been
headed by the Prime Minister or a very senior minister and have included in their member-
ship key economic ministers, there had been no difficulties in their relations with the
Cabinet. Almost invariably, their economic decisions had been accepted by the Cabinet
while only those with dominant political overtones were further discussed in the Cabinet.
On the contrary, failure of the Cabinet to define its social objectives had rendered difficult
the task of plan formulation and policy advice on the part of the officials of the planning
commission.
D. Relations with Finance Ministry
26. Since economic plans have to be fitted into the budget at the beginning of each
financial year except perhaps in the very first year of the plan period, working relations of
the planning ministry with the ministry of finance are at once intimate and delicate. For
a good number of years a partial solution was found in joint determination of available
resources and permissible expenditures and in assigning the capital budget formulation to
the planning ministry while the finance ministry continued to take care of the current budget
and the revenue sources. On account of unavoidable additional staff requirements for
new capital projects, there were cases of inadequate coordination between the two agencies.
The Treasury practice of pre-audit control of expenditures, particularly by a standing
parliamentary finance committee, also contributed to the problems of co-ordination. In
later years, in recognition of these difficulties the device of an inter-departmental budget
committee was used. Still later, treasury and planning portfolios tended to be combined
in one hand at the ministerial level.
E. Relations with other Government Departments
27. The first two plans of Burma, the Two Year Plan and the Eight Year Plan, were
formulated centrally. Particularly in the case of the latter, even the engineering work was
centralized at the planning ministry. Since excessive centralization produced resentment
by the implementing agencies, let alone inducing a sense of participation and enthusiasm,
planning work was decentralized in the formulation of the later two plans, namely, the
First and Second Four Year Plans. Shortage or lack of engineering personnel at the
planning ministry as well as the growing acquaintance of executive agencies with the
process of planning no doubt expedited the decentralization.

28. Planning and supervisory functions were at first vested in distinct entities of the
Ministry of National Planning and the Economic and Social Board. However, the difficulty
of separating the two functions once the development programme gets underway, the
overlapping of personnel and the resultant confusion led the Government to merge the
two bodies into one single National Planning Commission some years later. Still later, in
order to remedy weakness in implementation, a separate supervisory body was revived in
the form of Development Projects and Operations Supervision Commission although this
body never got around to actual working. Under the Revolutionary Government,
supervisory functions, at least in the industrial field, are entrusted to individual officials
rather than to a committee as a whole. This may prove to be the most effective form of
supervision since field inspection by persons with authority is usually much more effective
than a whole committee poring over outdated progress reports on paper.
29. Planning and implementation functions have always been kept separate in Burma
apart from a few lapses such as when the implementation of the Soviet Gift Projects was
entrusted to the Planning Ministry. Particularly where collaboration with foreign agencies
is required, association of the Planning Ministry, in virtue of its possession of central
economic intelligence, in the planning and preliminary phases of development projects may
be desirable, as in the case of the Chinese Loan Board.
F. Staffing of Planning Secretariat
30. While exclusively national staff was used for the formulation of the first plan
(Two Year Plan) and the last plan (Second Four Year Plan), the preparation of the second
plan (Eight Year Plan) was largely entrusted to American engineering and economic con-
sultants while the third plan (First Four Year Plan) was a joint effort between foreign and
national planning staff. One lesson to be learnt about the use of national staff is that
there should be inter-change of personnel with other government departments so as to
promote mutual understanding of work processes and points of view. The lesson about
the use of foreign staff is that personnel from academic institutions or dis-interested
governments would have been more useful than commercial firms who acquire vested
interests and tend to work to perpetuate their contracts.
