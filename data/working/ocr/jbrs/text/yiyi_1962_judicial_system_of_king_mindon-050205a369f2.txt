Most of these parapuik are about seventeen inches by six inches and they vary
from a single folio to about sixty-four. Parapuik last at most for about one hundred and
fifty years so our parapuik are mostly from the latter half of the nineteenth century.
Parapuik was used for making drafts, notes, etc. on everyday matters. Transcribing on
palm ieaf is equivalent to our printing. Sometimes if the parapuik has more pages than
necessary the surplus pages are used for making drafts of letters, orders, private notes on
loans and a multitude of other matters. Generally these are in running or cursive hand
which is very hard to decipher. Perseverence however is necessary as they tend to yield
extremely valuable information which is sometimes the only material on certain mattcrs
because even if fair copies had been made they are now no longer available.
For historical purposes most of the information gained from the 300 parapuik
already studied is on the subject of administration in its various aspects mostly confined
to the reign of King Mindon.

King Mindon was very much concerned about bribery and corruption. In the time
of his predecessor (King Pagan) these vices had been rife and the people suffered great hard-
ships. By corruption we mean the taking of presents, fees in excess of those laid down in
the regulations, extortion, exaction, etc. He wanted to stamp out these evils. As a first
step in this direction he issued a number of orders which states the fees to be taken in
each specific case. We will deal with them fully later. A few years hence Mindon
realised that in spite of his orders bribery and corruption was still rampant. He therefore
ordered some of his amat to draft a number of regulations aimed at suppressing bribery
and corruption. These amat were:
Pukhan: kri: mrui, ca wankri: ပုခန်းကြီးမြို့စားဝန်ကြီး

What constituted bribery and corruption? The taking of money or presents from
relatives and servitors even though they may want nothing at the moment, from princes
whether they want favours or not, from the queens, amat, chief of groups and the people
in connection with favours from officials in connection with appointments and transfers,
from contestants in criminal and civil suits, from revenue collectors, from people in
connection with public works and embezzling of government funds and property.
Everyone in the King's service as well as their families were bound by the orders
passed by the Pukham: Wankrī: and his colleagues. Everyone whether male or female
when found guilty of corruption would have to reimburse twice the value taken. In addi-
tion to this punishments were laid down which varied from rank to rank, the amount taken
and the number of offences.
First of all we will deal with the personnel of the Lhwattoau. For taking up to
ten ticals of silver or the value thereof if the culprit is an amat of the lowest order he shall
suffer one month's detention. If he is an amat of the middle grade he shall suffer fifteen
days detention and if he be an amat of the highest order he only reimburses twice the
amount taken in front of the Lhwattoau in full session. If it is a woman member of the
family who is guilty she shall suffer one month's detention at the Nokrum:
(နောက်ရုံး) and if it be

The penalty for taking more than ten ticals of silver or the equivalent is naturally

one month's detention. If it is a member of the family who is guilty, if it is a woman she

Provincial officials great and small such as mrui, wan (os), buil (is), cackai
(6), mrui, up (6), nakhaṁ (5), mrui, care: (:) etc. as well as their fami-
lies were all subjected to the Regulations for Bribery and Corruption. The punishment
for these officials is the same as those for the amat of the lowest grade. The penalties for
clerks in the district is less severe than for those in the capital. Perhaps this is so because
the provincial clerk has less opportunity to be corrupt or because the Lhwattoau was more
anxious about corruption in the capital. For taking up to ten ticals of silver they get five
strokes and for more than ten ticals ten strokes. Thus their punishment is only half of
that suffered by their counterparts in the capital.

This undoubtedly meant a certain degree of specialization and therefore aided efficiency.

have regular salaries. The clerks too would be paid out of the royal treasury. Therefore
Mindon said there was no reason for them to make exactions or practise extortion. Thus
they were not to take any presents from the contestants nor were they to delay cases.

An analysis of the above order gives us the following facts. Before Mindon's time
civil cases were decided by the local administrative officers who took large fees and did
not have a sound knowledge of law. Moreover, they had little time for settling civil
cases which were time consuming. Thus the people suffered. When Mindon tried to
correct this state of affairs by prescribing a schedule of judicial fees the provincial
officers did not abide by it. To curb this evil twin measures were taken. Of these the
first was the separation of civil from judicial duties. The second was the appointment of
the kwam bhui: thin :- · custodian of judicial fees, to collect and keep the judicial fees
earned. According to the new order, if they had not been provided for by the King they
were to receive a salary of fifteen ticals of silver per mensem. In practice, however,
they seem to have received twenty. If the King could send his personal retainers as
kwam: bhui: thin: one wonders whether he was providing them with a shortcut to wealth.
For those who were appointed in the normal way Mindon intended each branch of the
administration to act as a check and balance to the others. Thus the mrui, wan was
ordered to see that the kwam: bhui : thin: as well as the judge abided by the rules and
regulations. Later when we deal with the judges we will see that they were asked to keep
an eye on the mrui, wan and his officials. The next step after the appointment of kwam :
bhui : thin: was the appointment of judges which had been hinted at in the above order.

It was suggested that the following appointments would facilitate the settlement of
civil suits in the Lhwattoau. They were four tară: cac care: kri: (09)

the Tara: rum: ( တရားရုံး ) four tara: nakha ( တရားနာခံ ) and four tarā; care: ( တရားစာရေး)

judges with two clerks each should be appointed one to each quarter of the city (c)
and for the districts one hundred judges with two clerks each. Thus the hearing of suits
would be facilitated.2
Cases brought before the Lhwattoau, Tarā: rum: judges of the four quarters and
district judges were to be examined at first by the tară: cac (06) i.e. examiner who
was to record the plaintiff's case with relevant dates, deeds, papers, tax assessment num-
bers, revenue depositions, accounts and whatever was relevant to the case. Then he had
to summon the defendant instructing him to come on a certain date bringing all evidence
pertaining to the case. When the defendant arrived everything pertinent to the case had
to be examined without delay and the evidence submitted by both parties heard imme-
diately. Whenever necessary the contenstants may obtain true copies of cactam: (06:)
etc., from the Rhwetuik (Treasury).
In appeal cases, the case history – the names of the plaintiff and defendant, the
prosecution and the defence, the evidence and witnesses offered must be submitted
by the original examiner.

In cases where witnesses were necessary a party must provide more than the three
stated by law. He may call as many as fifteen. If the rival contestant can cross examine
successfully and discredit the evidence of witnesses he may do so. It is on the evidence of
the remaining witnesses that the case will be decided. If the rival contestant is able to
discredit all the witnesses the first party will not be permitted to produce more witnesses.
If only one witness remains it would be regarded as though the first party could not pro-
duce witnesses. If two witnesses remain and their testimony agree with that of the party
who called them the party may swear in more witnesses. If more than three witnesses
remain the best three should be chosen for examination. A witness should not be ques-
tioned on the truth of a certain statement made by the contestant who calls him but be
questioned on all possible points of the case 1.
When a defendent fails to appear before the court on the day summoned in original
or appeal cases he should be detained in the lockup for three days. For every day of his
absence after the date that he should appear at court he will be fined 5 kyap in cases of
patrimony (pet), 1 kyap per person robbed in robbery cases, and in others one percent
of the value of the case must be given to the other party as food allowance.
Three days after the arrival of both parties all documents relevant to the case
should be ready. The tara: cac must finish examining these in three days. If for no
satisfactory reason the tară: cac fails to do so he shall be sent to the lockup where he must
still carry out his duties. Should there be negligence or carelessness in the examination of
documents and witnesses pertaining to a case the tară: cac will be detained for three days
at the lockup where he must still cary on his work. If the tara: cac delays the case be-
cause of bribery, ill will or favouritism he will be dismissed with royal permission and for
bribery he will be punished according to the Bribery Act.
Officials who do not use the Regulations of 1873 as their guide in settling civil
suits will be punished as follows: For the first offence sevendays in the lockup, for the
second offence seven days imprisonment, and for the third offence dismissal from the
service with the royal permission. When heads of groups, etc. who are not officials but
have the authority to settle civil cases do not abide by the Regulations of 1873 for every
offence they will be given ten lashes that will draw blood.

As the first step in this he issued schedules of fees to be taken in civil and criminal cases.
Then came the Bribery Act which was aimed at offenders on all levels of the services. As
he did not receive all the judicial fees collected, some of which disappeared into the
pockets of officials, the office of kwam: bhui: thin: was created to collect the judicial fees.
To improve the method of settling civil cases he had a number of kuṁ ( 5 ) judges
appointed throughout the realm. Even then civil cases dragged on. So to expedite the
decisions of cases more judges, magistrates, tară: cac, etc. were appointed. Then we have
Thibaw's orders which caused a lot of changes in Mindon's set up. How successful was
Mindon in his attempt to suppress bribery and corruption. He could not have been too

able feature of these regulations was the comparative harshness of penalties for officials of
the lower grades as compared to those in the higher places. It would almost seem
