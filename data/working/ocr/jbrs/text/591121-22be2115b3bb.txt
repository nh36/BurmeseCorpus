Palace Chronicle were the Pyu, Kanyan and Thet. We now know a lot
about the Pyu (O.B. pyū, Chinese p'iao). During the 6th to 7th century
A.D. they entered Burma, probably from North Laos in the east, occupied
the plains of North Burma as far as Halin in the west, and
Irawady valley as far as śri Kṣetra (Old Prome) in the south, and also the

time ruled much of North Burma from their capital at Tagaung (Takon) on
the river above Mandalay. But we know next to nothing about the Kayan
(O.B. Kamram, Kamlam), except that they certainly existed-possibly in the
south-eastern hills and plains.

Another mention of these "Kamram slaves called
monastery" and dedicated "with all their ricefields", occurs in the last
of Prince Rājasūra's inscriptions (Pl. V 37634), still at the site north of the
Shwe Chaung. It is a large two-faced stone; and the mention comes
in the 4th line from the end of the reverse. Apart from ten Pali lines at the
beginning of the obverse, it consists of two long lists of slave-names, seemingly
Burmese. Perhaps the last 40 lines of the obverse may be the names of the Kamraṁ
slaves, now largely illegible.
One other possible mention of the name, here written Kamlam, occurs at
Plate III 309, line 33, in the four-faced Shwegwincha inscription, Taungbi village,
just above the river-bank north of Pagán city wall. The date is lost. The donor,
Gangabijey, was a minister probably of Klacwa, who reigned from 1235 to c. 1249
A.D. Gangabijey gives the most detailed list of dedications, big and small, to be
found in any Pagán inscription; but large portions of all four faces are now illegible.
The latter part of the second face gives a tantalizing list of textiles dedicated,
only legible in fragments. Thus in line 34 there is clear mention of Karyan tanmi
prók, “embroidered Karen smocks" - the unique mention of the word ‘Karen” in
Old Burmese. In the line above there is mention of Kamlaṁ pajan 18. I tako
pajań or pucań to mean the "pure cotton-cloth" of this and other inscriptions,
often coupled with pu-chuiw ('pahso', dyed cloth), pu-khrañ (modern 'löngyi'), etc.

In or about 638 A.D., Hsuan-tsang visited the "level country", Samataţa,
roughly Chittagong. It was his nearest point to Burma. He lists the Buddhist
countries beyond to the east: first Sri Ksetra; then, "to the south-east, in a
corner of the great sea, the kingdom of Kāmalanka”; then further east, Dvāravatī,
etc. It is generally admitted now that Dvāravati was the Old Mon kingdom of
Lower Siam (Ratburi, P'ra Pathom, Lopburi). If so, Kamalanka should surely have
been a Buddhist kingdom on the Burma side, around the head of the gulf of
Martaban. The Sanskritized Kāmalanka (Chinese in Chia-mo-
lang-chia) is fairly close in sound to kamraṁ/kaṁlam; and since no nearer
alternative is available, one may tentatively propose the identification. Outside
Hsuan-tsang, no other mention of Kāmalaṁka is reported.
In Burma, we are so used to think of Lower Burma as Rämañña, land of
the Mons (rmāñ), that we are apt to forget the fact that, while Siam in the 7th
century A.D. was certainly a Mon-dominated country, this was then not true of
Lower Burma. The oldest Mon inscription of Siam, at Vat P'o Rang, Nagara
Pathama, is dated by Coodès c.600 A.D. The earliest Mon inscriptions in Burma,
those at Thaton, hardly go back beyound the middle of the 11th century. And
though almost all are Buddhist, and indeed Hinayanist, there is a geographical, as
well as a cultural gap. The U T'ong copperplate inscription (see Quaritich Wales,
Dvāravati, 1964, pp, 20-25) is not Pāli Buddhist, but Sanskrit Saivite; and it is
close to the route leading from Dvāravatī to the Three Pagoda Pass into Burma.

The oldest (unedited) inscription in
Śrī Parameśvara, presumably a
Kawgun Cave (I.B. IV Pl. 355b) is that of King
Saiva; Dr. Barnett dated it 6th-7th century A.D. The same cave contains Burma's
finest stone relief of Vișņu Anantaśayana. At Thatôn itself only one bronze standing
image of the Buddha aatedates, I think, the 11th century. Previous to that, it seems,
it was known, not as "the city of the Good Law” (Sudhamma) but as Rakṣapura,
abode of the Malayan sea-pirates or Bhilū, who had ravaged this coast off and
on, ever since Soņa and Uttara encountered them in 253 B.C.
That was at Mt.Kelāsa, 30 miles north of Thatôn. The early Buddhist
area of Lower Burma starts from there and runs north to Old Pegu and beyond.
But Malayan settlements still clung to ‘Bhilu-gyun' at the mouth of the Sittaung;
and centuries passed before, at Zôkthôk (some 35 miles north of Thatôn) the Bhilū
cut off their "Ropes of Hair” and dedicated them to the great pagoda.
Hsüan-tsang, famous as a Buddhist champion all over India, no doubt had
early information about the spread of Buddhism in Further India that followed
the break-up of Fu-nan. He must have heard with joy of the coming of the Pyu
to Śrī Kṣetra, and the triumph of the Mons in Siam, both south (Lopburi) and
possibly north (Lamphun). He may have sought to foster links between them by
encouraging the movement towards Buddhist unity among the Kamlam. (Were they,
one wonders, an offshoot of the Northern Mon-Khmers?). But Kāmalanka did not
last long as a kingdom. In 675 A.D. or thereabouts, when I-tsing also visited
Samatața, his parallel list of Buddhist kingdoms beyond omits Kāmalanka. South-
east of Sri Kṣetra, he now places the kingdom of
