A PROBLEM OF HISTORICAL INTERPRETATION★
JOSEF SILVERSTEIN★★
It is a curious fact that, despite the extensive and varied literature available on
Burma and India, there is no study in existence which gives a comprehensive and satisfac.
tory description and analysis of the impact of the political unification of the two countries
on the lives of the peoples living in them. Scholars who have written about either or both
of these countries look upon the annexation of Burma to India as an unavoidable accident
of history which had relatively little effect on India and slightly more on Burma. For the
Burma historians, the most important aspect of unification was the political changes which
occurred under alien rule. Specifically, they interpret the political progress toward self-
rule in Burma prior to separation as a by-product of Indian advances. They call attention
to the effects of unlimited Indian migration upon the local economy and the rising nationa-
list movement. This historians who are oriented towards economics and social organization
give attention to the role of the immigrant Indians as laborers and money lenders and note
their effect upon the changing characteristics of Burma's economy. They also make
reference to the different religious and social ideas which were held by Burmese and
Indians and the racial conflicts which erupted. While these are significant results of
unification, there are not the whole story. There were many less obvious and dramatic
effects upon Burmese life, which, when more fully explored and understood may recast
the present interpretation of the whole period.
Consider the history of the railroad in Burma. It was introduced only after lower
Burma was annexed and it contributed greatly to social mobility and the development of
agriculture and mining. While its benefits went to Burma, its costs in capital investment
and unkeep were borne by India; when the two territories were separated in 1937, the
major portion of the financial debt Burma owed to India came from this source. The
railboard was intimately linked with unification and its history clearly reflects the impact
of India-Burma unity on the social and economic growth and development of Burma.
Yet, no study of the growth and influence of the railway has been made. A brief exami-
nation of its history will suggest some of its many implications on the modern history of

The history of the railway in Burma cannot be considered in isolation from railroad
development in Indta during the nineteenth and twentieth centuries: before 1900, Indian
railway policy passed through three distinct phases. Beginning in 1849 and lasting to
1869, railroad building and management was undertaken by private English companies
which were guaranteed a high fixed rate of interest. The policy failed to produce both the
trackage and the efficiently managed railways that had been anticipated. As a result, it
was replaced with a new policy which called for the Government to build all new lines
and gradually buy out the private companies when their contracts expired. This too,
proved to be less than ideal and beginning in 1882, and third policy was introduced which
allowed private firms to manage the Government-owned railways on a guaranteed interest
basis.! Because railroads were built to serve two purposes, defense and commerce, a
variety of gauges of track were laid and railway operation was not uniformly profitable in
India. By the end of the century, the Indian sub-continent had substantial railway lines
but did not have a single network of common track.
-
The first railway in Burma was constructed during the second period of railway
policy in India. The Government began construction with a line between Rangoon and
Prome a distance of 161 miles and opened it for service on May 1, 1877.2 Known
originally as the Irrawaddy State Railway, its name was changed to the Burma State
Railway. Between 1877 and 1896, 886 miles of track were laid.3 Unlike the practice
in India, the entire length of track in Burma was uniform narrow gauge. Initially, the
railway extended between key ports and towns in Lower Burma; after the third Anglo-
Burmese War, in 1885, the line was pushed northward to Myitkyina in Upper Burma. As
the outset, its primary purpose was military; after the Third Anglo-Burmese War it mainly
served commerce.
--
A great many details are lacking about this early period of the railroad in Burma.
From news items and letters in the leading Burmese newspaper - the Rangoon Gazette
Weekly Budget it is clear that many of the residents in that province of India were
troubled about the service on the line and the equipment in use. The origiginal engines
and rolling stock were purchased in Karachi from the Indus and Sutlej State Railway
when it was converted from narrow to wide gauge.4 Some of this equipment was used and
rebuilt before purchase; as a result, there were many complaints about the engines being
badly overage and that the State had paid more for rebuilt than it would have had to pay
for new equipment. Other complaints concerned frequent breakdowns and slow
service.5
During the period between 1877 and 1896, the Government invested approximately
800 lakhs Rupees or 4.7 million on British Pounds in developing the Burma rail network.
Despite complaints of the type noted above, the railway was widely used and showed a
profit every year after 1882. It gave an annual return on capital of from 1.3% in 1885 -
the year of the Third Anglo-Burmese War to a high of 5.43% in 1887. Average annual
return was 3.94%. On the basis of mileage of track and return on investment, the Burma

Regardless of local conditions in Burma, railway policy in India changed during the
last fifteen years of the nineteenth century and the Burma State Railway felt its effect.
During this time private British companies were formed under the stimulus of guaranteed
interest, to take over the management of existing Government railway property. In July
1896, rumors circulated in Rangoon to the effect that a private company was in the process
of formation to take over Burma's railway. The Rangoon Gazette Weekly Budget reported
on July 10 that the Rangoon Chamber of Commerce met and passed a resolution which
called upon the Government to make the terms of sale fully public before it was completed
"so that those so largely interested in the carrying arrangements of the country [may have
an opportunity of seeing that the trade will not be unduly effected by what appears to be
the granting of a strict monopoly to a private syndicate."7

that, the guarantee was lowered to 2%. In adddition, any surplus after all interest and

On September 1, 1896, the Burma Railway Company took over management of the
State line. Its official responsibilities, as outlined in its prospectus were "to undertake the
completion, construction and working of the existing lines." Both the Government and
the company were to provide new capital. If the company failed in its performance, the
Government had the right to buy it out prior to the termination of the contract; other-
wise, the agreement was to run for 25 years beginning on August 31, 1896; if the Govern-
ment did not exercise its options to buy out the company when the contract lapsed, it
could do so ten years later. The prospectus noted that the conditions of the company's
contract was similar to those established in the 1890 agreement between the Secretary of
State for India and the South India Railway Company.10

noticed in the British Parliament. There, the record shows that only one question was

II
From the changeover in 1896 to 1929, the Burma Railway Company managed the
lines with no interruptions. According to the Company's chairman -- in his final report to
the annual general meeting of the stockholders -- the line grew and prospered under Com-
pany direction. The original £ 2 million capitalization, was increased by an additional
one and one-quarter million in 1902 and one million more in 1909. After that date, all
new capital was supplied by the Government. With these funds, the length of the original
line was extended an additional 1,970 miles with 205 miles in double track. During the
period, the original main line was relaid with heavier rails to support new equipment.
On this lengthened and strengthened line, the company saw its freight increase from
1.2 million tons per year in 1896 to 5 million tons per year in 1928. The number of
passengers increased during the same period from 8.4 million to 27 million per year. In
pounds and shillings, this meant that the return to capital on an investment of £ 100
increased from £ 3-5-0 to £ 14.12
Behind these statistics lay a number of important facts. Internal tranquility
together with increased demand for Burma's raw materials expanded production and the
need for more transportation. The First World War provided the Government with an
excuse for establishing a rice export monopoly which continued into the early nineteen-
twenties. Out of the enormous profits it realized, the Government invested a large share
in the construction of railway feeder lines in Lower Burma.13 Thus, under both private
and state management, the Burma Railway grew and prospered with the province and its
record of development and profit was vastly different than that experienced by the railways
in the other provinces of India.
III
Following the end of World War One, numerous political changes in India took
place. In response to the British wartime pledge to gradually develop self-government in
India, the Government of India Act of 1919 created a bicameral legislature in which the
members of the lower house, the Legislative Assembly, enjoyed limited powers which they
exercised fully. At about the same time, Indian demands forced the Secretary of State
for India to appoint a committee of ten in November, 1920, to investigate the adminis-
tration and working of all Indian railways.14 These two developments combined to shape
the future of the Burma Railway Company.

The Railway Enquiry Committee of 1920 or the Acworth Committee was asked to
consider, among other questions, the relative advantages of the following methods of
management direct state management, management through a company domiciled in and
directed from London, management through a company domiciled and directed from
India, and management through a combination of the last two methods. In addition, the
Committee was asked to "advice as to the policy to be adopted as and when the existing
contracts with the several railway companies can be determined."15 Since the Burma
Railway Company was located in and directed from England and its contract was due
to lapse during the nineteen-twenties, it was one of the several railways considered by the
Committee.
It is not the place here to review the general body of the Report. The full Report
and several excellent commentaries are available. Rather, it is germane to consider certain
aspects of the Report which bear upon the general thesis of this eassy. Before considering
them, however, a few words need to be said about the Committee itself. The ten members
are usually described as able and well-informed on railway questions. The chairman,
Sir William Acworth, was recognized as a railway management expert. Prior to accepting
his assignment he had written an important study criticizing state management of railways.
The other nine members, included railway directors and officials, engineers, a banker and
two Indian legislators.16
<<
From the point of view of Burma much of the Report is irrelevant. According to
it, the defects in the combined private management-state ownership are due primarily to
the failure of the Government to provide the railway with adequate funds for capital expen-
ditures on development and extensions and even for the essential operations of renewal
and repairs."17 While this may have been true elsewhere, it was not the case, as noted
earlier, in Burma.
Or consider the extensive section of the Report which discussed Indian public
opinion and the Indianization of the railways. The Report declared that the Committee
attached great importance to Indian public opinion because "it is with money secured on
Indian taxation that the Indian Railways have been almost entirely built. It is the Indian
public that uses the railway and pays the rates and fares."'18 Again, while all this may be
true for India proper, it did not take account of the Burmese who used and paid the fares
on the railway in their own Province. On the question of Indianization, the Report
included a statistical analysis of the number of Indians and their job classification on all
railroads in British India, including the Burma Railway Company. In Burma, only three
of the 104 persons classified as officers were Indians. Since the remaining 101 were not
identified it is impossible to tell if any were Burmese or Anglo-Burmese. Throughout
the discussion of this question, no mention was made of Burmanization and Burmese
public opinion.
On the whole, the Report was Indian-oriented and Indian-concerned from beginning
to end. Further, it did not take note of the historic and economic fact that the Province
of Burma was different and distinct from the other provinces of India. Thus, the recom-

cussed widely in India and Britain; it did not have the same response in Burma. Officially,

Before considering the debate in the Indian Legislative Assembly, it must be noted
that in the early nineteen-twenties, the legislature was a new institution with limited
authority. The members took responsibilities seriously and gave attention to all matters
that came before them Indian railroads -- their service, finance, operation and growth
consumed a great deal of their time and, as might be expected, Burma's railway, quite
naturally was discussed along with those of the other provinces of India. 19
Debate on the question of State managed railways in India began on September 7,
1922. Maulvi Miyan Asjadullah proposed a motion in the Assembly which recommended
to the Governor General in Council that the Indian Railways Act of 1890 should be
amended in order to allow the Government to resume management of the railways in
India. In the brief debate that followed no mention was made of the Acworth Report;
the discussion ended inconclusively with no decision taken because the Government was
not prepared to discuss the question.
Although the Government asked for a two month delay before the question should

IV
On April 1, 1937, Burma was separated from India and became a new political
entity under British rule. One of the conditions of separation was that Burma would
repay India for capital investment and contribute toward the pension funds of retired civil
The total debt was Rs. 584.5 million; of that amount, Rs. 344.5 or 59% resulted
from investment in Burma railways from 1877 to 1937. Despite the many years in which
the railway earned heavy revenues, “little if any of the capital costs had been repaid by
the time of separation."32
The debt remained unpaid until the spring of 1954. On April 8, the Finance
Minister of the now independent Union of Burma announced that his country had reached
a debt settlement with India in which the latter reduced its debt demands, waived unpaid
interest and agreed to accept payment in currency which would be realized from the sale
of Burma's rice to India at fixed prices. The price was set a little below the world price
and Burma had the option of delivering set quantities over a three year span or delivering
the total amount in one year. The agreement was advantageous to both sides; India
needed rice in order to guarantee sufficient food for its population during the period of
the first Five-year plan; Burma, faced with a declining world market, was offered a sub-
stantial order which could be delivered out of the growing surpluses it was accumulating.
In this manner, both sides sought to remove a problem that was born in the colonial past
and which continued to undermine their current good relations.33

The above brief history of the railway in Burma has been offered to demonstrate
how wide and deep the impact of unification was on one aspect of Burmese life. In the
few historical studies of the Burma railway which exist, the subject is always approached
as though all decisions concerning it were made in Burma and in no way show any rela-
tionship to Indian policy. Conversely, all studies of the railway in India take no account
of the unique record and development in the Burma Province. All studies of the Burma
railway omit any explanation of why the railway went into private management in 1896
and why it returned to government operation in 1929. Here then, is a clear example of
an important socio-economic institution which was conceived, developed and governed
during the period of unification by British and Indian policy decisions. Its place in the
growth of modern Burma can only be understood against the background of India-Burma
unification.
If the above is true of the railway, what of the other economic developments in
Burma such as river and road transport, mining, oil drilling and commerce generally.
The example of the railway suggests that the modern histories of Burma and India have
overlooked or ignored many of the ramifications of India-Burma unification which had
significant effect upon the development of Burma. In their interest to trace the rise of
nationalism and self-government, the historians have concentrated on politics and perso-
nalities and not upon the social and economic institutions. While their work is important,
it is not complete. There are many gaps and relatively unexplored areas in Burma's
history. It is time for the historian and social scientist to turn his attention to the
problems of economic and social development in relation to the more dramatic study of
politics and people and possibly begin anew by asking the question, what any influence.
did unification have upon this particular problem or that event.
