It is a matter of common knowledge
that the Malays of Malaya, who originally
came from Minangkabau in Central Sumat-
ra, used to be governed by their own cus-
tomary law known as adat law. This law
is the whole of customs and traditions in
the Malay coummunity which in the course.
of time have obtained the character of laws
and are administered and enforced by the
chiefs or elders. But the Malay adat law is
not customary law pure and simple. It is to
a considerable extent influenced by ancient
Hindu law. And when Islam came to
Malaysia some time in the 13th century
A.D. the Muslim law made great inroads
on Malay customary law to such an extent
that had it not been for the coming of the
British rule, Muslim law would have become
the general law of the land."

II. MARRIAGE
Essentials of a valid marriage
A scholar has warned that generaliza-
tions about Malay marriages are unsafe and
that a satisfactory degree of reliability could
be achieved only if the author concentrates
on a small area. 26 This caveat is equally
applicable to generalizations about Burmese
marriages although the present position of
Burmese law on this question is more settled
and definite than that of the Malayan law.
Under Burmese law, marriage is purely
a civil contract with no religious or sacra-
mental element. To constitute a valid
marriage (i) the man must have attained
puberty; (ii) the woman must have attained
the age of 20 years. If she is under 20 years,
the consent of her parents or guardian must
have been obtained. Such consent would,
however, be unnecessary if the minor girl
had been married before. (iii) The parties
must be mentally competent to contract.
(iv) The relationship between the parties
must be such that their union will not be
against the sentiments of their community.
(v) The parties must give their mutual and
free consent to become husband and wife
in praesenti. (vi) There must finally be
consummation of the marriage. 27
Muslim law, on the other hand, treats
marriage not only as a civil contract but as
a sacrament. 20 According to the Shafii
school, which is the prevalent school

It will thus be seen that there are
some similarities between Malayan Muslim
Law and Burmese Buddhist Law. For
example, both legal systems look upon
marriage as essentially a civil and consen-

religious element in Muslim marriages.
Both consider the boy's attainment of
puberty as conferring upon him the capacity
to marry and both systems do not permit
marriages in futuro if the parties are
minors. There are, however, certain
differences as well. It is proposed to
discuss these differences briefly. The first
point of difference relates to the marriage-
able age of the girl.
