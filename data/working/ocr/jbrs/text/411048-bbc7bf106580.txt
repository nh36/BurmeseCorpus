Introduction
According to Circular No, 199 of United States Geological Survey,
published by F. S. Grimaldi, Irving May and M. H. Fletcher, Nicols
and Slattery discovered in 1926, that a trace of Uranium in fused sodium
fluride caused, an intense yellow-green fluorescence under Ultra-Violet
light. The intensity of the fluorescence is, found to be proportional to
the amount of Uranium present inside the fused flux, and this relation-
ship is the basis for the quantitative determination of Uranium, by a
Fluorimeter.
Fluorescence is defined as the emission of light from a substance
while it is actually being exposed to direct radiation such as Ultra-Violet
liget. This phenomenon is well exhibited by the Fluospar, hence the
name. (Senator The Hon. W. H. Spooner, Australian Atomic Energy
Commission.)
The fluorescence test for Uranium is specific when the exitation is
with long wavelength (3650 A°) Ultra-Violet light. Many elements.
interfere by quenching the Uranium fluorescence. A few micrograms
of Iron, Cobalt, Chromium, Nickel or Manganese are said to reduce
the fluorescence of Uranium by more than ten per cent. On the other
hand the Uranium fluorescence are said to be enhanced by minute
amounts of elements. which by themselves do not fluoresce in fluoride
phosphors.
Quenching elements for 2 grams of flux
Pt., Au., Pb., Cr., Mn., Co., Ni., Lanthanum, Titanium, Cerium,
Praseodymium., Neodymium., Mg., Sn., Fe., Thorium.
Niobium fluoresces only with short wave excitation (2536 A°).

Mercury has been reported as a strong quencher of Uranium
fluorescence, but it is volatilised in the preparation of the melt.
Two techniques are used to reduce or to eliminate interference due
to quenching.
(i) “Dilution" method or ' direct method' involve no
chemical separations, Price, in 1945, found that it
was possible to reduce quenching to a negligible factor
by using sufficiently small sample for analysis. The
degree of quenching is said to depend only on the
concentration of the quenching of the flux, not on the
ratio of Uranium to quenching element.
(ii) "Separation" method involves separation of Uranium
from quenching elements before the fluoride phosphors
is made. The separation is accomplished by extraction
of Uranyl Nitrate by Organic Solvents, (Ether and
Ethyl Acetate.)
Containers
Platinum or Gold dishes can be used as containers for melting
fluoride phosphors.
As platinum dishes were not readily available, we used a gold dish
of Grimaldi's design, as used by the United States Geological Survey.
(Fig. 1.)
The design was improved by Dr. Freddy Ba Hli, by narrowing the
bottom from 35 mm. to 34 mm. in order to release the melt easily,
within three minutes, after solidification and cooling at room
temperature.
This special gold dish is a circular gold plate of 0.2 mm. thickness
and of 46 mm. diameter, hollowed to a depth of 10 mm. leaving a rim
4 mm. width. The diameter of the top of the hollow is 38 mm. The
diameter of the bottom is 27 mm. on the curved surface of 10 cm.
radius. The hollow has a slant depth with a rounded curve of radius
3.5 mm. These specifications have to be adhered to, in order to produce
phosphors, which can fit the pit of phosphor-slide in the fluorimeter,
retaining the form, 34 mm. diameter, and 1.5 mm. thickness at the centre.
The gold dish is cleaned, by boiling in (1 to 1) HCl., before and
after the use, and reshaped in the original dies.

Reagents and Apparatus
Mixed Carbonates Fluoride Flux
Sodium Fluoride is used for the preparation of the Uranium
Phosphors, although its melting point is high, with a tendency to stick
to the gold or platinum dish. The salt can attack the gold dish at
elevated temperature, resulting in some quenching of the Uranium
Fluorescence by the dissolved metal.
To minimize solution, metal from the dish, and to obtain phosphors,
which readily part from the dish, a mixed flux of sodium carbonate,
potassium and sodium fluoride is used. This melts around 600° C.
To minimize the quenching effect of impurities in the flux, sodium
fluoride, purified by repeated re-crystalization, or reagents of analar
quality are used.
We mixed 9 parts by weight of NaF, 45.5 parts by weight of Na 2
CO3 and 45-5 parts by weight of K₂ CO3, (in the absence of Na₂ CO 3.
72 parts by weight of NaHCO 3 is substituted.) The mixture is fused
by melting in platinum basin and after cooling the melt for 3 minutes,
the shrunken crust of flux is broken up and transferred into a clean
porcelain motar and pestle, and it is crushed to a fine mesh and stored
in an air tight bottle. This 'fused flux' melts at 605° C. and does not
stick to the gold dish and it is found to be satisfactory for dissolving
uranium residue completely from the platinum or gold dish, for
measurement of fluorescence under ultra-violet light.
Fluorimeters
"Reflection" or "Transmission" are types of flourimeters.
The main parts of a fluorimeter are:-
1. An ultra-violet light source.
2. Primary and secondary filters.
3. A phototube together with an electrical system for measur-
ing its response.
Ultra-violet light source
As the source of excitation, long wavelength Ultra-violet light
(3650 Aº) H 100-E4 high pressure mercury vapour lamps, are used.
(2536 A°) short wave radiation is dangerous to the eyes.

The primary filter absorbs visible light from the light source with-
out seriously reducing the intensity of the ultra-violet light. Corning
filters, No. 5960, 5874, 5 mm. thickness are satisfactory.
The secondary filters allow the visible fluorescent (550 milli-micron
band of Uranium fluorescence) light to pass through but absorbs ultra-
violet light. Glass filters, corning No. 3484 or No. 3486 are used.
Detection of Fluorescence and Measurement of
Photo Electric Current
Fluorescence is measured by photo-multiplier tubes directly with a
micro-ammeter, using Model 514 M electronic photometer.
Procedure
Preparation of melts
In order to produce proper fluorescent melts, we observe the
following routine :—
(i) We weigh almost exactly two grams of the flux in a
short time and transfer all into the gold dish, contain-
ing Uranium residue.
(ii) The flux is melted in the gold dish in a small Meker
burner flame, keeping the temperature near the
melting point of the flux. The art is to keep the melt
just where re-solidification and re-melting is taking
place. We rock and swirl the melting flux for two
minutes, by putting the dish in and out of the
flame, alternately, causing the flux to alternately
melt and begin to crystalize. At the end of two
minutes of heating, we keep the melt in the hottest
part of the flame for two seconds and then remove
from the flame, keeping the melt still in horizontal
position until the centre of the solidified flux turns
white.
(iii) The phosphor is allowed to cool for three minutes
in the gold dish, from where it slips off by mere
inversion of the dish. It is immediately stored in
dessicator.
(iv) The fluorimeter is set to read a certain value for a
standard disc of known fluorescence of the unknown
phos-phors then measured. We remove the phosphor

Summary
We are perfecting our practical techniques in, two simple fluori-
metric methods, viz.
(i) "Dilution" method and
(ii) "Separation " method, in order to support the geologi-
cal mapping of Uranium bearing regions in Burma.
To supplement and confirm our determinations by
fluorimeter, we are devoloping volumetric methods of
assay of uranium, using the special apparatus, "Jones
Reductor" for referee assay.
