When a porous clay pot is filled with water and kept in a beaker
containing dry soil, the water oozes out of the pores and wets the soil
in contact with the pot. The water is held in the form of films around
the soil particles and the layer of soil touching the pot becomes almost
saturated with water. The water films spread on to the neighbouring
dry soil particles and gradually the wetness spreads from particle to
particle. The flow of water through the soil could be compared to the
flow of heat along a metallic bar which is heated at one end, or to the
flow of electricity along a wire. The difference in attraction for water
between two portions of the soil which are not equally moist was called
by Buckingham as the capillary tension or potential in 1907 and he
said that this capillary potential was similar to the Electro Motive Force
between two points on a wire carrying electricity. In a soil which is
saturated with water the films round the particles are less convex than
the films round the particles which are not saturated, and because the
films are in contact there is a tendency for the water to flow from a
region of greater saturation to a less saturated region, or in other words.
the films of greater curvature exert an attraction towards the films of
less curvature. This force of attraction is considerably great when the
films are thin. When a dry piece of blotting paper is soaked in ink it
sucks the ink very rapidly at first, but the same blotter when moist will
not suck ink with the same avidity as when it was dry. In the same
manner the water is held by a suturated soil with weak molecular forces
and so could be easily removed but a drier soil holds on to its surface
thinner films of water with greater force, and so considerably larger
forces are needed to remove the water from a dry soil when compared
to soils which are more moist. Consequently, a dry soil has a greater
attraction for water than a wet one. The capillary potential is greater
in a dry soil than in a more moist soil.

or tension and the apparatus is called a tensiometer. It consists of: a
porous clay pot 2-3 inches long and 1 inch in diameter to which is

the glass tube which is also full of water is made to dip into a beaker
containing mercury. The outside of the clay pot is wiped dry, and it is
kept in a 600 cc beaker which contains at the bottom an inch of soil
whose capillary potential is to be measured. More of the soil is added
all round the clay pot, and the soil is packed as firmly as possible. The
clay pot and the rubber stopper should be completely covered with the
soil, and the mouth of the beaker is covered with oil paper to minimise
the evaporation of water from the surface of the soil. The glass tube
is clamped vertically to a stand. The packing of the soil round the
clay pot is done as quickly as possible, and the time noted. As the water
oozes out of the clay pot, mercury rises in the glass tube, and the rise of
mercury is recorded at 15 minute intervals in the beginning, and after-
wards at 30 minute intervals, when the speed of rise of mercury has
become greatly diminished.
A dry soil absorbs water very rapidly, and the mercury rises very
fast in the manometer. As the soil becomes more and more moist the
capillary potential falls, and the rise of mercury becomes proportionaely
slower. When the suction preasure of water approaches one atmos-
phere, air enters the clay pot and there is no more rise of mercury; on
the other hand the pressure begins to fall. The maximum tension
exerted by a soil does not exceed one atmosphere, when measurements.
are made with this simple tensiometer.
Mandalay soil which is a stiff, calcareous clay was used to study
the phenomenon of capillary tension, and the data obtained have been
plotted in the graph. The experiment was conducted with soil samples
containing different amounts of initial moisture, and the capillary
tension in millimetres of mercury at different time intervals was
recorded.
A family of curves was obtained which shows clearly the relation-
ship between capillary tension and the initial moisture in the soil. For
the oven dry soil free from moisture the maximum of one atmosphere
tension was obtained in hours. As the soil contained more and
more moisture the capillary potential was progressively smaller and the
time taken to reach the maximum was considerably increased.

and thoroughly mixed by stirring and left overnight in a closed vessel
to become uniform in moisture content. This could be done successfully
until a moisture content of 25 per cent was reached; beyond that the
soil sample became too sticky and full of lumps that it was not possible
to prepare samples with uniform moisture content. A moisture deter-

after the experiment to find out the increase in moisture.
When the amount of initial moisture was more than 10 per cent
the maximum tension of one atmosphere was reached only after an
interval of one week, and when the amount of initial moisture was
more than 20 per cent the attraction of soil for water was so small that
the rise of mercury in the tube was less than the fall of mercury due
to the cubical expansion of water in the clay pot, as the temperature of
the laboratory was increasing during the daytime.
In this experiment there are a number of manipulation difficulties
and hence various sources of error are likely to come in: (1) the prepara-
tion of soils with varying moisture contents is not easy; (2) Hysteresis
effects and imperfections in the samples produce a lot of variations in
the results; (3) The packing of the soil round the clay pot could not
be done as one wishes it to be done, and duplicate experiments may not
give the same results on this account; (4) There might be some
evaporation from the surface which also would introduce an error in
the rise of mercury, and the expansion of water in the clay pot may be
quite appreciable to counteract the entry of water into the porous pot
especially as the day becomes warmer and warmer. In spite of these
defects valuable information has been obtained.
Summary and Conclussion
Dry soil has a greater attraction for water and exerts a capillary
potential on soil particles which are more moist. The capillary potential
becomes rapidly diminished as the soil becomes more and more moist
and is very slow for clay soils containing over 20 per cent of water.
The capillary potential measured with the simple type of tensiometer
does not exceed 1 atmosphere. When the force exerted by the soil
exceeds 1 atmosphere, air enters the porous pot from outside and the
pressure falls. The results of the experiment obtained by this simple.
apparatus should be viewed more from a qualitative than a quantitative
stand point.
