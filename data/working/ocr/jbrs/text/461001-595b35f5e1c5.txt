1. Child's Horoscope
Some of our emotional impressions tend to cluster together and form a unit mass.
The result is a durable association of ideas which has a dominating influence on us con-
sciously or unconsciously. This group of over-riding mental-factors is a complex. Its
nature is beyond the grasp of our intelligence. The search for the roots from which the
complex has sprung into existence is however rewarding.
To discover the roots of the Burmese complex we must needs penetrate behind the
social events and institutions which form the facade of Burmese society. These events and
institutions are familiar ground to most Burmans. But owing to the blurring effect of use
and wont, their edge has been taken off and their significance has been all but lost to us.
To retrieve the loss we must start from earliest infancy.
Soon after a child's birth Burmese custom requires an astrologer to be called in to
cast the child's horoscope (Jātā ɔɔ). The horoscope contains the name given to the
child by the astrologer, the date and the hour of birth, and the state of the sky at birth.
It is in fact the map of the sky which is supposed to indicate the future events of the
child's life.
Burmese astrologers observe in the heavens eight and not seven planets. They are
Sun, Moon, Mars, Mercury, Jupiter, Venus, Saturn and Rāhu. The first seven are known
to western astronomy, but the eighth (Rāhu) is peculiar to Burmese astronomy. It is
described as a dark opaque planet. All eight planets are the abodes of nats (spirits), and
they preside over the days of the week after which they are named. But a week has only
seven days. Wednesday is therefore divided between Mercury and Rāhu. Mercury presides
from sunrise to mid-day and Rāhu from mid-day till night time on Wednesday.
The planet that presided on the day of a person's birth is believed to have a shap-
ing influence on his life for good or ill. It also determines his character, and it bears the
same name as the day of his birth. His name is chosen to indicate the day of the week on
which he was born as well as the planet presiding at his birth. For this purpose the
Burmese alphabets which are 33 in number are divided into eight groups. To each day of
the week is assigned one group except Wednesday and Saturday. One group has to be

2. Naming the Child.
About a fortnight after the birth of the child, an auspicious day is chosen for the
naming ceremony. On the appointed day the child's head is washed with boiled soapnut
water (Kinmundat mεy:) if it has not been done earlier, and the child's horoscope
name is publicly announced. This naming ceremony of the child is the first important
public event in his life.

The belief that the planets can change the course of human affairs is as old as man.
Primitive people must have observed some interrelation between the happenings in the
heaven and on earth. When the path of the sun rose higher day by day, vegetation was
seen to flourish and grow and the crops to ripen. And when it declined back towards the
horizon vegetation began to wilt away. It was also noticed that when the moon was at
the full, fits of mental disorder came upon half-witted men. The conception that the
movements of the heavenly bodies help to weave the web of human affairs thus took root
and grew.
It is so deep-rooted that the advance of civilization has not succeeded in
eradicating it. We are reliably informed that no less than one hundred leading Hollywood
stars constantly consult astrologers at fees ranging from three to ten dollars a telephone
call or a visit to know what to eat, what to wear, where to live and when to make love.
One of them once asked an astrologer on the telephone: 'I am a Sagattarius and I have
had a marriage proposal from a Taurus. Do you think we will get along?” Another star
asked: 'My husband and I would like to have a baby. When do you think the stars will
be exactly right? Thus astrologers are handsomely paid for interpreting the stars to the
stars. 2
It may be mentioned that there is no surname among Burmans. Even the wife
retains her own maidem name. This may appear strange. It is however as old as our
first parents. Adam's sons are Cain and Abel and his wife is Eve. The father's name is
not tacked on to their names.
In addressing a younger man the prefix Maung (c) which means 'younger
brother' is used. Familiarly Po (ɔ):) may take the place of Maung (ɔε). If he is of the
age of one's elder brother Ko (♡) which means ‘elder brother' is substituted for Maung
(c). For an older man U (§:) which means uncle, is employed. One who feels himself
superior to the person addressed uses the prefix Nga (c). For a girl or a woman the
prefix is Ma () or Mè (s) or shin (9ε). It becomes Mi (8) when speaking to an inferior.
It may at times be used as a term of endearment. But this is rare. The prefix Daw (cs)
is used in addressing an elderly woman. But U (:) and Daw (cs) are also prefixes
signifying a higher status.
To a Burman some days are lucky, while others are unlucky. Among lucky days
also some occupy a premier position (qipе). For any undertaking a lucky day has to
be chosen. Even in a simple matter such as dressing the hair, one's birthday and Monday
should be avoided as inauspicious days.

3. Early Home Influence
The mother is the pivot of the Burmese home. She keeps house, and she feeds,
clothes and washes her baby. She herself puts him to bed. Baby-sitters are unknown in
Burma. Older children and relatives help the baby.
A child is breast-fed by his mother till he is about a year old. Even after he is
weaned, he is in close and constant contact with his mother. He hangs about her, and
she often takes occasion to snatch a few moments to fondle him in the midst of her
crowded life at home: This tie of filial affection becomes stronger as the child grows.
The child is taught to give what he can as he becomes older. Obedience to parents
is the accepted rule. Spare the rod and spoil the child'. (en mosum) is the
traditional guiding principle of parents. In urban areas, however, the rigour of this rule.
has been relaxed with the introduction of modern educational methods. An American
Visiting Lady Professor who taught in the Faculty of Education of Rangoon University
has shrewdly observed: 'The atmosphere a Burmese baby grows up in is one in which
there is little punishment or effort to dominate. There is much affection, indulgence,
attraction and patience. And there is little inhibition or restraint.”3
The home with its varying needs and wants, its recurring stresses and strains is in a
sense the microcosm of Burmese life, There the mother fulfills her proper function. She
guides, teaches, trains, helps, encourages, comforts and nourishes the young. The child is
thus helped in his growth and development towards self-sufficiency. This earliest training
at the hands of the mother stands him in good stead throughout life.
As soon as a child learns how to speak fluently he is taught his daily prayers which
stress the reverence due to the Buddha, his Law, and his Order as the three sacred gems.
Parents and teachers are placed on the same level with the three sacred gems as those to
whom a boundless debt of thanks is due. The child is also told to cling to the five
precepts. They are these. Thou shalt not kill. Thou shalt not steal. Thou shalt not
commit adultery. Thou shalt not speak falsehood. Thou shalt not drink introxicating
liquor. These precepts though given a religious colour are in truth and in fact the
foundation of the good life. This early indoctrination moulds the Burman's life.
4. Going to School
In villages, when a child is about 4 or 5 years of age he goes to a primary lay
school where he receives a rudimentary education. At about six, a boy generally goes to
the monastic school of his village. There he is taught not only the three R's, but right
values viewed from the Buddhist angle. The moral training he receives first at home and
emphasised in school enables him to tell good from evil, truth from falsehood, right from
wrong, the beautiful from the ugly. Up to this point the sole purpose of instruction is
how to lead the good life and not how to earn one's living.

5. The Monastery
Literacy is almost universal in Burma thanks to her monastic schools. Every
village has a monastery which is the fount and focus of village life. In a town or city
there are more monasteries than one. Owing to the complexity of life and the diversity of
races their influence is neither so profound nor so pervasive as that of the village
monasteries.
In every monastery there is a presiding monk (Sayadaw) who has under him other
monks (Upazins) and novices (Koyins). Sometimes there is a cluster of monasteries
(Kyaungdaik) within the same precints (parawun). Over it is the Abbot (Gaing-ok or
Sayadawgyi).
Every monastery has a small piece of consecrated ground (Thein) where monks
observe the sabbath (Uposatha), congregate for confession (30000564) and initiate
candidates into monkhood (ပဉ္စင်းတက်).
It must however be remembered that within the monastery everybody is equal.
The community is self-governing. The levelling democratic principle prevails. Seniority
is reckoned by the number of years from ordination (Was). 'There was nothing in the
way of differences of rank in the circles of brethren, but the natural privileges and claims
to respect, which belong to greater seniority i.e. to the greater length of spiritual standing,
which was reckoned from the date of ordination'. 4 The entire monastery precincts is
held sacred, and no layman may enter it with shod feet.
A member of the Buddhist Order is bound by disciplinary rules. In joining the
Order he renounces the world to lead the higher life that leads to Nibbāna. He has left
home for the life of homelessness. All earthly ties of relationship and rights to property
are completely severed. He becomes a member of the community of mendicants
(Bhikkhusanga) and leads a chaste life. Though he takes no vow of poverty at his ordina-
tion, he can call his own only the four necessaries of life (ogo::ul:) to wit, raiment,
food, lodging and medicine.
Strictly speaking, anything that does not come within the circle of these immediate
necessities of life cannot form part of a monk's property. 'No express vow imposes on
him the duty of poverty, both the marriage tie and the rights of property of him who
renounces the world, are regarded as ipso facto cancelled by the going forth from home
into homelessness.'5

It is pertinent to remark that there has been a lapse from primitive austerity in this

Poggalika. The former belongs to the entire Order and the latter to the individual monk.
This is a concession to human frailty. Yet Buddhists generally look askance at the
acquisition of worldly possession by a monk as a negation of his avowed flight into

In addition to the five precepts which every layman is taught to observe from
childhood, a monk has to keep five other precepts. They are: Thou shalt not eat food at
unseasonable times i.e. after mid-day. Thou shalt not sing, dance or play any musical
instrument. Thou shalt not use any garland, perfume and unguents. Thou shalt not sit,
stand or sleep on any elevated place. Thou shalt not receive any silver or gold.
As a mark of humility and separation from the world a monk is clad in a yellow
robe consisting of three pieces called dugok, kowut and thinbaing. A dugok is rectangular
in shape and is thrown over the left shoulder with the ends hanging loosely before and
behind the body. A thinbaing is a loin-cloth that reaches the ankles, and a kowut is a
square-shaped piece that covers the chest and shoulders and reaches the knees.

example of a life of poverty, chastity and self-negation imparts added strength to the early
moral training he received at home. Indeed, the early impregnation of the religious spirit
together with the influence of the parents in childhood and the precept reinforced by the
example of the monk-teacher are the dynamic forces that have given the Burmese their
national character.
6. Novitiation (Shinbyu)
When a child becomes a teen-ager he reaches a somewhat different level of
experience. The hey-day in the blood begins to be turbulent, and it does not wait upon
the judgment. To set off this disturbing upsurge, Burmese parents initiate a boy before he
is in his teens into the penumbra of the Buddhist religious Order by a novitiation ceremony
(Shinbyu). After the ceremony, the boy is admitted to the monastery as a novice or
neophyte (samaṇera). He does not become a monk. He remains at the periphery of the
fraternity.
Before the novitiation ceremony the novice-to-be has first of all to learn by rote the
duties incumbent on him in his new life. His horoscope is studied by an astrologer who
choses an auspicious date generally before the beginning of Buddhist Lent (July.)
Then follows a colourful farewell celebration which stands in contrast to the
austere life the novice-to-be is about to lead within the monastery walls. On the appointed
day he appears in his best dress bedecked with jewellery. He goes round the village or
town on a gaily caparisoned pony or a decorated motor-car shaded by gold coloured

7. Ear-boring
What the novitiation ceremony is to a Burmese boy, the ear-boring ceremony
(c) is to a Burmese girl. Both are somewhat intoxicating experiences to
young minds that get the first taste of public attention. But there is a sharp shade of
difference in significance. One stresses the development of a new self-awareness and the
other the assumption of a new role.
Before a girl reaches her teens her ear-boring ceremony takes place on the auspicious
day and hour chosen by the astrologer. It is a festive occasion to which friends and rela-
tives are invited. The astrologer and the ear-borer are present. When the former indicates
the right moment, the latter passes a golden or silver needle through each lobe of the ear
and bends it to form a loop. The wound is allowed to heal and the hole is gradually
enlarged to receive the ear-ring.
The boring of a girl's ears has not the significance the bringing out of a girl as
debutante has in Western society. But it does mean that she has to give up her childish

of learning but on the inculcation of what gives value to human life in a somewhat Spartan
milieu. Burma is therefore happily free from the teenage problem which the Western
nations find impossible of solution. They say in utter despair that ‘the bringing up of

When a Burman reaches the age of 20 he can with the approval of his parents be
admitted to the Order by an ordination ceremony. The doors of the Order are open to
all men without distinction of caste or rank thus emphasizing the potential equality of all
But murderers and robbers, as well as persons afflicted with leprosy, goitre, leuco-
derma, consumption and epilepsy are excluded from the Order. Neither can persons in
the royal service especially soldiers, be admitted to the Order as the right of the Sovereign
who is the head of the Army would be interfered with. Nor can slaves and debtors gain
admission as it would involve an infringement of the right of creditors and owners.'13
The ordination ceremony is quite impressive. 'In a chapter of at least 10 monks
under certain circumstances only 5..... the candidate (for ordination) after having provided
himself with the requisite alms-bowl and robes, and chosen his spiritual leader or tutor,
Upaj-jhāya (Upyitzè), is examined by the president of the chapter concerning his eventual
disabilities, his name and the name of his spiritual tutor. These things being ascertained,
the candidate requests to be ordained, whereupon the president formally proposes to the
chapter to assent to this request, twice repeated. If the assembly by their silence give the
consent, the president exhorts the newly-ordained brother to stick to the four necessaries,
and to avoid during his whole life the four capital sins. He thus becomes a member of the
Order and is bound by the rules of the Order.'14
10. Gains and Losses
The spread of Western education in Burma has not been an unmixed good. There
are obvious gains. Knowledge has increased considerably. Skills unknown before have
been imparted and the mental horizon has been vastly widened. But there are serious
disadvantages. The swelling strength of the economic motive in education dulls our sense
of right values. What is worse, the educated class develops a superiority complex which
clashes with the egalitarian principles of his religion learnt in early years at the village lay
and monastic schools.

The Buddhist believes that the disparity of circumstances and attainments in life is
referrible to the deeds done in the past existence. What was sown in the previous life is
reaped in the here and the now. Deeds good or evil have inescapable consequences. This is
the law of Karma which is the Sanskrit for the Pali Kamma. Though a man cannot avoid
the inexorable effects of his past deeds he can by his inherent god-like powers shape his
future destiny.
A Burman's unswerving aim is therefore the accumulation of merit that will lift him
to a higher plane in the life which is to come. He does not merely amass a fortune. The
wealth that he acquires is made to live in deeds of gold. He spends lavishly in building
pagodas, monasteries, zayats (rest-houses) and in inviting all and sundry to feasts of good
thing (ahlus).
The transvaluation of human values owing to the impact of Western learning is a
matter for deep regret. It assumes the most menacing form in the apotheosis of science.
The march of science must be matched by a comparable increase in humanizing
knowledge. Else, science will depersonalize man and increase material goods instead of
what constitutes the good. Without moral values, science will end in manufacturing lethal
weapons and building death-factories.
Science is now passing through an ambivalent crisis. She has jettisoned traditional
religion and dogma in her sabbathless pursuit of the ordered reality underlying the
universe. To her dismay she finds herself in the twilight realm of doubt and uncertainty.
She discovers that the structure of nature is discordant with both our senses and our
imagination. Nature as we see it does not represent things as they are; more, it is incompre-
hensible to our minds. For example, space is at once finite and boundless. Again light is
treated as consisting of waves for certain purposes and of particles for other purposes.
Even our sense of stability is awry. Things that appear motionless are very much alive
with electrons that circle their nuclei at the dizzying rate of billions of times in a second.
Science is confronted with a yet greater dilemma. Her discoveries have added
immensely to our material comforts and raised our standard of life. But she equates
human value with utilitarian economic value and drives men into ceaseless activity in
wresting the maximum use or profit from the available world. Men have however come
to realize that they are engaged in a Sisyphean task which yields no satisfaction or

It needs therefore to be stated with agonised emphasis that science must be
balanced by the arts which nourish the roots of our complex, to wit, ardent religious faith,
filial piety and reverence for exemplary teachers. Loss of these roots means loss of
