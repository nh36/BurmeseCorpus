Foreword
It is obvious that the author of this paper has made a serious study of the
ancient epigraphs of Burma comprising lithic inscriptions, terra-cotta records as well
as mural writings in ink. No one without adequate knowledge of such languages as
Sanskrit, Pali, Mon and Burmese could have produced such a scholarly paper like
this. This learned writer has dealt with some aspects of oriental linguistics, with
special reference to the Burmese language, pertaining to the early era, medieval period
and modern time.
The introduction of this paper is a very clear perspective of the historical
background of the Burmese race and language and its cognates.
Having pointed concrete cases of inconsistency in Old Burmese orthography
relevantly and in a representative manner, the author has illustrated the rule of vowel
changes such as:- o><u; 0> subscript w; iw > ui or uih; à > a and e>
a. It is also pointed out the fact that a voiced consonant in Old Burmese had and
can become unvoiced in modern mother tongue of the Burmans. He then elucidated
the employment of the double dotted tonal token known in Pali as Visajjani and
in Sanskrit as Visarga, citing relevantly important authentic documents in the form
of lithic inscriptions and mural writings in ink, especially on the walls of Lokahteikpan
temple of Pagan which very few people know about.
After explaining tonal system, this learned scholar supplied a comparative
chart of common glosses of Old Mon and Old Burmese as well as a comparison of
Old and Modern Burmese with their respective phonetic pronunciation. A scientifically
inguistic study of the syntax of Old Burmese citing the extant examples from Maha
Janaka Jātaka written on the walls of Lokahteikpan temple along with extracts from
Mahā Nārada Jataka are also discussed.
Finally, examples of some loan words among many that have been collected
by him and his staff, as have been ascertained and shown along with their respective
origin, namely, Sanskrit, Pali and Mon are well displayed. A comprehensive, if
not a completely complete, philology of the Burmese language, if not an all-embracing
oriental linguistics is yet to be written. As Dr.J.A. Matisoff, Professor of Linguistics
of California University remarks "Nai Pan Hla's paper looks extremely interesting.
Dr. A. B. Griswold thinks very highly of him..." and I, as one of the linguists, regard
this paper though rather short, a really beautiful beginning beneficial character in
tackling with the problem of the philology of the Old Burmese language leave alone that
of orientalia.
Rangoon,
October 16, 1975.
U Lu Pe Win*

Preface
In 1972, I was requested by the University of Hawaii to present a paper to
the First International Conference on Austroasiatic Linguistics held in 1973. I wrote
for them a paper on " Comparative Study of Old & Modern Mon". As I was unable
to attend the Seminar it was circulated to all the international participants.
It was
also published in the following year by the Kagoshima University, Japan, in October
issue of their Bulletin. The University of California, Berkeley also called for papers
from me every year. Recently, the National University of Australia sent a request to
me to contribute in their research Journal too. But I have not been able to comply
with their request, owing to the pressure of departmental duty.
This short paper is intended to be presented to the Eighth International Con-
ference on Sino-Tibetan Linguistics at the University of California, Berkeley. It has
been approved and accepted by the Board of the Linguistics Committee to be
read on 25-10-75. However, since I am unable to attend the Seminar to read
the paper myself there, I have to decide the publishing of it in our JBRS for the
benefit of those who are interested both at home and abroad. This short paper is
just a beginning of my study in Burmese epigraphy and phonology.
To make this paper a success, my grateful thanks are due to Sayagyi U Lu Pe Win
& Dr. Htin Aung for their encouragement; U Myint Kyi, Head of Burmese Dept. and

dalay University and Daw Than Nu, Anthropology Dept. of Rangoon University for

Introduction
Racially all the indigenous peoples of Burma belong to Mongoloid (yellow
coloured people) but linguistically they are divided into three main groups: the
Tibeto-Burman, the Mon-Khmer (Austroasiatic) and the Thai-Chinese. Geographically,
the Tibeto-Burman family extends a long series of languages and dialects spoken from
Tibet in the north to Burma in the south and from Baltistan² (Kashmir) in the west
to Yunnan in the east. In Burma, it comprises Burmese, Arakanese, Intha, Taungyo,
Tavoyan, Merguese, Chin, Kachin, Karen, Kaya (Karenni), Kadu, Danu, Naga and
various other hill tribes. In fact, the extinct Pyu language which had flourished in
the 7th-8th century A.D. at Old Prome (Srīkṣetra) belongs to Tibeto-Burman group as
revealed by Dr. C.O. Blagden.* Historically, the Pyu were the earliest comers into
Burma among Tibeto-Burman stock. They might have reached central Burma in the
first century A.D. I consider that the original homeland of the Tibeto-Burman family
would probably be in the north-west of China. According to the Chinese contem-
porary accounts, the Pyu Kingdom was sacked by Nanchao of Yunnan in 835 A.D.
after which the Burmese founded their Kingdom at Pagan. Of course, a number of
Pyu people migrated north due to the attack of Nanchao and they merged with
Burmese at Pagan. The name Pagan is said to have derived from Руūgāma (Pyu
village). Later on, the Burmese of Pagan Dynasty came into contact with the Mons.
a different language belonging to Mon-Khmer family. The Mons of Pagan Dynasty.
had already attained to a considerable high stage of civilization whose. Kingdoms
were located in lower Burma and lower Siam known as Suvaṇṇabhumi and Dvaravatt
respectively. Dvaravati had flourished in Old Siam from 6th to 11th centuries A.D.
before the arrival of the Thais.

The Ruins of Pagan
The ruins of Pagan, today, with not less than five thousand greater and smaller
monuments covering a wide area of over ten square miles, whose survivors are still
profoundly preserved and which from time to time became awe and inspiration of
every generation, is one of the most talked about tourist centres in Southeast Asia
and perhaps it is one of the wonders of the world. Unfortunately, Pagan was very
badly shaken by a strong earthquake on the 8th of July 1975.4 It was the worst-hit
and damages were the worse that had ever been occurred to Pagan during the long
history of nine hundred years.. I imagine that it was worse than the destructions
made by the Turks in the last quarter of 13th century A.D. Though most of the temples
and stupas of Pagan developed cracks and their finials or pinnacles toppled or tilted
due to the quake, they would be skillfully restored to their original shape and design
in due course. Consequently, Pagan can still be remained for every visitor as the
wonder that was old Pagan, even after the historic earthquake.
History & Epigraphy
Later Burmese kingdoms were known as Sagaing, Pinya, Myinsaing, Ava, Tongoo
and Mandalay. The Burmese had three successive wars with the British in 1824,
1852 and 18855 which led to the British annexation of Arakan and Tenasserim, the
delta region of Pegu Division and finally of the rest of Burma proper respectively.
Burma was occupied by the Japanese during World War II from 1942 to 1945 before
she regained her Independence from the British on the 4th of January 1948. Burmese
is the chief and official language of Burma since then.
Burmese language is rich in literature. It has a great number of epigraphs on
stone or clay; on the walls of the inner temples of Pagan below the mural paintings;
on palmleaves and folded-papers (parabaik) and in printed books.
The epigraphical records of over a thousand stone inscriptions, dedicatory in
nature, dignified in style, with many allusions to incidents from Buddhist scriptures
and Jataka tales, began from the early 12th century A.D. Burmese literature on
palm-leaf and folded-paper manuscripts of an imaginative style in verse and prose
came into being in the 15th century untill printing became prevalent in the 19th
century.

Vowels and Consonants
The Burmese alphabet, borrowed from Mon,' has evidently derived from the
Pallava script of South India. Significantly, the Burmese, through Mon, adopted their
vowels and consonants from Sanskrit as they have 12 vowels and 35 consonants in
the olden days. But they later discarded two consonants as they have no use. These
two consonants are $/s and $/sh². Of course the third s which is in use nowadays
is pronounced th which is very different from Indian or Mon sound.. Thus the
Burmese pronounce sila (virtue) as thila Milá / and siri (glory, splendour) as thīri
eiri and so on.

The Earliest Burmese Records
During the time of King Kyansittha, the written language was found mainly
in Mon and perhaps Mon was his official language. After his death his disinherited
son, Prince Rajakumar started writing Burmese for the first time though he did not
drop Mon, Pyu and Pali languages which he engraved together on each face of a
quadrilingual inscription stone. All the four languages are found to be of the same matter.
The extinct Pyu and Old Mon languages appeared on this stone were at first unknown
to the scholars of Burma until Dr. C. O. Blagden of the School of Oriental and African
Studies in the London University found the key to tackle them and edited them in
1909. This quadriligual inscription has been known as Myazedi inscription because
it was unearthed near a modern pagoda of this name. Within half a century onwards
from the first writing in Burmese began there are only a few dated Burmese inscriptions
that have been discovered at Pagan. It is very curious enough to see a small temple
at Pagan, called Lokahteikpan which contains both Mon and Burmese ink-inscriptions
on its inner walls below the mural paintings depicting life story of Lord Buddha and
Jātaka tales (Buddha's previous birth stories).
All these Burmese records are found to be in the beginning stage. The
spellings are very inconsistent which are haphazard and diverse. It is known as
Transitional period. The transit from writing Mon to begin writing Burmese as begin-
ners. It is also called pre-Standard Old Burmese or Burmese sub-period. Burmese
orthography became standardised only in the reign of King Nataungmya (Jeyyasinkha)
1211-1230 A.D. when Burmese became the main language of Pagan Dynasty.

The use of Visarga (:) two dots'
In Old Burmese, the use of Visarga (:) two dots, transcribed (h) is almost not
found in stone inscriptions. In Old Mon it indicates the explosive heavy tone as in
Modern Burmese. Strangely enough, this usage is found in Old Burmese written at
Lokahteikpan temple whereas Old Mon glosses are written side by side. I wonder if

Evolution of some final Consonants
As in Old Mon, Old Burmese had finals a ; w ; h and r which are
no more in use in modern literature. In olden days, final a was denoted
for the short tone either written next to or below the final consonant or vowel.
It is represented now by a dot ( . ) as in ne, / né / ; to, / to^/ ; pui, / pơ" /
; mo, / mɔ/ ; etc. which is known as amyit. In transcription of Old Burmese the
final a written on the line next to consonant or vowel is transcribed as apostrophe
and for the one written below as a comma under the vowel pointing out a tonal
sign. Final w is used only when praying or wishing; i.e. ce sow / se ŏ / %; it has
long been symbolized as a dash to the right of the vowel o and transcribed long ō.
It has evolved thus- (သောင် > သော် > ဘော်). The Mon has gone too far thus
( mod > em > am > -kow > kau) - meaning flower.
ကော်
Final h has integrated or merged with Visarga (:) two dots as both produce
the same explosive or heavy sound. Medial final r of Old Burmese is not in use
Old Burmese had it mostly in loan words from Sanskrit as in nerpān; purhā;
tirya etc.
now.
One more to note is that though final y is still in use, it has a substitute
sign of a dash to the left to indicate heavy tone. Both Old Mon and Old Burmese
have no such symbol. Both the two languages have invented such modifications
side by side. This symbol of a dash to the left derived from ey thus-
( ငယ် > ပြ > > '). Curiously enough the Burmese consonant jh has
interchanged to cy but the Mon still maintans jh. It evolved thus- (c) > al > > oj).
This jh to cy theory has pointed out to me by Sayagyi U Tha Myat, author of
Burmese Alphabets, since over 20 years. I discovered this evolution practically in an
old palm-leaf manuscript only five years ago though others may not accept it as yet.
