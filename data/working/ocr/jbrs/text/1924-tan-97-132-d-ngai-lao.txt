All that Ssu ma Ch'ien and the Chien han shu know definitely
about Li-chien, is that the Parthian king, in 140-86 B.C., sent to the court of
China jugglers, male or female, of Li-chien. The jugglers, male and female, of
Alexandria were famous; and this exportation, with that of female dancers and
musicians, is attested by way of the Indian Ocean both in the Periplus of the
Erythran Sea and in a passage of the Hou han shu relating to the beginning
of the 2nd century A. D. The Chinese, who appear first to have received an
indirect echo of Ptolemaic Egypt, gave to the Mediterranean East the new name
of Ta-ch'in when, at the end of the 1st century A.D., they entered into relations
with the Syrian East." There is nothing strange, therefore, in the idea that
Egyptian traders and jugglers should come by sea to China at the end of the
1st century A.D. But if Tan is Tanis, what are we to make of Yung-yu-tiao
(*ywong-yau-d'ieu), who reigned over Tan from 97 to 131 A.D., during the
reigns of Nerva, Trajan and Hadrian ? I can make nothing of the name unless,
by dint of perseverance, one may extract from it the imperial name Augustus.
If the Tan, on coming to China, gave themselves out to be envoys from
Augustus, it is merely what the Ta-ch'in traders called themselves in 166 A.D.,
in claiming to be an embassy from Ngan-tun (Marcus Aurelius Antoninus).
The Tan envoys who came to China in 97 and 121 A.D. plainly passed
through Burma, for they arrived at Yung-ch'ang, the extreme south-western
prefecture of China, the headquarters of which lies east of T'êng-yüeh, some
60 or 70 miles from the present Burma frontier. Whether they followed the
line of the Irawaddy or cut across the north of Burma, it is difficult to say; the
latter is, I think, more probable.
