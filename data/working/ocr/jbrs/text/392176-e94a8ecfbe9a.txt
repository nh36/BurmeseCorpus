Abhidhan is the word for a dictionary in Burmese. It has been
the name generally designated to Abhidhānappadipikā, the Pali
dictionary of synonyms compiled in Ceylon in the 12th century. Most
probably this famous work was introduced to Pagan just after its
compilation and has been studied by the Burmese clergy ever since.
A commentary on it was written by a Burman scholar at Pinya in the
14th century. Internal evidence shows that the commentator consulted
Amarakos'a, the Sanskrit dictionary of the 6th century, and its
commentaries. So I venture to conclude that lexicons and lexicography
have been known to the Burmans for some centuries now.
However, the said dictionaries of Pali and Sanskrit are different
from the modern dictionaries in conception. They are books of
synonyms, something like Roget's Thesaurus, all the names given to a
particular thing being grouped together and put into verse for the
purpose of memorisation. The proper sense of the word "dictionary"
came to be known to the Burmans only in the 19th century when a
certain Mr. Charles Lane compiled an Anglo-Burmese dictionary with
the help of the Prince of Mekkhara in 1833 at Ava. I should like to
mention here in passing that the Prince had a copy of Dr. Johnson's
English Dictionary in his collection of Western books. Side by side
with the study of foreign languages such as Pali and Sanskrit (and later
English also) the lexical study of the mother tongue was also
carried on, consciously or unconsciously, by the Burman scholars. For
the first beginnings of Burmese lexicography are to be found in the
nissayas or word-for-word glosses in the vernacular with which Pali texts
were furnished. Nissayas are as old as Pagan. Some short specimens are
found in the Pagan inscriptions, both in Mon and in Burmese. The titles
of some of them by a learned monk called Shin Guṇavataṁsaka of the
early 13th century are recorded in the Pitaka-thamaing. Word-for-word
translations of the Pali and Sanskrit dictionaries have also been made

and revised many a time. An Abhidhānnissaya and an Amarakosanissaya

There is another class of literature which is more lexicographical
in nature than the nissayas. It consists of the glossaries of archaic and
hard words which are explained either in current Burmese or in Pali.
But it seems that they started rather late i. e. about the 16th century
when people began to feel the pressure of those obsolete words no
longer bearable.
But, as far as I can find out, a dictionary of the Burmese language
as we understand it nowadays was first compiled by Dr. Judson, with a
few vocabularies previous to his bigger enterprise done by Western
scholars before him. Two of these vocabularies were F. Carey's
'A Grammar of the Burman Language, to which is added a list of the
simple roots from which the language is derived' (Serampore 1814)
and G. H. Hough's 'An English and Burman Vocabulary' (Serampore
1825). Dr. Judson's dictionary was a Burmese-English rendering. It
was first printed posthumously at Maulmain in 1852. Many revised
editions of this work have appeared ever since.
In 1913 it was suggested by Mr. C. W. Dunn that Judson's
Dictionary be revised and since then scholars like the late Dr. J. A.
Stewart, Dr. Hla Pe, Mr. C. W. Dunn, and Mr. H. F. Searle, have
worked on "A A Burmese-English Dictionary" of which the first part
came out in 1941.
During these pregnant years a Buddhist monk of Pyapon, U Obhāsa
by name, was working quietly and single-handed on a purely Burmese
dictionary. He completed it but unfortunately it was lost during the
War. But after the War he worked on it again and in 1947 he managed
to bring out the first part of his dictionary and later another part, but
since then he has stopped his dictionary work altogether.
In 1944 I was entrusted by the Pyinnyatagun Athin or the Burmese
Academy now defunct, with the job of compiling a Burmese-Burmese
dictionary. Since then I have been working on it off and on for ten years.
I have published one part and another part is in the press. So much
for a short sketch of the history of lexicography in Burma. (I have
left out the story of later Pali dictionaries as they are well known
to-day.)

A lexicographer has to concern himself not only in the language
itself, but also, and very much so, with its written form.
In 1849 Dr. Judson wrote in the preface to his Burmese and English
Dictionary: "It is exceedingly difficult to settle the orthography of a
language which has never been printed, and in which there are no
grammars nor dictionaries." The same difficulty has been experienced
by me, though in a lesser degree. Burmese orthography is still in a
liquid state, in spite of the fact that the language has been in print for
about one hundred and fifty years. There are works on orthography
dating back to the early 17th century. I have consulted over fifty of
them. They disagree in many points. The differences are sometimes
so incongruous that one simply despairs of finding a solution, and is
often given up to despondency. I admit that orthography of a language
changes with time; even that of such a well kept classical language as
Sanskrit or Pali changes. But when it changes out of all semantic and
etymological recognition I think it has gone a little too far, and that
there should be some attempt to arrest the change at some point.
I am afraid I am rather digressing. To come back to my point
I should like to tell you that generally speaking there are three parts to
a word or part of a word in Burmese, namely the initial, the medial
and the final. The initial of a word is often written in two or three
different ways in different works and at different times or in the same
work and in the same period. For instance the word for Buddha is
purhā at Pagan, phura at Ava and bhurā: to-day, not to speak of
barā: the latest member of the clan. There are five labial symbols
in the Burmese alphabet and all the letters, except ma the nasal, have
been used for the initial part of that word. Two of the above spellings
are in current use, namely phura: and bhurā. Here I may be allowed
to tell you that there are two schools of orthography in connection with
the two letters pha and bha, i.e. the pha school and the bha school.
There has been a tussel between the two since the time of the good old
kings. At one point a compromise was made that the word for Buddha
or its image or a pagoda is to be spelt with a bha and the word for a
queen, a derivative of the same word, is to be spelt with a pha, as
miphura. The Chief Queen of King Thibaw was said to have been
very much annoyed with this. Now I have told you that there are
two different initials for the same word with the same or almost
the same meaning. Which shall I accept and which shall I reject?
If, as is the case with dictionaries of other languages, both the
spellings are to be entered in the dictionary which one deserves the place
of precedence?

Difficulties of the same nature exist among other letters: for
instance, kyan and gyan, two variant spellings, for a spinning top; kale:
and khale for a baby; cani: and jani: for a wife; carap and jarap
for a rest house; che: and jhe: for a market; chi, ji and jhi: for a
plum; titut, di: dut, and dhi: dhut for an owl; thā, dā: and dha:
for a knife, etc. etc,
Among the medials -y- and -r- often get mixed up. The letter r
has lost its original phonetic value in standard Burmese and is
pronounced exactly as the letter y. So there occur many words which,
though differently written, have the same sound, and thus incorrect
spellings have crept in and some of them have become so established
that it is almost impossible to correct them. To avoid this difficulty
authors and scholars have left us guides in the form of rhymes. Two
words which have the same sound but different medials -y- or -r- are
made to rhyme. These rhymes called khweda are very helpful in
determining the orthography of a word but they do not cover the
whole range of the language and sometimes they disagree
among themselves. Apparent absurdities, supported by standard
orthographical works, also exist. For instance the transitive
verb 'to pulverise' in Burmese is khye, and logically its intransitive form
should be kye. But it is not so. Kre is the form recommended by the
spelling books. When in the negative sense, however, kye is accepted.
Another instance is as follows. The word for 'to fear' is krok, while
the word for 'to frighten' is khyok, which actually should be khrok.
And one more instance to complete the picture is the word for to fall
off, as leaves or fruits from trees'. It is krwe. You may expect a word
like khrwe for its transitive form. But no. It is khywe as given in the
spelling books. These are the problems in connection with the medials.
Now we come to the finals. The case of the finals is not so bad
as those of the initials and the medials. The regularity of the finals is
well observed and preserved in the standard poetical works, which are
meant to be and regarded as orthographical works too. However
confusion arises when folk etymology comes in. Many people have
played with folk etymology in Burmese for many years. They take their
dictum from the Pali grammarians that Pali is the mother of all the
languages of the world. They try to derive Burmese words, even such
primary words as the sun and the moon, from Pali. Shin Raṭṭhasāra,
the distinguished authority on orthography, has pup with a -p for
‘putrid' in his rhymes, while the folk etymologists prefer put with a
-t, because they argue that this word is a derivative of Pali pūti

Now I have talked to you about some of the apparent orthographi-
cal problems. These are due to the defective nature of our extant
texts. No autograph text seems to have come down to us, except the
original inscriptions. Our books are made from transmitted texts or
copies of copies. During the course of many years, which were often
disturbed by wars, errors, both voluntary and involuntary, have crept
into the texts. Mistakes on the part of scribes and correctors, misunder-
standing and misinterpretation, misplaced learning and irrelevant
scholarship are also responsible for them. When readings are faulty,
interpretation also becomes faulty and they are a great stumbling block
in lexicography. Texts which are edited with the help of textual
criticism are a long-felt desideratum. The Burma Research Society
has published quite a number of texts, but they are only collated texts;
they are not critical editions in the proper sense of the word. Here I
should like to cite an instance. When I was a student I was baffled by
a word in an original inscription. It was ukhruiw which occurs in the
sentence awici athay naray ukhruiw phlac ciy liy sate of an
inscription of 1326 A.D. The word was strange, the shape of the
word was strange. The lack of tonal marks made it stranger. One
word which has some phonetic resemblance to it is u: khyui, ‘horn of

studies I came across a word variously printed as ukhyui :, ukhyui :,

days. The context and situation are similar, if not identical; the only
difference lies in the spelling viz. the medials --r- and -y-. In such a
case the speakers of the Arakanese dialect can help. So I approached
them and made inquiries whether they had a word like ukhrui:.
Without hesitation they said yes and that the word meant 'cooked food
slightly burnt and adhering to the bottom of cooking pots'. Only
then the problem was solved, both semantically and orthographically.
Because a sinner is always cooked in the hells and to be cooked in a
hell like ukhrui: or khrui: (according to standard usage) is an
expression still current among the people.
Now I do not feel fair to worry you any more with my problems,
which are numerous and various. So in conclusion I should like to
say with Dr. T. W. Rhys Davids who writes in the Foreword to his
Pali-English Dictionary, "Anybody familiar with this sort of work
will know what care and patience, what scholarly knowledge and
judgement are involved in the collection of such material, in the
sorting, the sifting and final arrangement of it, in the adding of "cross
references, in the consideration of etymological puzzles, in the
comparison and correction of various or faulty readings, and in the
verification of references given by others, or found in the indexes."
