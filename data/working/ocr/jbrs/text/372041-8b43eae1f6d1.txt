Introduction
This paper is a preliminary investigation into the occurrence of
rainfall of Central Burma in relation to certain synoptic situations.
It must, therefore, be emphasized that this piece of work is just a
forerunner of many researches that should be undertaken later in con-
nection with the study of weather and climate of the whole country.
Owing to the limited amount of data available, this investigation was
carried out using the data collected during the post-war years only.
References made for this piece of work are given at the end of the
paper.
Central Burma is known to us as the Dry Zone, as it comprises
of the driest parts of the country. The rainfall in the Dry Zone is,
on the average, 20" to 40" a year. It is bounded on the north, east,
south and west by rainier regions. It is generally believed that the
small amount of rainfall that occurs in the Dry Zone is due to the
Arakan Yoma which shadows it from the effects of the Southwest
Monsoon and due to the absence of high ranges in Central Burma to
bring about the orographic effect.
The Annual Rainfall of the Dry Zone
Table I gives the normal monthly rainfall of some of the places
of the Dry Zone. It will be seen that the rainy season for the Dry
Zone is from May to October i.e., the normal Monsoon period. June
and September are the rainiest months. Outside this rainy season.
(i.e., November-April) a certain amount of rainfall occurs. Al-
though this outside monsoon period rainfall amounts to about 10% of
the annual average, it is important to know when the unseasonal
rainfall will occur, as it can cause widespread damage to dry season
crops.
Table II gives the maximum rainfall in 24 hours that had occur-
red at some of the places of the Dry Zone. Iyer and Zaffar studied
the distribution of heavy rainfall in India and Burma, and found that
in the Dry Zone of Burma there were less than twentyfive occasions
of heavy fall of 3" and above in 24 hours during the period 1891-1920,
and out of these there were less than five occasions on which heavy
fall of 5" and above occurred. From this, it is apparent that rainfall
of 2" and above in 24 hours could be regarded as heavy rain in the
Dry Zone.

The Premonsoon Period (April and May)
The month of April is normally the hottest month. The surface
pressure gradients are weak, but they are still able to draw in moisture
from the Bay of Bengal into the Dry Zone. The heating of the land
surfaces causes the accentuation of low pressure area over the Dry
Zone particularly during afternoons and evenings. By the month of
May this low pressure area is much more prominent and wider. The
general surface wind directions are from south and therefore there is
an inflow of air near the surface from the Andaman Sea through the
Irrawaddy and Sittang Valleys. The 'mean monthly wind data at 1. 2
and 3 km. during the months of April and May at Mandalay, Rangeon
and Akyab and the streamlines at these levels show that the air over
the Dry Zone at levels 3 km. and below have travel over sea and are
overrun by presumably drier and colder air of land travel at 3 km.
and above. These conditions are sufficient enough for instability to
cause afternoon and evening thunderstorms and showers in the Dry
Zone during April and May.
Apart from the rainfall due to these thunderstorms during April
and May, certain amounts of rainfall are caused by the storms or
depressions that occur in the Bay of Bengal. These storms induce
widespread rain in the Dry Zone with some locally heavy falls.
Storms of April and May generally originate in the South Bay
of Bengal or in the Andaman Sea. They usually move initially in a
northwesterly direction and recurve towards northeast in the neigh-
bourhood of latitude 18-20°N, before striking Arakan or the East
Pakistan coasts. A few of these storms, which originate in the Anda-
man Sea, cross the Deltaic and Martaban coasts of Burma particularly
if they occur during the first half of May.
The onset of the southwest Monsoon normally takes place about
the third week of May in Lower Burma. The monsoon usually
establishes in the Dry Zone during the last week of May. The Bay
of Bengal depressions and cyclones during the month of May usually
helps the onset of the monsoon to take place either slightly earlier or
about the normal date in the Dry Zone.

Southwest Monsoon Period
The normal period of Southwest Monsoon rain in the Dry Zone
is from the last week of May to the end of the second week of
October.
The character of weather during this period all over the country
is controlled very much by the formation of monsoon depressions.
These depressions are usually formed in the Head Bay of Bengal in
area north of latitude 18°N. The Head Bay depressions are rarely
severe and those of June usually move west-northwest, northwest or
north. In July these Head Bay depressions nearly all move in westerly
westnorthwesterly directions. Occasionally, a depression during this
month originates as a typhoon in the China Sea and crosses Indo-
China, Northern Thailand and Central Burma, before it enters the
Head Bay. These China Sea typhoons after crossing the Indo-China
coast are weakened considerably and the residual low pressure wave
moves westwards across Central Burma and passes into the Head
Bay where a depression develops. During the passage of these low
pressure waves, it is possible to have widespread rainfall in the Dry
Zone.
Owing to the paucity of observations, it is not very easy to detect
these westward moving low pressure waves across the Dry Zone but
sometimes they can be recognised from the trends of isobars, the
24-hour pressure changes, upper air observations and the progressive
unusual rainfall in the Central Burma. These low pressure waves are
more pronounced during August when they travel westwards at much
lower latitudes than in July.

districts of the Dry Zone. Some of the heavy falls were at Tantabin
(Shwebo) 3.64" on the 8th., Amarapura 3.15" on the 9th. and Natogyi

Interplay between ‘old' and ‘fresh' monsoon air. It is also noticed
that a spell of weak monsoon followed by another period of strong or
moderate monsoon causes some widespread rain in the Dry Zone.
This may be due to the interplay between the "old monsoon air"
(monsoon air trapped and desiccated by the mountains of North Burma
and the Shan Plateau) and the "fresh monsoon air" which brings up
moisture from the sea. This "fresh monsoon air" gains entry into
the Dry Zone through the Irrawaddy and Sittang Valleys or this "fresh
monsoon air” can be monsoon air which reaches the Dry Zone across
the Arakan Yoma which partially desiccates it.
Monsoon rainfall in the Dry Zone. As the rainfall during this
period is essentially due to the Southwest Monsoon. the activation or
the strengthening of monsoon along Arakan and the adjoining Delta
region can cause some widespread rain in the Dry Zone, although
there may or may not be any falls which can be classified as heavy.
September and October. These months form the later part of the
Southwest Monsoon season. In the Dry Zone the rainfall for Septem-
ber can be as much or even more than that of June in a particular
year. This is explained partly by the frequency of typhoons or rem-
nants of typhoons that go across Burma during this period. The
direction of movement of these disturbances are either westerly or west-
southwesterly, and their paths lie along a lower latitude (between 15°N).
than those of the July disturbances (low pressure waves). Owing to
the fact that these disturbances have to travel through the less moun-
tainous regions, their chance of survival is rather great and the condi-
tions for development into depressions are more favourable when they
emerge into the East Central Bay of Bengal or the Andaman Sea. An
example of China Sea typhoon which passed through Central Burma
as a land depression is given below:
Land depression of the 6th.-the 10th. September 1951.
evening of the 6th. September 1951, a prominent low pressure wave
was moving in westnorthwesterly direction across the northern part of
Thailand. On the 7th. morning it was observed to be more prominent
than the usual low pressure waves. On the 7th. evening and the 8th.

isolated heavy falls occurred in the Dry Zone during the period from
8th. to 10th. September.

from the country. The normal date of withdrawal from the Dry Zone

October is characteristic of the post-monsoon storm period. The
China Sea typhoons and their remnants which move westwards or
westsouthwestwards over the plains of Thailand emerge into the
Andaman Sea and develop into depressions or cyclones which either
move northwest with a recurving towards north or northeast later, or
move in the westerly direction to the east India coast and again
emerge into the Arabian Sea after passing over the Indian Peninsula.
Some of these disturbances whose actual path lie through the head of
the Gulf of Thailand gain more energy and pick up additional mois-
ture and are pretty severe once they emerge into the Andaman Sea.
Among the storms of October, only those which take some north-
westerly tracks produce rain in the Dry Zone.
During the month of September and October, thunderstorm acti-
vities increase generally in the Dry Zone. These thunderstorms are
caused by the interplay between the air of continental origin and the
monsoon air which remains in the lower layers. These thunderstorms
are generally accompanied by showers which some times produce
noteworthy amounts of rainfall.
November. The years on which this investigation was conducted
did not have any November storm that caused rainfall in the Dry
Zone.
The normal tracks of the easterly low pressure waves lie south
of latitude 15°N. and they emerge into the North Andaman Sea or
even into the South Bay of Bengal.
The thunderstorm activities are still widespread during this month
particularly within the first two weeks. These thunderstorms are
sometimes fairly widespread over an area of a district size. During
November, at levels 1 kilometer and above, the winds have generally
travel over land from southern part of China. This air overruns the
monsoon air which remains in the lower levels, favouring the condi-
tions for instability to cause thunderstorms and showers.
December and January. By December and January, the charac-
teristic winter conditions are well established with cold northerly
winds at the surface and the strong westerlies up and above. There
are no typhoons or their secondaries which pass over the Dry Zone
during these months, but the Bay of Bengal can still have depressions,
some of which are due to the typhoons and their remnants that pass
into the South Bay after crossing Lower Thailand and the Malayan
Peninsula. These storms of December in the Bay of Bengal either
move in the westerly direction towards Madras coast of India or
move initially in a northwesterly direction with recurvings towards
northeast heading for Arakan and the adjoining deltaic area. These
storms are usually filled up or weakened before they strike Burma
coast. They can cause considerably widespread rain in the Dry Zone.

Storm of December 1950. Markedly unsettled conditions were
noticed on the afternoon of the 1st. December 1950 in the South Bay
of Bengal. A depression was formed on the morning of the
2nd. December with its central region near Latitude 9 N. and Longi-
tude 86°E. It moved northwards and was centred on the next
morning near Latitude 12°N. and Longitude 86°E. It then intensified
into a cyclonic storm of moderate intensity and moved into the Central
Bay of Bengal with its central region near Latitude 14 N. and Longi-
tude 88°E. It was pretty severe on the 5th. morning and its centre
was near Latitude 16°N, and Longitude 90°E. It crossed Arakan
coast near Sandoway during the early hours of the 6th. December and
weakened. This storm had caused widespread rain in the Dry Zone
from the 4th. to the 7th. Some heavy falls of 3" and above were re-
corded on the 6th. morning in Thayetmyo, Minbu, Mandalay, Meiktila
and Yamethin districts.
Rainfall due to such cyclone in the Bay of Bengal over the Dry
Zone can be regarded as induced by the cyclone itself and here again
we can assume the interplay between the different air masses that have
invaded the Dry Zone. It is believed a series of small and active dis-
continuity zones are formed between the tropical maritime or equa-
torial maritime air and tropical continental or polar continental air.
January, February and March. The period January to March or
even upto the middle of April are characteristic of eastward moving
disturbances which are generally secondaries of the temperate zone
disturbances. These disturbances have their origin in the Middle East
and travel eastwards over Northern India and some of them reach
North Burma Areas inducing same weather. The mean track of these
disturbances lie about Latitude 25°N. but some of them travel at much
lower latitude and effect the Dry Zone.
These disturbances which are known as Western Disturbances in
India, some times show up quite conspicuously as moving low pressure
areas but majority of them could not be followed on day-to-day chart.
They are, however, detected by the shapes of isobars, 24-hour pressure
changes and departures, upper air observations and by the progress of
weather from west to east.
Example of Western Disturbance of January 1948. This distur-
bance which was located in the Central part of India on the 4th. January
1948 was moving slowly eastwards. It was located on the morning
of the 5th. January over Central Provinces and on the morning of the
6th. it was in Bihar and the adjoining western part of Bengal. It then
moved over the Head Bay and across the North Arakan into North
Burma causing widespread rain on the 6th, and 7th. January. Note-
worthy falls of 1" and above in 24 hours were recorded on the 6th.
and the 7th. morning in Minbu, Magwe, Mandalay and Kyaukse dis-
tricts. Some of these heavy falls were the maximum rainfall in 24 hours
ever recorded in the Dry Zone. Similar disturbance occurred between
the 21st, and the 28th. January 1951, when a low pressure wave passed
from northeast India into the Northwest Burma on the 21st. On the
22nd., the wave invaded into the Dry Zone and on the 23rd. it remained
over the same region causing widespread rain with some locally heavy

Concluding Remarks. From the foregoing examples of synoptic
situations for the years 1948-1951, it will be seen that the rainfall in
Central Burma is the maximum during the period of summer monsoon
or the Southwest Monsoon. It is well known that the character of
weather during monsoon is controlled very much by the formation of
monsoon depressions around the Head Bay of Bengal and these de-
pressions are always accompanied by a period of strong monsoon
along Arakan and the adjoining Deita. Before the outbreak of these
depressions, low pressure waves from east move across the Dry Zone
and cause widespread rain. The rainfall during the monsoon period
in the Dry Zone is due to the interplay between fresh monsoon air
and monsoon air which is trapped and desiccated by hills of North
Burma Regions and the Shan Plateau. The fresh monsoon air can
either be the one which has just passed over the Arakan Yoma and
desiccated or the air that comes up the valleys of the Irrawaddy and
the Sittang as some of the synoptic situations are favourable for the
air to flow up these valleys.
As stated above, the rainfalls during the pre-monsoon and post-
monsoon periods are due to the Bay of Bengal cyclonic storms.
During the occurrence of these storms, it seems there is an interplay
between the dry continental air and the air that enters into the Dry
Zone from the Bay of Bengal.
Also quite a good deal of rainfall in the Dry Zone are caused
by local thunderstorms caused by instability.
