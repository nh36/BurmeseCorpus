THE ERADICATION OF FICUS
by
Ko Ko Lay 1
In 1952 my attention was drawn to the problem of eradicating the
strangling fig by a directive from the University Council. It is a well-
known fact that the strangling fig grows on pagodas, monuments
pucca buildings, stupas etc. and causes extensive damage. The species
commonly found in Burma are Ficus religiosa (Peepul or Bawdi-
nyaung), F. bengalensis (Banyan or Nyaung) and F. Benjamina (Java
fig). Of these the most common one and that which causes the greatest
damage is Ficus religiosa. The fruits of this plant are eaten by birds
and the seeds pass through the digestive tracts without any apparent
damage. Indeed it is claimed that the seeds are not viable unless they
are treated by the digestive juices of birds. From the droppings of
birds the seeds germinate on any masonary structure or in the forking
or axile of trees. The young seedling soon establishes itself by sending
extensive roots which bind the plant very strongly to its support. Once
established it is very difficult to get rid of this plant, as the roots are
able to regenerate new plants every rainy season even if the aerial parts
have been removed. It is also very difficult to get to the roots as they
are extremely well buried in the substrata, whether that be a trunk of a
tree or a crevice in a masonary structure. Eventually the establishment
of the plant causes utter ruin to the structure on which it is growing.
I received a copy of the experiment conducted by Dr. K. T. Jacob,
Director, Bose Institute, Calcutta, who pointed out the use of Dicotox,
a chemical preparation manufactured by May and Baker. In the letter
was also mentioned the strength of Dicotox used at Calcutta and a
desire that similar experiments may be conducted in Burma in order
to have "a set of comparable results, and provide data for a further
eradication campaign". Unfortunately I was unable to secure Dicotox
in 1952 before the rains, and therefore was unable to conduet any
experiment that year.
In the meantime from local enquiry, I found that the Imperial
Chemical Industries were putting on the market an arsenic preparation
called 'Weedicide', which they thought might also be of value in the

The experiment was conducted on accessible plants on the University
buildings at Rangoon. Most of the plants grew on the sides of the
Faculty of Science Building and some on the sides of the Faculty of
Arts Building and a few on the calverts between these two buildings on
the Chancellor Road. Experiment was conducted only on Ficus religiosa
as the other species were not available.
Experiment
Dicotox As suggested by the Director of Bose Institute, I used
four percentages of Dicotox suspension, viz. 9, 13, 17, and 23. For
each percentage 7 plants of various sizes and growing in various
localities were selected. Spraying was done on three consecutive sunny
days at about 7 a.m. The actual results for the experiment are given
below:
9% Dicotox-The plants selected varied from one, which is but
5 inches high, with many small leaves, and a profusely branched collar
of about 1 inch in diameter with the roots well hiden behind drain pipes
to one about 3 feet in height, much branched with many large leaves
and the basal collar of about 4 inches in diameter, with the roots
profuse, and creeping in all directions, and having their extremities well
hidden between cracks in the brickwork. Observation showed that the
youngest plants withered away after 6 days from the last treatment.
The bigger ones had completely withered leaves on the 4th. day, but the
stem remained green for over 21 days. The stems finally abcissed off in
about a month's time. Observation after the rains showed that the
plants were not completely dead, and that in the case of the bigger
plants new shoots tended to develop from the collar. The younger ones.
died completely.
13% Dicotox-The suspension was sprayed exactly in the same
way as described above and the plants selected were also very similar.

The same experiment was repeated again with only two sprayings
and the results were quite satisfactory. The plants were quite dead
within a month and there was no regeneration after the rains. With
only one spraying the results were not satisfactory as some of the plants
had only withered leaves and regenerated in about 2 months time when
the rains started.
Arsenic Weedicide-With this chemical the plants were not
sprayed. I followed the instructions which required cutting a plant
with a dah or a machet and pouring the solution of Weedicide on
the exposed surfaces. Two percentages of Weedicide were used viz. 25
and 50. The solutions were poured twice with ten days' interval in
between. The experiment was conducted in the middle of March 1953.
Four plants each were tried with the two percentages. The results are
given below:
25% Weedicide-The plants used were all large, with the roots
embedded fully or only partially exposed, and the crown more or less
on the surface. After the first pouring and before the second pouring,
the lateral roots, leaves and most of the branches were dead, but the
crown was slightly green. Ten days after the second
pouring the plants were completely dead, and there was no
regeneration after the rains. In most cases the plants had fallen off
from their perches.

5% Potassium bichromate - Three rather large plants were
sprayed with this solution. Each plant was sprayed thrice
sprayed on three consecutive days, another on alternate days, and the
third on every fifth day. All the plants withered but did not completely
die. The one sprayed at 5 days' interval regenerated during the rainy

15% Potassium bichromate-Sprayed on three plants in the
same manner described above. The plants looked completely dead in
• a month's time. There was no apparent difference in the plants.

the rains.
Conclusion
Strangling fig can be effectively eradicated by using 13% Dicotox
or 25% Weedicide or by 10% Potassium bichromate. Dicotox is
advantageous when the plants are inaccessible as the solution can be
sprayed from quite some distance. Arsenic is very effective when used
on rather large plants, its principal disadvantages are that the plants.
must be first wounded, which may not be possible in every case,
and that it is very poisonous and might be dangerous to life.
Personally I think Potassium bichromate is easiest to use, though the
spraying must be more on the walls then on the plants themselves, and
the solution has to be used very much more liberally. All three
solutions are effective in eradicating nyaungbin and can be used as
availability or circumstances my dictate.
