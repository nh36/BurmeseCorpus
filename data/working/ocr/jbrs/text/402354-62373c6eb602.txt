DATED BUDDHA IMAGES OF NORTHERN SIAM
by
A. B. Griswold. Artibus Asiae, Publishers, Ascona,
Switzerland, 1957.
This is a delightful book to read and to look at. It illustrates with
annotations the bronze images of Northern Siam covering a period of
four centuries and ranging from royal donations to crude works of
village craftsmen. The author has selected these images because of
their known dates and has used the dates as a practical means of
determining the chronology of Buddhist sculpture.
The earliest specimens (6th-12th century) come from the Mon
School of Dvaravati with their heavy facial features and incised lips.
Then there are various other types such as the Sukhodaya high classic
style of images looking ‘like visions seen in ecstasy', products of the
first school of Tai art in Siam; and the famous lion-type images with
their arrogant and majestic facial expressions.
The author has discovered the disappearance of a leaf from the
original manuscript of the Garland of Time, causing a hiatus from 1455
to 1476 (p. 44). He thinks that since in 1556 Northern Siam was
conquered by the Burmese maybe it was the Burmese who for political
reasons tore a dangerous page from Siamese history (p. 64, note 38).
In considering this point let us examine Number 82, the finest type of
lion-images, height 2.20 m. with a bilingual inscription, Burmese in
front, Tai in rear. The Tai inscription as translated in the Book reads:
"In CS 927, Year of the Ox, second year of the decade, in the second
month (October-November)... on the 8th day of the waxing moon,
which is surely a good and auspicious day, at 3 in the afternoon, the
Commanding General of the Victorious Armies and Protector of the
City, servant of the pious King of Kings who is Lord of the White
Elephant and the Golden Palace, Sovereign over all the princes and
mandarins, having been appointed as defender of religion and protector
of the people in Chiengmai, being mindful of the religion and desirous
of promoting it, and at the same time proposing to transfer the merit of
his act to the Princess Regent who is the ancestress of the city, joined

together with the princes and officers both military and civil and all
citizens intent on merit-making, and collected a quantity of Buddha
images that have been broken by rioters. For they proposed to save
these images from annihilation, by melting them down and using the
metal to cast a new image. The duty of supervising the collection was
assigned to Mün Luang Lek, Mün Luang Jâ Bân, Mün Nangsü, and the
Burmese officials on the appointed day... Then in the 4th month
(December-January), on the 13th day of the waxing moon, at 1-30 in
the morning... they cast this Buddha image, containing metal of an
approximate weight of 50 million; and they performed the life-giving
rites, naming it the King Mengrai Buddha, to be an object of veneration
for men and gods to the end of 5,000 years. In consideration of the
meritorious act of casting this Mengrâi memorial and covering it with
lacquer and gold leaf, the Commanding General of the Victorious Army,
the Protector of the City, hopes for longevity in the present existence,
followed by rebirth in a realm of the Devas, namely either the
Tăvatimsa or the Tushita, to remain there until the age when Ariya
Maitreya shall descend to be the Lord; at that time he hopes to have
been reborn on earth in a good family, namely the family of a prince
or a high-ranking army officer, and when Maitreya shall have attained
Enlightenment he hopes to be ordained in the monkhood by Maitreya
himself, and finally to reach Nirvana with him... (pp. 92, 93)." We
would like to be given an opportunity of reading the Burmese inscrip-
tion to see if it is written in as grand a style as the Tai. This is the
Burmese General who Mr. Griswold thinks stole the missing leaf of
manuscript. Burning the whole manuscript rather than tearing a leaf
would be the Burmese way of doing things. Says a Burmese proverb:
if you want to destroy a tree, do not be content with cutting the
branches, but uproot it. On the other hand Mr. Griswold gives a good
account of the Burmese behaviour in treating the King with considera-
tion instead of deposing him and in taking him prisoner only when he
was accused of planning a rebellion (p 58 ). On his death in Burma
the King "achieved a surprising reincarnation: he became 'Yun Bayin
Nat', one of the thirty-seven Spirit Lords' of the Burmese pantheon,
and as such he is still worshipped to-day (p. 65, note 52.)."
