ACCOUNT OF THE ETHNOGRAPHICAL AND
LINGUISTIC SURVEY OF BURMA
by
L. F. Taylor M.A., 1.E.S., (retd.)
1. I have just read, with great interest and pleasure, Professor
Kenneth G. Orr's recent paper entitled: "The Place of Anthropology
among the Social Studies of Burma". He has brought together and
briefly summarised a considerable number of studies, mentioning even
a few of my own. But unfortunately he was not in a position to state
what materials had been collected and what work done by the
Ethnographical and Linguistic Survey of Burma because very little has
so far appeared in printed publications. Perhaps this is an appropriate
montent for me to contribute some information on this subject.
History of the Survey
2. Having previously gained some experience in investigating
and recording languages and dialects spoken in Burma, I was in 1923
attached to University College, Rangoon as "Research Lecturer in Indo-
Chinese Ethnography and Linguistics”. The purpose of this appoint-
ment, so I was informed, was to enable me to establish forthwith a
proper Ethnographical and Linguistic Survey of Burma. Unfortunately
however no funds whatever had been provided for my work. The
Government of Burma considered that the University of Rangoon
ought to make that essential provision, while the University looked to
the Government to do the same. During the two and a half years that
this impasse lasted I did some touring at my own expense, but occupied
most of my time in looking through thousands of official reports and
files dating from about 1830 and extracting every item of Ethnographi-
cal or Linguistic interest. In this way I went through the records
belonging to the Political, Settlement and other departments at the
Secretariat, including those in the Secretariat Library. I also received
many files from Divisional and District record rooms as well as from
the Shan States. I was able in this manner to amass a considerable
amount of information, partly Linguistic but mainly Ethnographical,

the ideal method, but instead I had to collect as many

way I might at least make a skeleton Survey of the principal and
representative languages of all the groups. Eventually my work was
extended for another two years, terminating in 1929. It was therefore
only from the middle of 1926 till the middle of 1929, that is for a little
over three years, that I had available the funds necessary for my work.
In 1929 was felt the coming of the great financial depression and there
was little likelihood that the work would be extended and financed any
further. The Survey therefore went into abeyance and I returned to
duty as Inspector of Schools. Mr. Snow, who was then the Director
of Public Instruction, with the approval of the then Secretary to
Government, agreed to my suggestion to allow me to continue the work of

"It is open to Mr. Taylor to resign the service of Government

4. As a result of this rather fascist decree reversing the permis-
sion already given me and forbidding me to use my own leisure time in
my own way upon scientific and useful work, and having regard to the
importance of the Survey, I decided reluctantly to make the great
financial sacrifice and retire upon proportionate pension in order to
carry on the work, hoping to secure financial assistance from other more
enlightened sources. (The length and severity of the depression
hindered these efforts.) I was also determined to vindicate the quality
and quantity of my work and my method of conducting it, about which
doubts were seemingly held in certain quarters. Having retired I carried
on for as long as possible in Burma and then took my materials home
to Europe. It is possible that by doing this I may have saved them
from total loss, for had they remained in Rangoon they might not have
survived the Japanese invasion.

5. Soon after my return home I decided to take steps to clear
up all doubts about the quality of my work. I was determined to
vindicate not only its quality but also my own conduct of the Survey.
I therefore opened up a correspondence with the Vice-Chancellor of
Rangoon University. The latter responded with the suggestion that
I "should permit the University to ask a competent third party, say
Dr. Blagden, to report in detail" on the work accomplished. This
seemed to me a very fair proposal. Dr. Blagden held the full confidence
of the University authorities, was acquainted with some of the
languages of Burma, and was an Honorary Doctor of the University.
Also, to my own knowledge, he was very competent for the purpose.
I therefore hired the empty basement of a London bookshop and
stacked up my materials on tables and benches ready for inspection.
Dr. Blagden paid several visits to my materials, listed them, examined
whatever interested him, and took away samples of his own selection
for more detailed investigation. The result was a long and detailed
report in which he fully vindicated me in every way. I also sent samples
to Sir George Grierson who was the chief consulting authority on such

"The great difficulty, of course, will be in the case of

is read to him. The recording machine will, of course, be stopped,
while the reciter is being told what to recite. It will be started

I had, at first, intended to confine the selection of languages to those
that were comparatively well known and of which well educated
speakers would be available. My first proposal submitted to the
Government for sanction contained twelve languages only. But the
method of recording contained in the instructions suggested the
feasibility of including many languages spoken by illiterate and back-
ward tribes, thereby greatly extending the scope and value of the
undertaking. But when the recording expert arrived he told me that
these instructions were impracticable, and that his machine once started
must run through to the end. The outcome was that many speakers
wandered far from the arranged texts. In some cases they recited
quite different pieces that they could more easily remember. It
followed therefore that in many cases the written texts were useless as
an accurate basis for the phonetic transcriptions. Such pieces would
have to be entirely reconstructed from the gramophone records them-
selves. Such reconstruction is not at all an easy matter, particularly
when the language is a hitherto unstudied one with neither dictionary
nor grammar available. I invite any doubter of this to attempt such
a reconstruction for himself.
After many attempts I submitted a few specimen transcriptions to
Dr. Daniel Jones, Professor of Phonetics in London University, and
asked him if he could get one of his students to check them up. He
was good enough to send me some helpful notes, and informed me
that :-
-
"It is by no means child's play to check such transcriptions
from the records. It takes a long time to do them correctly and
is quite trying work."
If the mere checking of a transcription is such trying work, the original
task of reconstructing such transcriptions from gramophone records
must be even more trying. Here again Prof. Daniel Jones agrees:-
"The work of making a transcription from a gramophone
record is extremely arduous and long.

Recent History of the Survey
23. Those interested in the Survey may like to know what efforts
have been made since I left Burma and how matters stand now. In
paras 17 to 19 above I related how, settled at Gibraltar, I had completed
the writing of a book entitled ; "Some Indo-Chinese Phonetic
Transcriptions". I also had the opportunity of doing much checking
up and arrangement of all my linguistic materials. With regard to the
book: When I began writing it I asked the Vice-Chancellor of
Rangoon University whether the University would assist in getting it
into print. The Vice-Chancellor in reply authorised me to employ a
well known Cambridge firm of printers to produce it at the expense of
the University. This I did and obtained several sets of proofs of the
entire book. One set, as stated above, was sent to the Vice-Chancellor
for his inspection. That set should still be in his records provided they
escaped the destructive hands of the Japanese. I was in the process of
correcting them when, on the 1st September, 1939, I was called up for
active service in the Navy and given a Commission. I was shore based
at Gibraltar, but my service duties were at first so heavy that I had to
put all proof correcting aside for the time being. Later the Military
authorities decided to take over the flat that I had been occupying for
their own purposes, and I was given a few hours notice to clear out.
As it was quite impossible to move my books and materials at such
short notice, the Royal Engineers moved all my belongings into one
large room, locked them up, and placed a seal upon the door. Here
they remained for almost four years. When the news arrived of the
occupation of Burma by the Japanese the printers of the book without
reference to myself, melted down all the type that had been set up.
There was certainly a shortage of this alloy and the printer evidently

25. As soon as the military authorities had unsealed the room

26. It is perhaps as a result of Dr. Blagden's and Sir George
Grierson's reports that in 1946 the Secretary of State for India and

grant be made to enable me to continue my work on the Survey. This
proposal was not accepted. I was informed that Burma had decided to
declare for independence and the outgoing caretaker Government felt

29. Another paper I have completed is entitled :
"The signifi-
nance of Word-groups in Descriptive and Comparative Indo-Chinese
Linguistics". These languages are mostly monosyllabic and isolating,
and the unchangeable word was for long regarded as a root. It is now
certain that many of these words are of compound structure, though
this fact has been concealed by extensive phonetic decay. We can find
large groups of words in each language characterised by having similar
sounds and expressing shades of the same meaning. The recognition
of this fact and the finding and proper grouping of such words is a new
development in the study of these languages, and is necessary before
basic word-analysis can be undertaken. The development of this
investigation will be of great importance for comparative purposes ⚫as
well as for throwing light upon the morphological histories and deve-
lopments of these languages. I refer in the paper to pioneer work done
by Dr. J. A. Stewart (J. B. R. S. 1912), Mr. Duroiselle (J. B. R. S. 1913),

30. Another paper dealing with word-groups is entitled:
