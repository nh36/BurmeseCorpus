THE RICE INDUSTRY OF BURMA. *
Burma is the largest exporter of rice of any country in the
world; her exports are more than the combined exports of Siam
and Indo-China, the only other exporting countries on a large scale.
It does not appear necessary to say more than this to emphasize
the vital importance of the subject to Burma.
The industry has many different aspects; there is the agri-
cultural aspect of the actual cultivation of the crop; there is the
technical aspect of the milling of the paddy into rice; there is the
commercial aspect of methods of buying and selling, and of
grading the rice for the market; and there is the economic aspect
of the disposal of the surplus of the crop which is not needed for
internal consumption. It is this last aspect with which I wish to
deal tonight, and very little will be said about the other aspects,
each of which affords ample material for a separate lecture.
Before considering the economic problems which the rice
industry raises, I propose to deal briefly with its history, because
it is of great interest to all those who are concerned with Burma's
future prospects, and because, without some knowledge of the past,
we cannot obtain a true perspective of the present, or estimate the
prospects of the future. Whether any rice was exported from
Burma before the annexation of Arakan and Tenasserim in 1825 is
not known for certain, but an export trade from Arakan developed
soon after the British occupation: while Moulmein became an
important centre for the export of teak and for ship-building,
Akyab became a considerable port for the export of rice, most of
it going to the Madras ports. As early as 1845-46 the exports
from Akyab were 74,000 tons; eight years later they were 86,000
tons, and the average for the seven years from 1854-55 to 1861-62
had risen to 160,000 tons. Since then the population of Arakan
has increased more than the cultivated area, which is strictly
limited, and exports now amount to about a quarter of a million
tons and account for less than 10 per cent. of the rice export trade
of Burma. It is, however, interesting to note that, for some years
after the second Burmese war and the occupation of Rangoon,
Akyab still exported more rice than Rangoon, and I have seen a
note in the official records in Akyab sometime in the sixties,

It was only after the annexation of Pegu in 1852 that the
rice trade really became important, and it received encouragement
first from the American Civil War, which temporarily put a stop
to the import of Carolina rice into England, and secondly from the
opening of the Suez Canal in 1869, which greatly facilitated export
to Europe, and by increasing the demand from the West raised the
price. The area under rice in Lower Burma was approximately one
million acres in 1855. It increased to two million acres by 1872,
and then grew even more rapidly, owing to the opening of the Suez
Canal, and reached five million acres about 1895, eight million
acres about 1910, and is now approximately ten million acres.
Meanwhile the area in Upper Burma, which was about one million
acres at the annexation in 1885, increased to two million acres by
1903, largely owing to the construction and improvement of
irrigation canals. Since then it has increased slowly, and now
varies from two to 2 million acres. Of this, approximately a
a third depends on the large irrigation canals in Kyaukse,
Mandalay, Shwebo and Minbu Districts, and the rest is either
irrigated by small irrigation works, many of which are privately
owned, or is dependent on rainfall. The rainfall in most of Upper
Burma is very capricious, and this accounts for the large variations.
from year to year in the cultivated area.
Of the large irrigation works, those in Kyaukse and the Man
and Salin Canals in Minbu are many centuries old, and show the
remarkable talent of the Burmese in this direction. The Mandalay
Canal, the Shwebo and Yeu Canals and the Mon Canal in Minbu
had all been contemplated and most of them attempted by the
Burmese, but the difficulties were too great for their limited
technical skill to overcome, and their successful construction had
to await modern engineering methods.
At the present moment therefore there are some 12 million
acres of rice land in Burma, of which about 10 million acres depend
on a remarkably steady rainfall, and nearly another million on
equally secure irrigation systems. A study of the figures of the
last ten years and a knowledge of the country leads to the conclu-
sion that there is little prospect of this area increasing to any
marked extent. The plains of Lower Burma have been pretty

The out-turn of this area is estimated at between 7 and
million tons of paddy. The out-turn per acre varies considerably
from year to year. Though Lower Burma is one of the most secure
agricultural areas in the world, the distribution of the rainfall
varies a good deal, and a shortage of a few inches of rain in
October may make a difference of several hundred thousand tons in
the weight of the crop. To take an actual case in recent years, the
difference between the matured areas in 1931-32 and 1932-33
was only 2'6 per cent, but the difference in the estimated out-turn
was as much as 17 per cent. Over a period of years, however,
there is surprisingly little variation, and we may safely assume
that Burma will in future have about 7 million tons of paddy a
year to dispose of.

children included. One and a half lbs. a day is rather less

purchasing power. In the past there has never been any difficulty
in finding a market, and it is the question of markets in the future
that I now wish to discuss.

We must now consider what countries take this rice and what
are the tendencies of Burma's trade with these countries.
In the early days of the trade, the bulk of Burma's exports
went to Europe, and it is recorded that in 1868-69, out of 500,000
tons exported 400,000 went to Europe, while out of the 250,000
tons exported from Siam and Indo-China only 80,000 tons went to
Europe.
In this connection I have included the United Kingdom with
Europe; the published statistics show them separately and the
demand from different countries is of importance to the exporter,
but the figures are not always very reliable because a good deal of
rice is shipped to "Channel ports for orders", and goes on to the
Continent of Europe. For present purposes the demand from the
United Kingdom and from the Continent of Europe may conveni-
ently be considered together. Up to the end of the 19th century
about two-thirds of Burma's exports still went to Europe, and the
first year in which India took more than half the crop was 1897,
which was due to the famine of the previous year in India. Up to
the outbreak of the Great War, however, the Europe market
remained the most important, but the war completely dislocated the
trade and it has never returned to its previous state.
For one thing, the consumption of rice in Europe decreased
with the spending power of the population, and for another thing
the rice trade has been seriously affected by the recent trade restric-
tions imposed on foreign imports in many European countries.
Rice in Europe as a whole is not a staple food, but is more or less
of a luxury. To the average English housewife it is a food of which
she buys a pound, wrapped up in an ornamental cellophane packet,
and costing 3d. or 4d., which makes it a much more expensive food
than flour or potatoes. Being a special food of this nature, a small
extra cost for the better varieties is not grudged, and the Europe
market generally wants the higher grades of rice rather than large
quantities of the lower grades. Now of recent years two European
countries, Italy and Spain, have largely increased their production.

There has, however, been strong opposition to these imports
from a section of Indian opinion which supports the doctrines of
economic nationalism, and Burma has even been accused of “dump-
ing" her surplus rice-crop on Madras. I understand dumping to
mean disposing of surplus at a price below the cost of production,
or possibly below the home price, and I have never seen any
evidence that Burma has put rice on to the Madras market in this
way. As a matter of fact, Burma does not control the price of
rice; there is for one thing far too much competition from Siam
and Indo-China for her to do that. The price of rice is a world-
price, and it fell during the depression in company with the price of
all other food-grains, and all the three exporting countries had to
dispose of their crop where they could, and for such price as they
could get. The quantity of Burma rice shipped every year to India
is the quantity demanded by India, and this depends as stated
earlier partly on the price, and partly on the local crop in India.
The very large influence of the local crop in India is shown by the
surprising variations from year to year in the amount of the Burma
crop taken by different parts of India. For instance, in 1933-34

on account of the Behar earthquake. In 1935-36 these ports took
only 267,000 tons. Madras ports in 1933-34 took 576,000 tons, in
1934-35 649,000 tons, and in 1935-36 684,000 tons, in spite of the

1934-35 and 26 per cent higher than in 1933-34.

consumption, and it is not likely that such a small proportion could
have any appreciable effect on the price of the home-grown
article. So far from doing any harm to the cultivators of Madras
or any other part of India it would appear that a supply of Burma
rice at a price within reach of the Indian consumer is a real need
to India, and that any restriction of such supplies would merely
decrease the quantity and raise the price of food of the poorer
classes without any appreciable benefit to the local cultivators. It
is to be hoped therefore that the demand for an import duty on
rice, which might after separation be extended to Burma rice, will
not come to anything, and that India will remain a free market for
Burma rice. It that is so, there is good reason to hope that as long
as the price does not go too high the Indian demand will increase
rather than decrease, and will compensate for the decline of other
markets.
On the whole therefore it appears that in the future Burma
must look to India as the main market for her rice-crop and that
two-thirds of the exportable surplus is likely to go to that country.
In that case, Burma should endeavour to produce a crop which
will appeal to the Indian market, and should be content with produ-
cing a limited quantity of higher-grade rice suitable for the strictly
limited European market. Finally, it appears likely that a rise in
the price to that which ruled from 1920 to 1930 might reduce the
demand from India, that a steady price not exceeding Rs. 250
might in the long run be the best thing for this country. This,
however, is not a matter on which I wish to dogmatize, and if a
higher price was within the reach of the Indian consumer it would
be all the better for Burma, but we must face the fact that
over-production and inability to dispose of surplus stock has ruined
many producers of other articles in recent years, and that failure to
sell her paddy crop would be a disaster for Burma of the first
magnitude, and that it is better to continue to sell the whole crop
at a reasonable price than to get a high price for part of it, and to
be left with a large unsaleable carry-over at the end of the year.
Burma is fortunate, much more fortunate than many people realize,
in never having had to face this yet, and it is to be hoped that she
never will have to face it.
F. BLEACH.
