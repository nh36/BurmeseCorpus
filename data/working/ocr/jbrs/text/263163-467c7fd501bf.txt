The Konbyin Terrace of the Irrawaddy at Thayetmyo
In the eight miles of its course between the two nodal points determined
by the Minbyataung and Tondaung hill ranges the River Irrawaddy has
swung far from side to side at successive stages of its history, and on the
gently sloping alluvial tracts eroded and aggraded in this manner the modern
towns of Thayetmyo and Allanmyo have grown up. The present set of the
river is to the west and over a length of about five miles it is cutting away
at a solid rock bank capped by river gravels deposited during the Konbyin
stage. The town and cantonment of Thayetmyo and the neighbouring
villages of Thigon, Ledaingzin, Thedaw, and Tondaungywa extend along this
right bank Konbyin terrace. On the east the river is bordered by a wide strip
of Lebyin alluvium on which are situated Allanmyo, Ywataung, and Inbauk.
Along the right bank of the river there are many sections, easily
accessible at low water, which show the base of the Konbyin gravels resting
unconformably upon the Irrawaddian and Peguan strata, but none of these
sections exposes more than a few feet of the gravels. Inland, on the higher
ground away from the river bank, much greater thicknesses of the Konbyin
deposits still remain in places, capping the ridges between the chaungs
draining into the Irrawaddy. Extensive excavations for the foundations of
the Burma Cement Works and cuttings for a railway line to the Tondaung
Quarries have been made near Ledaingzin and Thedaw during the past
twelve months, and have provided a complete succession from the base of
the terrace to the highest Konbyin deposits still surviving in the vicinity.
Deep artificial sections of the Irrawaddy terrace deposits are rare, and even
where extensive natural ones are available, as, for example, in the deeply
incised chaungs of the Dry Zone "bad lands", more often than not they are
high up on a cliff face and difficult to examine in detail. Many of the sections
at the cement works will soon be covered up permanently, and the cuttings
along the railway will probably be grown over rapidly, so during a recent
visit to Thayetmyo I took the opportunity to measure them and to search
for fossils and prehistoric implements.
The base of the Konbyin gravels lies about 120 feet above sea level
along the river bank at Ledaingzin village. In tube wells and foundation
excavations at the cement works the base of the gravels has been found to
vary a few feet above and below this level, owing to irregularities of the old
river bed in which they were deposited. The lowest point located is about
116 feet above sea level, in the foundations for the crushing plant, about
1500 feet inland from the nearest point on the river bank.
The Konbyin gravels have a well-marked basal bed a couple of feet
thick, composed of large pebbles up to about 6 inches in diameter. Above this
basal bed the pebbles are much smaller. The gravels are strongly current-
bedded and contain many subordinate beds of coarse sand. Lateritisation
subsequent to deposition has made the deposits extremely ferruginous

A thorough search through the exposures and the excavated material
failed to yield any fossils, other than abundant well-worn fragments of fossil
wood and occasional fragments of indeterminate bones, probably derived
from the Irrawaddian Series. Several rolled implements, however, were
obtained from the basal pebble bed in the crushing plant excavation, and are
described below, and figured on plates III and IV.
Plate III, figs. 1a and 1b.-A small implement of thinly banded creamy
Drown felsite. The shape is decidedly "rostro-carinate" and recalls certain
primitive types from Southern India (1, pl. 1, fig. 1: 2, fg. 1, no. 3; pl. I,
no. 8). The ventral plane has been made by the removal of two or three
flakes; the sides by plunging flakes struck from the ventral towards the
dorsal surface. The latter is practically plane and does not appear to have
been worked. A peculiarity of the implement is the great increase in width
and thickness from the butt towards the keel and point.
Plate III, figs. 2a and 2b.-A small hand axe of dark greenish brown,
almost black, siltstone. One face has been worked completely, and has a
strong ridge down the middle. The other face is smooth, save for a few
pebble markings, and is probably the original surface of the pebble from
which the tool was made. Implements of this type are abundant in Southern
India (cf. 1, pls. I-IV; 2, pls. I-IV).
Plate III, figs. 3a and 3b.-A long narrow implement of creamy brown
felsite. The implement has four definite faces and is subrectangular in cross
section. The whole of the butt and three of the faces are flaked. One face
(figured in 3b) appears to have been part of the surface of the original
pebble; it is much more deeply patinated than the remainder of the
implement and shows only pebble markings. No precise equivalent of this
type has been illustrated from Suthern India, unless reference may be made
to Cammiade and Burkitt's plate II, fig. 2; but I have found similar forms in
the N. W. Punjab.

Plate IV, figure 1 is of light yellowish-green felsite, full of fine siliceous

Plate JV, figure 2 is of creamy brown felsite, more strongly veined than

Plate IV, figure 3 is of brownish-grey hard siltstone. It resembles the

section away from its rounded butt and chisel-shaped point.
The affinities of these Thayetmyo implements are undoubtedly with the
Pre-Chellean or Early Chellean cultures of India, Africa, and Europe. They
must have been manufactured before the deposition of the Konbyin gravels;
thus they provide a definite anterior limit for the dating of that stage in the
evolution of the Irrawaddy valley.
Evidence has already been obtained that the river had left the
Konbyinmyint and that the deposits of that terrace had been considerably
denuded before the advent of Chellean man in Burma (3, 5). The Thayetmyo
Konbyin implements demonstrate conclusively that Konbyin erosion was
completed and aggradation had commenced at some period later than the
Pre-Chellean or Early Chellean. In late Paleolithic time a further cycle was
well under way, for sufficient specimens of the small flake culture have now
been found on the surface of the Konbyin near Thayetmyo and elsewhere
(cf. 4, p. 4) to confirm the supposition that the alluvial plain of the Konbyin
stage was by that time available for occupation by man.
