1. Childhood joys and efforts to recapture them
A detailed presentation of the subjects
falling within the scope of our study will
run to volumes. This must be left to other
hands. We are at present concerned to
give a swift and perceptive eagle's eye view
of the matter in hand taken in a single
sweep. Things are viewed in the mass as
well as in the round. But the salient
features are not allowed to be obscured by
the wealth of facts.
Since the dawn of history, the joy of
life has ever struggled for expression in the
human breast. True, a child announces
his entry into the world with a cry. It is
equally true that the stirrings of his gaiety
of spirit glimmer when he smiles in his
sleep and crows for sheer joy. As he grows
up he catches fitful and fleeting glimpses of
the beauty of the world and is then flushed
with the tingling joy of life. He feels the
sweet breath of the morning and the charm
of earliest birds and revels in the rising sun
when first on this delightful land he spreads
his orient beams, on herb, tree, fruit and
flower glist'-ring with dew. Indeed he may
even see heaven in a wild flower.²
Poets to whom the essence of things is
unfolded in a lightning flash speak of
heaven that lay about us in our infancy,³
of the happy early days when we shone in
‘angel infancy', and of the widening dis-
tance from heaven as we grow older.5 We
then find ourselves incessantly tossed about
between joy and sorrow, laughter and tears,
good and evil.
In moments of depression we have
nostalgic longings for the exhilarating ex-
perience of childhood with its fun and
laughter. And we may even try to re-
capture our rosy visions of youth when the
world was supremely beautiful to us. Such
rapturous moments cannot be ours unless
we forget ourselves and escape the stress
and strain of existence.. One who has gained
a glimpse of the entrancing radiance at the
heart of our dark world has described his
luminous insight.
I saw eternity the other night,
Like a great ring of pure and
endless light,
All calm as it was bright;

2: Song, Dance and Music
The joys of childhood and youth find
spontaneous musical expression particularly
in rural areas where there is a shared body
of belief and custom. The popular mani-
festation of the art of music is in song and
dance. Speaking broadly music assumes
two dimensions-folk music and theatrical
music. The border-line can scarcely be
drawn precisely. But it is real. The former
is more or less private, and the latter is
essentially public. One exists mainly in
spontaneous performance with no musical
training. And the other consists of trained
performance matched by a rhythmic combi-
nation of tones embracing harmony and
melody. This demands consummate skill
which can be acquired only by. intensive
and prolonged training.
Country folks have throughout the
ages externalized their emotions in song
and dance. In the home the mother puts
her baby to sleep with a cradle song. Even
children learn to sing nursery songs and
make amateurish attempts at dancing in
pure merriment. In seed-time and at harvest
knots of young folks break the tedium of
their labours by song and dance. Both in
the house of mirth and in the house of
mourning local musical talent is afforded an
opportunity to display its tonal skill. Vocal
music is accompanied by instrumental
music. The musical instruments usually
played on such occasions are: the elongated
drum (2:0) with one end kept open like

3: Burmese Songs and the lyric note
When people were unlettered, songs
survived only in their mouths and in their
memories. But when speech was converted
into writing literacy began to spread, and
songs came to be written. The Burmese
people became skilled in the art of writing
only during the days of Anawrata of Pagan
(1044-77 A.D.) So we have no record of
Burmese songs prior to that time.
At first scholarship was the by-product
of religion and learning was the monopoly
of the clergy. Indeed monasteries were little
oases of intellect in the midst of the general
mental Sahara. In process of time kings
became patrons of learned monks who
began to shed their influence in Court. Our
earliest men of letters were therefore either
monks or courtiers who had received
instruction from monks. The cloister was
in truth and in fact the cradle of our first
litterateurs. Burmese Literature has there-
fore been suffused with religious sentiments.
A multitude of Burmese songs are out-
bursts of exultant joy or poignant grief.
They range from hymns to nats (ε:),
and classical songs(☐☐:) to sonnets(600:006)
and lover's laments (605). The songs

4: Principal Burmese Dances
Between song and dance the nexus is
intimate, yea unbreakable. There is no
form of Burmese dance without music,
vocal or instrumental or both. The simplest
form of individual Burmese dance is a-nyein
(). It is a kind of concert in which a
nymphet who is a prospective starlet per-
forms by means of lissome and willowy
movements of the body and the limbs and
sings to the accompaniment of chamber
music of a sort.
The dancing may assume a more ele-
borate form. The body is then thrown into
different graceful poses with facility by the
supple twisting of the hands, fingers and
elbows. Even the legs may be doubled up
or stretched to suit the action to the words
of the song.
There is also the posture dance or yein
(:) performed by a group of young men
or young women. It resembles in some
respects the Western ballet. But in Western
ballet the emphasis is so nicely balanced
between posture and music that the feeling
and the attitude of the performers are
brought home to us by their poses, facial
expressions and bodily movements.
A distinguishing peculiarity may be
pointed out in the Burmese dance. We do
not see the sexes mix in dancing, much less
whirl in close embrace as in the West. In
fact only on a single occasion the leading
actor and actress (hyperbolically styled
Prince and Princess respectively) are seen to
touch each other's hands. This takes place
when they dance a duet (l::) and plight
their troth (s) towards the end of the
play.10

5: The influence of the Jataka Tales
The Burman's religious ardour is made
abundantly clear in the wealth of figurines,
paintings, carvings, sculptures, and other
works of art that adorn the sacred edifices
of Pagan. One and all owe their inspiration
to the Jatakas which are the stories of the
former lives of Buddha. The major stories
which are ten in number are zats in the
strict acceptation of the term. The minor
ones which are popularly believed to be 550
in number are known as nibats in contra-
distinction to the major tales or zats.
loosely speaking both sets are known as
zat-nibats.
But
Now zat-nibats form part of the
Buddhist lore of which the monks were the
depositories. At the monasteries religious
instruction was imparted to the devotees
who repeated from memory what they had
learnt to the masses. The appetite of the
people for religious tales was thus whetted
and the ground was prepared for the rise
of the drama.
The periphery of the drama had been
reached. Song and dance had been popula-
rized. The Jataka tales afforded generous
material for the satisfaction of our innate
love of story-spinning. Added to thern was
our love of mimicry. All these factors
went to make the dramatist's essential tools.
One thing however was yet lacking - the
power to fuse them together into a moving
dramatic whole by means of the magic of
words and imagination.

perforce to wait till there appeared among
the laity men with a gift of verbal mastery
and imaginative ardours.

8: The wind of change from Siam
The wind of change in regard to
both the matter and the manner of drama-
tic performance came from Siam, now
Thailand. In the irony of things Siam
whose relations with Burma were punc-
tuated by recurring wars exercised a
paramount influence on the growth of
the Burmese Drama. Burma attacked
Ayuthia, the then Siamese capital no
less than six times. Yet the aftermath
of each war was a cultural gain to Burma
to a greater or less degree.
Siamese cultural influence flowed into
Burma not with a sweeping movement,
but by slow penetrating processes through a
time-span of several centuries. Hence
the need to telescope the centuries for
the purpose of illuminating in proper
perspective the sequence of events (pro-
ximate and remote) that have some bear-
ing on the cultural impingement of Siam
upon Burma.
We can glimpse the first premonitory
symptoms of the cordial relations between
Burma and Siam in the despatch
of
envoys with rich gifts by the Siamese
King to Tabinshweti, the founder of the
Toungoo dynasty (reigned 1531-50 A.D)
following the latter's unsuccessful assault
on Ayuthia in 1548 A. D. The envoys
craved for the return of the Siamese
Princes captured in the field of battle.
What was more, the envoys suggested
the establishment of friendly relations
between the two countries. Tabinshweti
accepted cheerfully the suggestion; more
he released not only the Siamese Prin-
ces but the other prisoners of war in-
cluding actors and actresses.16 The way
was thus prepared for the penetration of
the Siamese cultural influence into Burma.
Inspite of the rapprochement between
Siam and Burma, Tabinshweti's successor
Bayinnaung (reigned 1551-81 A.D) adopted
an unfriendly attitude towards the Siamese
King who happened to possess more
white elephants than he. The Burmese
King demanded that one of the Siamese
King's white elephants should be given
him as a present. The answer was na-
turally a blank refusal. It may be explained
that to the Buddhists of both Burma and
Siam, a white elephant is regarded as a
sacred animal.
Bayinnaung then launched an attack
on Ayuthia which fell in 1564 A.D. The
victors scized an immense booty and
carried away captive the Siamese King,
his Queens and Princes as well as several
Siamese artistes.17
It is from these Siamese artistes that

13: Festal Occasions: General Characteristics
One of the paradoxes of the Burmese
character springs to notice. The Burman
is a full-hearted upholder of his religion.
which affirms tirelessly the impermanence,
the suffering and the unreality that inhere
in life. And his daily devotions end
with a prayer for the attainment of
Nibbana, the ultimate release from the
weary round of existences. Yet he is not
tortured by anxious thought. Nor is he
troubled by the cares of this life. What
is more, he seizes every opportunity to
give vent to his inherent love of mirth
and fun. He seeks to effect a synthesis
between these two attitudes which stand
over against each other in sheer polarity

"The Tabaung sky looks dismal and is
dappled with pearly clouds. Petguni and
the egret shine by the side of the silvery

"Rivers and rills seem glorious with
silvery sands. The tharapi (Ochrocarpus
siamensis. Ed.) buds and sprouts and sheds
its sweetness. The early fall of rain forms
torrents that sweep sere leaves into larger
streams. The touching mingle of mist and
gloom fill even the spirits of saints with
nameless longings. In this self-same season,
the Sage journeyed to Kapilawut-the seat of
the King. Sheldrakes, thaungdins, kingfishers,
terns, drongoes, saruses, minas, parrots,
cuckoos, storks, imperial pigeons, bilons, .
