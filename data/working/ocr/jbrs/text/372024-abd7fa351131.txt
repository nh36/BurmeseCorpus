The holy Koran says that everything in nature has its mate for the
work of procreation: everything is either male or female. The
Mohammedan religion encourages marriage and the raising of offspring,
because the control of the sexual instinct is as essential as the control
of the stomach, and when beyond control, sexual passions know no
bounds. The procreation of species is the primary object of the sexual
instinct. In the interests of the offspring, marriage should take place
before lovers give rein to passion; and the fact that they are married
should be known. For that reason the Mohammedan religion insists
on marriage, and disallows all concealed marriages. Prostitution, tem-
porary marriage, concubinage and illicit intercourse are condemned.
A man is prohibited from certain unions, which are regarded as
incestuous. He may not marry his mother, daughter, sister, aunt:
paternal or maternal, brother's or sister's daughter, a woman who has
suckled him, foster-sister, mother-in-law, daughter-in-law, or step-
daughter under his guardianship. He may not marry his wife's sister
unless his wife is dead or divorced. He may not marry a woman who
is undergoing iddat of her previous marriage. Iddat means the period
a wife must wait for the dissolution of her marriage before she can
marry again the period is usually 3 months after divorce. Religion
also forbids a victor in war to share his bed with a female captive,
unless she is first set free and agrees to the marriage: in which case
she must be married according to the custom of the conqueror. A
Mohammedan man is also forbidden to marry an idolatress or fire-
worshipper, unless she is converted to Mohammedanism.
Misled by selfish interest, some have argued the Mohammedan
religion allows plurality of wives. It does so only in exceptional cir-
cumstances :-"If thou fear that thy consort is not a fit companion for
thy happiness, thou mayest marry of thy choice, provided thou art
able to deal justly among them. But if thou canst not deal justly
among them, marry but one”. (Ch. IV, Sec. 1, V. 3).
In ancient times polygyny was a practice wellnigh universal among
Egyptians, Greeks, Aryans and Hebrews. There was no religion which
absolutely forbade it. The Code of Hammurabi recognized cases where
a second wife could be taken. The Greeks allowed polygyny :
Alexander the Great had more than one wife. The Aryans practised
polygyny and also polyandry: e.g. The five Pandava brothers had a
common wife, Draupadi. The Hebrews never objected to plurality of

Under Mohammedan law marriage is a purely civil contract, to
legalize sexual intercourse and the procreation of children. Like any
other contract, it is constituted by proposal and acceptance, and can
be terminated by mutual consent. It is accompanied by payment of
Mahr (dowry) which may be regarded as the price paid to the wife
for the connubial rights of the husband. Like any other contract, it
can be contracted through an agent. For valid marriage, the parties
should either have the capacity to contract marriage, or, if minors, it
should be contracted through their guardian or guardians. There must
be no impediment to the marriage: that is, the parties must not be on
the prohibited list of relations, and the woman must not be already
married to another. (Mohammedan law forbids polyandry).
Some impediments do not absolutely forbid marriage, but render
it invalid. Marriage of a man who already has four wives is invalid.
Absence of proper witnesses makes the marriage invalid. A Moham-
medan woman cannot contract a valid marriage with a Non-Moham-
medan unless the latter is converted to Mohommedanism. A Moham-
medan man cannot, as stated above, contract a valid marriage with an
idolatress or a fire worshipper unless the latter is converted to
Mohammedanism, but he can contract a valid marriage with a Christian
woman or a Jewess. He is forbidden to have at the same time two
wives related to each other by consanguinity, affinity or fosterage. The
reason of the bar is to avoid confusion of kindred. However, if a man
contracts a marriage involving such unlawful conjuction the marriage.
only is invalid offspring born of the marriage are legitimate. The
impediment can be removed by divorcing the wife who stands in the
prohibited degree of relationship to the new wife. Finally, as stated
above, he may not marry a woman undergoing iddat of her previous
marriage.
If the impediment is absolute and permanent, the marriage is void,
and does not create any civil rights or obligations between the parties,
and the offspring of such a union is illegitimate. If the impediment is
only relative, temporary or accidental, the offspring is legitimate.
Marriage imposes on the husband the duty of paying a dowry to
the wife, of maintaining her, and of providing her with a suitable resi-
dence. It imposes on the wife the duty of being faithful and obedient
to the husband.

II Preliminaries to Marriage
Marriage can be brought about in many ways:-The parents may
arrange it. A go-between may arrange it. The man or the woman
may choose his or her mate, either with the parents' consent or by
elopement. The first way, betrothal by parents, is quite simple. The
parents on one side propose if those on the other side accept, the
betrothal ceremony is performed. Similarly with the second way if
the arrangements of the go-between are accepted by the parents or
guardians on both sides, the betrothal ceremony takes place. If the
man and the woman fall in love and a romance develops, the man
asks his parents to make a proposal to the woman's parents, and if
they give their consent, the betrothal is celebrated. Sometimes consent
is refused on one side or the other on the ground of social inequality,
or because the man or the woman is intended for another, or because
they are of a different religion. When consent is refused, the romance
is either broken off, or the lovers try to force the parents to agree by
cloping, or else they go and contract marriage in the presence of a
magistrate under the Special Marriages Act, the marriage being made
complete by the expression of mutual consent in the presence of wit-
nesses. If after the elopement, the parents still refuse their consent, the
lovers may live together, have children and grow old without their
union developing into marriage but such cases are very rare, and are
condemned by the religion and the community.
The scripture recommends that the man and the woman should
see each other before the time of betrothal, so that they may not repent
after marriage. At the betrothal some relatives and close friends are
invited and food is served. It is known as the "Salamati” ceremony.
At this ceremony the man's parents or guardians propose to her on
behalf of the man. The ceremony takes place at the woman's house,
but she stays in her own room. Arrangements are made for payment
of the dowry. The right of the wife to her dowry is fundamental.
The amount is not fixed, but must not be less than Rs. 3/- or Rs. 4/-
(See M. D. Manek, Mohammedan Law, Chapter IV), payable either at
consummation of marriage or afterwards. One of the parties-usually
the man's parents-undertakes to bear the expenses of the marriage
ceremony. Sometimes engagement rings are exchanged. This ends
the betrothal ceremony.
Some Burmese Mohammedans copy the Burmese custom of fixing
the marriage day after casting the horoscopes of the two persons. After
elaborate calculation the astrologers find and fix the propitious day.
But Mohammedans never marry during the Mohammedan Lent, which
is a period of one month in each year.
After betrothal the young man visits the girl in her own house.
Usually at first some of her relations are present, but after repeated
visits they gradually drop off till finally he is allowed to see the girl
alone. Burmese Mohammedan couples are free and more independent
than those in India: for the girls here have escaped the 'purdah' of
Indian Mohammedans, and go about unrestricted, visiting bazaar or
cinema unattended. But the men are recognized as the "superiors";
and under the superficial pattern of women's freedom and independence,

she marries between the ages of 16 and 30 a man of about the same

At a Burmese Mohammedan marriage also, after the bridegroom has
taken his sip, the child will refuse to take the drink to the bride until
the bridegroom gives him or her some money. Again the bridegroom.

the first week of his married life there.
IV. The Married Couple
In the Burmese Mohammedan community there is no joint family
system. In theory the married couple exercise their new social and
economic privileges and set up house. In practice, however, this may
not be possible. In India, under Mohammedan law, when no new house
is available, the wife follows the husband to his father's house, but she
does not become a member of his family. If the husband is earning
he will contribute towards their maintenance. In Burma, however, it
is quite common for the husband to go on living with his wife's family
after the first week, until he can set up house himself. He does not
become a member of his wife's family.
Even in Indian Mohammedan households the wife retains certain
rights. In Burma she is free, but is expected to be dutiful and obe-
dient to her husband. The following quotations from the Koran will
illustrate her status and her duties :-

But the Mohammedan religion sets the highest value on the chas-

scripture allows stern measures in the case of (a) the wife rebelling

"the wife hating her husband". Three remedies are suggested. If
it is mere resistance to the husband's authority, simple admonition is
recommended. If resistance is combined with hatred, stronger re-
medies are suggested. He may keep her separate from himself, to
show his disapproval of her conduct. If she goes further and deserts
her husband, he can beat her and bring her back to her senses and
her home.
The scripture also lays on the husband the duty of maintaining
and loving his children. He must look after their welfare and teach
them the rules of good conduct. The children, in turn, must respect
and obey him.
The family, as everywhere in Asia, is a social unit. In theory the
husband is the lord of the household; but in spite of the popular
opinion that in Mohammedan society he is a civil tyrant, there is in
practice plenty of 'give and take' between husband and wife. Even
in India there is an atmosphere of harmony in a Mohammedan family.
In Burma, with the typical freedom of the Burmese woman, the wife
shares with the husband authority in the family.

to reconcile the parties is not required by law, but the relations on both
sides try their best to do so. When it is the wife who desires divorce, she
will naturally approach the elders of her community, who, inturn, will
attempt reconciliation. When it is the man, he can divorce the woman
of his own accord, without any such attempt.
Divorce is discharged by special rules regarding the dowry. If the
husband wants the divorce, he must complete payment of the dowry
to the wife at once. If the wife wants it, she does not return the
dowry; but if it remains partly unpaid, she has to forgo the unpaid
portion. If the desire for divorce is mutual, they may settle among
themselves the question of the dowry.
The Arabic word for divorce is 'Talak', which means "The knot
is undone". As marriage is only an agreement to live together, divorce
is only the end of that agreement. The husband has only to say the
werd "talak" to his wife: and that constitutes the act of divorce, even
if spoken in jest or while drunk. But if a condition is attached, it does
not amount to divorce: e.g., if a husband says to the wife “Talak, if
you go to the cinema this evening".

'Iddat' is the period of continence imposed on the wife on the
termination of her marriage, the basic idea behind it being to make
certain of the paternity of the child born after divorce. Therefore when
there has been no cohabitation, Iddat is not necessary.
Nor is it neces-
sary if the husband has been abroad for some time when "Talak' is
pronounced. When there has been cohabitation, the period of Iddat
lasts for three full menstrual periods. Therefore no 'Talak' can be
pronounced during the wife's period of menstruation. If the woman
is not yet pregnant, and does not menstruate from some physical defect,
three calendar months are the period of Iddat-or rather three lunar
months, for the Mohammedan month is lunar. If the wife is pregnant,
the Iddat period lasts until the child is born, no matter whether the
interval between the divorce and the child-birth is more or less than
three lunar months.
Throughout the period of Iddat the wife is entitled to maintenance
and lodging in her husband's house. In some schools of Mohammedan
law she is entitled to lodging, but not to maintenance during this period
unless the divorce remains revocable. In other schools she is entitled
to maintenance throughout, whether the divorce is revocable or not.
This is the case in Burma, where, until the period of Iddat ends, the
wife retains all her rights except that of cohabitation. Though that
is the law, however, in actual practice she at once leaves her husband's
house when "Talak' is pronounced; but the divorce remains incom-
plete until the Iddat period expires.
When divorce is revocable, the husband can always revoke his
Talak by cohabiting with his wife during the period of Iddat. When
the Talak is not revocable, the husband cannot cohabit with her during
this period. In any case when the period is complete, divorce is com-
plete. And the husband cannot marry her again until she has married
and divorced another husband. This prohibition is intended to make
the husband think twice before rashly divorcing his wife.
Recently a "Burmese Muslim Marriage Bill", sponsored by the
Burmese Muslim Congress, was moved in the Burma Parliament. The
Hon'ble Minister for Judicial Affairs, U Khin Maung Lat, said that
though in Islamic faith both sexes were allowed equal rights, in actual
practice, in the matter of divorce, the treatment was one-sided. If a
Mohammedan man wished to divorce his wife, he had only to declare
thrice that she was no longer his wife. But the Mohammedan woman,
however ill-treated she might be by her husband, was not at liberty to
divorce him. In response to the demands of the Mohammedan women-
folk this Bill was introduced, with the object of protecting them from
hardships and helping them to the enjoyment of full Islamic rights.
The Bill laid down the conditions under which women married accord-
ing to Islamic rites, could sue for divorce, and specified procedure in
such cases. The Bill occasioned some controversy, but was duly passed
in 1952.

Burma has never been conquered by Mohammedan invaders, as
India and Indonesia have been. Mohammedan missionary zeal has
never affected the country. But since the early days of Islam some
Mohammedans appear to have settled in Burma. The number was
always few. The first to reach Burma were the Arab mariners sailing
their trading-ships between Arabia and the Far East. The first
Mohammedans mentioned in Burmese history were Byat-wi and Byat-ta,
the two hero-brothers, who helped to defend Thaton on the eve of its
destruction by Anawrahta in 1055 A.D. They reached Thaton origi-
nally as two children tied to a raft, set afloat from a sinking Arab ship.
After the fall of Thaton the younger brother Byat-ta took service under
Anawrahta, and his two sons became famous heroes.
In the 16th century a small company of Indian Mohammedan
gunners played a leading part in the wars of Bayinnaung. They
married Burmese women and settled down here.
Towards the close of the Burmese kingdom in the 19th century,
some merchant princes settled down in Ava under the King's pat-
ronage, and marrying Burmese women, were responsible for the rise
of a Burmese Mohammedan Community at the capital. Some of
these rose to the rank of nobles and took part in the administration
of the country. In spite of their religion, they were considered
Burmans in nationality.
When the British annexed the whole of Burma in 1886 and Burma
was declared part of the Indian Empire, the British Government at-
tempted to turn the Burmese Mohammedan community into an Indian
community. The policy of the British Government was to create a
minority problem for the Burmese. Ordinary Indians were never per-
manent settlers in the country, and therefore could not really become
a serious minority problem. Burmese Mohammedans, on the other
hand, have been permanent settlers in the country for many genera-
tions. The British introduced the official term 'Zarabadi' or 'half-
Indian', which the community resented as insulting. After the first
world-war, when the Burmese started their movement for national
freedom, the Burmese Mohammedans sympathized with them, and
started a movement of their own for official recognition as "Burmese
Musiims". In 1936 the British Government was prevailed upon to
recognize this name. In 1937, when Burma became separated from
