THE TAUNGTHAS OF TILIN TOWNSHIP
INTRODUCTORY.
In one of the remote corners of Pakokku District, still preserving many
of their old customs and speaking a distinct dialect, are a people of whom
little is known by the outside world. The origin of the Taungthas of Tilin
Township is obscure and there is no historical information about their
ancestral home. Tradition however assigns them to Popa' whence they are
said to have migrated first to Pauk and then to Tilin². But even in Tilin they
do not seem to have been able
to have been able to found a permanent home and
there is evidence that they were driven further west into the Hills.
There are several tribes in the present Chin Hills District - such as the
Welaungs, Kwenams and Shivalarengs in the Gawng Basin, the Kranams and
the Baungshes of Ridwi village and the Leisis on the Mon Chaung -- who
are believed to be akin to the Taungthas and whose dialects are said to be
similar to that of the latter. Later on, however, some of the Taungthas seem
to have either left the Hills as they found life too hard or were driven back
to the plains by the Chins, and it is probable that the present Taungtha
villages in Tilin Township were established (or re-established) by them. This
movement between the plains and the Hills and back seems to have been a
continual process — - Taungthas from the plains leaving their homes either
en masse or in driblets following local disputes, and Taungthas already settled
in the Hills returning to the plains and founding new colonies or joining exist-
ing ones and we have evidence that two of the Chin tribes referred to above
are descendants of Taungthas who migrated to the Chin Hills in compara-
tively recent years. Thus, about six generations ago, nineteen households
from Wabin village, Tilin Township migrated into the Chin Hills and es-
tablished the villages of Kranam and Kwenam in the present Kanpetlet Sub-
division of the Chin Hills District. Forty households of this stock are now
claimed to exist, and they still speak a dialect which is said to be akin to the
Taungtha dialect, and they still do not marry outsiders. Twenty seven years
ago, some of the Kranams and Kwenams (as they call themselves) migrated
eastward and founded the village of Ridwi, twenty miles due west of Tilin.
There are now fifteen households of the same stock in this village.

The above view of the frequent oscillation of the Taungthas between the

they were strong enough to withstand the latter's opposition accounts for the
many affinities and the differences that exist between them and the Chins. It
also explains why the Yaw Burmans still regard the Taungthas as being more
akin to the Chins than to themselves, and offers the only rational explanation
of the word Taungtha. a Burmese word, the Taungtha word for it being
Roungtoo.

That the Taungthas are a race distinct from both the Burmans and the
Chins there is very little doubt; and though on account of its similarity with
Chinbok their dialect has in successive Census Reports been included in the
Kuki-Chin group the fact that they are a distinct race has always been re-
cognized. In Spinning Magic also Mr. (now Dr.) J. A. Stewart after dis-
cussing several customs and beliefs common to both pointed out certain vital
differences. One of them may be quoted here. Thus, while there does not
seem to be any trace of totemism in the Chin Hills, the Taungthas held
definitely totemistic beliefs and each of the castes into which the latter at
one time seems to have been divided worshipped a nat (rè) of its own.
PHYSICAL CHARACTERISTICS AND OCCUPATION.
There have been one or two attempts at describing the physical character-
istics of the Taungthas. Thus, at p. 29 of the Burma Gazetteer, Pakokku
District, Vol. A. it is stated that "their features are more Aryan than Mon-
golian in type." I do not know what justification there was for this des-
cription at the time it was written (1913), but it is now impossible to
distinguish the Taungthas from their Burman neighbours except when they
speak their dialect or wear their distinctive dress. Neither is it possible to-day
to distinguish between the occupational capabilities of the Taungthas and of
the Yaw Burmans, and they both follow the same pursuits. * The Taungtha
villager to-day is almost completely Burmanized in this respect hard work-
ing and industrious in the field, gay, easy-going and pleasure-loving outside.

CASTES.
-
There is evidence that the Taungthas were divided into casts - called
tôns according to their social position, wealth and order of settlement.
There are said to have been five tons originally, as follows: - (1) Khaung-
le tôn; (2) Ka tôn; (3) Pahnu tôn; (4) Sin-ngaw tôn and (5) Wayat tôn.
The Ka tôn is said to have consisted of warriors and fighting men while
the Khaungle tôn consisted of the prosperous and well-established in-
habitants of the village. No information is available as to what differ-
entiated the Pahnu and Sin-ngaw tôns from each other and the others.
Wayat tôn consisted of slaves and the lowest classes. All these five tôns
are claimed to be still in existence in Thanbaya village on the western
border of Tilin Township, while only three-Pahnu, Sin-ngaw and Wayat
are said to exist in Wetthet.¹
There were no restrictions on social dealings between members of
different tons and inter-marriage was freely practised, the wife usually
adopting the tôn of her husband. The only restriction that ever existed
seems to have been in respect of funerals, members of one tôn not partici-
pating in the funeral of a member of another tôn. At one time each tôn had
its own cemetery, but now that there is only one common cemetery the above
restriction also has disappeared. There is at present no awareness among the
Taungthas of a division into different classes.

words and phrases which are similar to Chin, but it has never been studied
and we have no exact knowledge of its origin or ethnology." There is no
written literature whatever - some of the Taungthas of course claim that
they had one at one time and that it had been destroyed by the Burmans, but

The Taungtha woman still wears a distinctive dress of her own. It con-
sists of a white htamein worn in the same fashion as the ancient Burmese
htamein, and a coloured wrapper over the breasts. The pattern on the latter
shows whether a woman is married or single and also indicates the age group
to which she belongs. When a Taungtha woman now wishes to appear res-
pectable, she dons an ordinary Burmese jacket over her white htamein. There
was, it is claimed, a distinctive Taungtha jacket at one time, but no trace
of it can be found now. The men wear ordinary Burmese clothes.
-
The contrast between the sombre white of the htamein and the dull pat-
tern of the wrapper and the usually gay colours of the Burmese dress is
striking, but the Taungtha dress - particularly in groups - has a quiet
dignity of its own.

Burmese practice in some of their public manifestations of sex freedom. Un-
til recent years, there used to be a festival during the month of Tagu which
lasted for several days and during which much sexual license was permitted.
Thus, on the 2nd lasan of the month, it was a custom for all unmarried girls
to treat the bachelors of the village to toddy, and as each cup was offered to
the man the girl embraced him and held the cup to his lips. This was re-
peated on the 8th lasan, when after the drinks were over the young men and
women were paired off and there was no ban to their going to any lengths
they liked. There was also a festival the culmination of which was a dance
executed by a villager dressed as the mythical toe, and during this festival
too bachelors and maidens would divide themselves into couples after the
dance and were permitted to go to any lengths they pleased. Yet another
occasion when the utmost sexual freedom was tolerated was at a funeral,
when the bachelors and maidens would gather at the funeral house to pound
rice used for making cakes to feed the guests. After the rice pounding was
over, the company would be divided into pairs by the leader and there was
no ban to their sleeping together. The only restriction that the more so-
phisticated section of the villagers claim to have existed is no restriction at
all, for it only referred to the consequences of such sexual license. Thus,
if a girl got into difficulties the man responsible had to pay her a money com-
pensation. Another custom of sending once a year, in the month of Tagu,
all the young men and women of each village into the jungle, who on return
next morning are met by the rest of the village with beating of gongs and
drums and each couple is considered duly married - is mentioned in the
Pakokku District Gazetteer, but elders in Wetthet deny all knowledge of its
ever having existed.

-
The Tagu festival, toe dance and pounding of rice at funerals are still in
existence, but the sexual freedom which accompanied them has been rigorous-
ly curtailed and now nothing further than the men and women gathering
together and a few innocent pranks are said to occur. But many men in
their middle ages in Wetthet testify to the existence of these liberties as re-
cently as a generation ago.
One manifestation of this sex freedom still lingers on, and it has no
counter-part in modern Burmese Society, urban or rural. If a young Taung-
tha man is in love with a Taungtha girl but cannot marry her because of
financial considerations, the parents of the girl allow the lovers to sleep
together and enjoy all the privileges of a married life until such time as the
man is able to earn money, when he is expected to marry the girl formally.
This intimacy before formal marriage is not placed on the same plane as that
of a marital relationship, and no ceremonies accompany it nor is there any

In their marriage customs also the Taungthas present certain features
quite distinct from Burmans. At the engagement - which takes place on an
auspicious day determined by the astrologers _ the bride-groom's party
brings a present of money and three pots of khaung-ye. The ceremony is
completed by the groom's relations tying several strands of unspun thread
round the wrist of the bride, and if afterwards the boy's party breaks the
engagement they are liable to pay a money compensation to the girl's people.
As among Burmans, there does not seem to be any definite marriage
ceremony. But the bride is usually taken away to the bride-groom's house
after the marriage, and the bride's father gives her a full complement of
wearing apparel and such other articles of household utility as he can afford.
If he is a man of means, he also makes her a gift of such property as he
wishes her to inherit and according to custom this gift acts as an estoppel to
the girl's making any further claims on her parents' properties. This of
course is quite different from Burmese Buddhist law, and the question as to
whether the latter would prevail against their own customary law does not
seem to have been settled.
Though the Taungthas have increased from 7,570 in 1921 to 11,187 in
1931, they are fast losing their identity into the larger and more important
Burmese community. Those in remote villages still however preserve much
of their original simplicity and naivete, and as long as they speak a distinct
dialect of their own they will continue to remain an object of interest. But
the next generation or two may well see the complete disappearance of both
their language and dress, and an attempt should be made to keep a record of
these two features of one of the most charming and unsophisticated com-
munities of Burma.
