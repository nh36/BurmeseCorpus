COPPER AND BRONZE ANTIQUITIES FROM BURMA
In comparison with the number of Stone Age artefacts which have
now been discovered in Burma the prehistoric antiquities which can be
assigned to a Copper or Bronze Age are extremely rare. Hundreds of
stone implements, ranging from the most primitive chipped cores to the
beautifully ground and polished products of the late Neolithic, have
been collected and studied, but only fourteen copper or bronze objects
are known to have been found within the geographical limits of Burma,
and most of these are from the Shan States. The reasons for this
relative scarcity of material belonging to the early stages of the Age of
Metals are, in the first place, the lack of organised research; secondly,
the rapidity with which small copper, bronze, and iron objects oxidise
and disappear when exposed to the weathering conditions of a monsoon
climate; and, thirdly, the fact that the magical virtues of fragments of a
"kyi-mogyo" are esteemed much more highly by the present day
inhabitants of the country than even those of a well finished "kyauk-
'mogyc."
I have only been able to trace fourteen Copper or Bronze Age
antiquities of Burma origin. Seven of these are now in the Department
of Oriental Antiquities at the British Museum; two in the Pitt Rivers
Museum, Oxford; one in the Museum of Far Eastern Antiquities,
Stockholm; two in the collection of Mr. A. A. Cameron of Kalaw; and
two fragments I encountered some years ago in the possession of
villagers in the Thayetmyo and Lower Chindwin Districts. I have
figured all these specimens on the accompanying plate, and have added
to them a socketed bronze celt from a little way over the Yunnan
border, collected on the 1868 expedition from British Burma via Bhamo,
which is now preserved in the Indian Museum, Calcutta.
Eleven of the fifteen objects figured are socketed celts. These and
the spear head are of similar design to types characteristic of late
Bronze Age and early Iron Age cultures over a large portion of Europe
and Asia. The socketed celt, "zone fossil" of the 13th-10th centuries
B.C. in Central and Eastern Europe, is now known to have been present
in China at least as early as the 12th or 11th century B.C. (Seligman,
p. 86). Golóubew (p. 31) has described how, in the cemetery of Dong-
son, Annam, an advanced Bronze Age culture has associated with it
primitive chipped stone hand axes, late Neolithic shouldered celts, and
knives with iron blades set in bronze hafts. He considers that the
indigenous people of the region, probably Indonesians, acquired the art
of metal working from their Mongolian conquerors during the south-

Professor Luce has suggested recently (p. 239) that conditions in

the definite information which can only be provided by systematic
excavations it is impossible to judge to what extent we are really
justified in assuming, on the evidence of the few scattered finds
illustrated in this paper, that a fully developed Copper or Bronze Age
intervened between the Neolithic and Iron Ages in this part of the
world. Precise dating of the material is also out of the question for the
time being, beyond the fact that the existence of an advanced Iron
Age culture at Hmawza in the 5th century A.D. provides a definite
terminus ad quem. The cultural affinities of this ancient Pyu civilisa-
tion of Hmawza, however, were with Southern India, not with China.
or with Indo-China. And we do not know at what particular date the
wave of Indian overseas expansion first reached Lower Burma.
I think it very probable that when the story is known more fully
the interactions of the late stone and early metal-working cultures in
Burma will prove to have been very complex, and may have been in
progress in Upper Burma long before the sea routes from Southern
India to Lower Burma were developed. For example, there is a copper
celt from Midnapur, S. W. Bengal, in the British Museum, very similar
in shape to Mackenzie's specimen from Thayetmyo District (Smith,
p. 184), which suggests, like the presence of numerous late Neolithic
shouldered celts as far west as Bihar and Orissa, that trade and migra-
tion between India and Indo-China, along the landward routes passing
through Upper Burma, proceeded without serious interruption from
very early times.
The Burma copper and bronze antiquities with which we are deal-
ing show little or no sign of Indian influence. As in the case of the
Burma neoliths their affinities are predominantly with Indo-China and
China. It would seem, therefore, as far as present evidence goes, that
the art of casting copper and bronze objects was introduced into Burma
from China, and it is of interest to note that the form of some of the
late neoliths from the Northern Shan States and the Kachin Hills
(Morris, p. 11 and Pl. X, figs. 1 and 2) suggests an overlap of stone
