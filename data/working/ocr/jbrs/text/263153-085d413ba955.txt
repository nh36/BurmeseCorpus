GASTRO-INTESTINAL MEASUREMENTS IN ORIENTALS, A
SUGGESTED STANDARD FOR THE STOMACH AND
INFORMATION ON THE APPENDIX, MESENTARY
AND PERITONEAL FOSSAE
It has been well said that were we to contemplate the innumerable risks
we run in the mere daily process of living our food would lose its taste, our
happiness its gilt and our worthiest ambitions would surely die in the
unsteady process of being born. Nature has a full day, she holds the complex
skein of life, but her busy hands wavers in the pattern and from time to
time she knits a faulty web.
Research has shown that, if we examine for instance the contents of the
abdomen we find in a certain number of cases instances of under or over
development, abnormalities of rotation, persistence of developmental, bands,
abnormal pockets or fossæ and various evolutional conditions which should
have adjusted themselves before or soon after the birth of the individual.
It was the purpose of this research to find out the prevalence of these
abnormalities as well as to create a standard for visceral measurements, in a
people whose dietary differs materially from that of western people from
whom our present standard anatomy text book measurements have been
derived. Apart from dimensional aberrations, the abnormalities above referred
to, and for which a rather fallable nature must accept responsibility, have
frequently disabled or destroyed individuals either directly or indirectly.
These facts supply ample justification for an inquiry into the prevalence of
such unwelcome anomalies. For the sake of clarity and illustration it may be
said that disablement or death may result from (a) gut strangulation in an
abnormal peritoneal pouch (b) twisting of a mal-rotated gut (c) gut
strangulation due to persistence of bands and adhesions which normally
disappear (d) many other conditions such as non-descent of organs, mal-
positions etc. These need not be detailed further.
Ambition is a barbed adventure and surely it is an iron circumstance
that ends a useful life where neither buns, brandy nor bad living have had an
evil share, but merely the fact that nature and growth have faltered in a
great collaberation.
As stated, the research was also instituted with a view to obtaining some
information regarding the measurements of the normal viscera of orientals.
The importance of this from the medical, surgical and radiological aspects,
needs no emphasis. The distensibility of viscera does not reduce the value of
the information obtained as one dealt with autopsy viscera in all cases.
Particulars of the mesentery, peritoneal fossæ and appendix position
were suitably included in the investigation. The specimens were taken from
autopsy material obtained in Rangoon. In all 36 cases were examined. There
were for the most part either Burmese Buddhists or Indian Hindus. The
former were all mendicants and the latter were rickshaw pullers. The
following are specimen diets of these two classes.

The alcohol used is a rather crude toddy distilled from rice. The Burman
eats large quantities of ngapi. Ngapi is fish (of different kinds) which has
been salted and kept in large earthen pots (Pegu jars) for about a fortnight.
The bones are then removed and the fish is pounded into a pultaceous mass.
The popular view that ngapi is merely pounded rotten fish is incorrect. The
smell is characteristic and forbidding to some but it is not the odour of
ordinary putrifaction.
METHOD EMPLOYED.
After opening the abdomen a survey was made of the various peritoneal
fossæ and the appendix position. The presence or absence of adhesions or
other abnormalities was noted. The mesenteries of the small and the large
intestines including the pelvic meso-colon were measured. It was not
considered necessary to
measure the transverse meso-colon. The next
procedure was the tying and removal of the whole gastro-intestinal tract from
the antrum cardiacum to the anus. All visible fat was then removed and the
mesenteries were cut off close to the attachment of the intestine.
Table 1 (A) records the findings in the case of the stomach, duodenum
and the mesenterial small intestine together with its mesentery.
Table 1 (B). This is merely a continuation of the findings and deals with
the large intestine, appendix, rectum, peritoneal fosse and the pelvic meso-
colon.

MEASUREMENTS. (ALL EXTERNAL)
The viscera were placed on a marble slab and the various parts
measured as indicated (see tables). The various parts were then cut off and
the stomach was subjected to its special measurements. These measurements
are indicated in the diagram (Fig. 1).
Greater curve-cardia to pyloric orifice.
Lesser curve-from a point opposite incisura cardiacum to pyloric
orifice
Height of fundus-from apex of fundus to a line drawn horizontally
from incisura cardiacum to a point opposite it.
Breadth of fundus-the horizontal line drawn from the incisura
cardiacum to a point on the greater curve.
First height of stomach-a perpendicular from apex of fundus to the
point on the greater curvature first touched by this line, (cutting centre of
fundus-breadth line at right angles).
Second height of stomach-a perpendicular drawn from apex of
fundus to level of the lowest part of greater curvature.
Breadth of stomach at its middle-a line joining the mid-points of the
greater and lesser curvatures.
Breadth of stomach at incisura angularis—from the incisura angularis
to the nearest point on the greater curvature opposite.
Breadth of stomach at middle of pyloric canal-a line joining points on
both curvatures midway between the incisura angularis and pyloric orifice.
Breadth at the pyloric orifice-a line across the pyloric orifice.
The measurements in the series do not need any detailed explanation.
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS.
Measurements of the stomach and intestines in 36 cases have been
recorded.
An accurate method of measuring the stomach has been described. The
method could also be used for measuring the living stomach at operation
and also from x-ray films; the necessity for having some standard method,
acceptable to Surgery, Anatomy and Radiology need not be stressed.
Regarding the figures obtained in the various viscera there is little
comment to be made. The appendix was retro-cæcal in 47.2 percent of
cases and its average length 3.05 inches. The length and breadth of the
intestines corresponded fairly well with those of Europeans. The length of
the mesenterial small-intestine varied between 15'6" and 28'8".
In the series examined no developmental abnormalities were found. It
has been shown elsewhere that a purely vegetarian diet tends to affect the
