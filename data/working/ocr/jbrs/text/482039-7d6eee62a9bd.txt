Pacific to the Indian Ocean and form a
bridge between Australia and Asia.
Together with the Malay Peninsular they
command the sea route between the Far
East and Europe. Thus, they are marked
out by nature as a meeting ground for races
and a centre for world commerce.

One may conclude then that already
private enterprise was expanding as rapidly
as could have been expected. For the
liberal policy was not creative; it did not
aim at creating trade, but merely at allow-
ing traders to use natural conditions to the
best advantage. This was especially the
character of the agrarian legislation of 1870.
The Commissioners of 1816 had been app-
rehensive lest freedom for capital should
lead to the expropriation of the peasant.
This had been the strong card of the Con-
servatives during the debates over the Cul-
ture System and had found expression in
the Indian Constitution of 1854. It was on
this point that Van de Putte's Land Bill had
been wrecked in 1865. As the law stood in
1870 there were two main obstacles to
capitalist enterprise in the agricultural deve.
lopment of Java. No one could obtain
land from Government over an area large
enough, for a period long enough and on a
tenure secure enough to justify a heavy out-
lay of capital. Again, in renting land from
cultivators for sugar plantations, it was
necessary to make a large number of sepa-
rate agreements with a host petty land-
holders. De Waal's Agrarian Law of 1870
surmounted these obstacles. It amended
Ariticle 67 of the Constitution of 1854 by
providing for the framing under administra-
tive authority of Ordinances which would
permit the capitalist to take up large areas
on erfpacht, or hereditary lease, and would
enable him to rent land from the natives
for cultivating sugar. Thanks to this law
the enterprising capitalist in Holland was in
a position to profit by the economic
changes which followed on the opening of
Suez Canal.
Intimately connected with the freedom
of enterprise for capital was the problem of
freedom of ownership for the peasant. The
Liberals applied their principle of freedom

to all aspects of social life and argued that
the ownership of land should be as free for
the peasant as for the capitalist. One

dominance of communal ownership. Ap-

round this difficulty in the Agrarian Law

In the study of this period, although

difficulty lies in making a selection from
the abundance of material. But there is
another and more serious difficulty.
We are too close to it. The men

worked for the emancipation of Java in the
Sixties worked by faith, the evidence of

the subsequent world changes due to steam

were right or wrong. But a new line of
