THE PLACE OF ANTHROPOLOGY AMONG
THE SOCIAL STUDIES OF BURMA
By
Kenneth G. Orr
Studies dealing with activities of Man have a long
tradition in Burma ranging from the chronicles of the
ancient kings to the modern research of the state
insitutions. The recent establishment of a department
of anthropology at the University of Rangoon and the
activities of visiting anthropologists of the United States
Educational Foundation Fulbright program have stim-
ulated interest in the position and potential role of
anthropology among the social studies in Burma. This
paper attempts an evaluation in terms of past contribu-
tions and those projected for the future. It follows from
my research and experience of the past year as a
Fullbright grantee attached to the University of Rangoon
as a visiting professor of anthropology.
The subject of the relationship between the various
disciplines studying Man, while controversial, is a par-
tinent and fruitful one. The practical problems of
division of labor and means of cooperative endeavor
between these disciplines faces Burma as the new
nations develops her scientific resources. But these
problems are also common to every area in which scien-
tific research on Man is being carried out. They are espe-
cially pertinent in view of the increasing responsibi-
lities of the social and historical disciplines in providing
a basis for Man's present and future welfare. I hope
the review and prospectus presented here might be a
means of stimulating further discussion of the subject
in Burma and elsewhere

Histories of Events
Historians have been interested primarily in special
knowledge of specific events, relying on written records
for the bulk of their data. The main objective has
been the recording of activities of political units and
their leaders. Three groups of data are recognized;
(1) indigenous histories, (2) contemporary accounts,
and (3) modern histories. The first two groups pre-
ceed and form the basis of the third.
Indigenous histories
-
Burmese and Mon records form the bulk of the
material. A few palm leaf copies and a single printed
history Dinnyawadi Yazawinthit "a third hand piece
of work" - represent the negligible Arakanese records
(Harvey, 1925, p. xix). Shan records are "consistently
reckless with regard to dates", and Siamese script has
changed so profoundly in the past few centuries as to
render the earlier records of little use at present
(Harvey, 1925, p. xix). The so-called hill tribes of
Burma, including the Karen, Chin and Kachin, have
only oral traditions of their past. The written records
are divided into the court chronicles, whose scope
included the realm of the kingdoms, and local histories
or Thamaing, which were restricted to cities or other
local places of interest.
The best known, most valuable, and last of the great
court chronicles was the Hmannan Yazawin- a compi-
lation of legend, chronicle, and folklore assembled by a
committee of Burmese scholars under King Bagyidaw in
1829. The earlier portion of this record, the "Glass
Palace Chronicle", has been translated into English
(Luce and Pe Maung Tin, 1923). Prior to this account
were: the Yazawingyaw, the "earliest history extant", a
legendary account of a mythical past written by the
monk Shin Thilawuntha in the 15th century; the Raza-
darit Ayedawbon, a story of King Razadarit of the 14th
century written in the 16th century by a Talaing officer

The Talaing chronicle of Syriam, translated by Furni-

Free Library of Rangoon. In this account a general
history of Pequ and Burma outside Syriam, is given
as a setting for the semi-legendary history of the city.
The majority of the manuscripts were destroyed deli-
berately by conquorers or through neglect over the
years.
The Burmese chronicles start their histories with
the supposed foundation of Tagaung, an early capital
apparently occupied by half Shan, half Pyu tribes, in
850 B. C. However, the earlier records are regarded
as copied from Sanskrit and Pali originals of Indian
legends (Hall, 1950, p. 7). Maung Htin Aung (1948, p.x)
points out that current folk-legends are historical in
that they amplify episodes mentioned in the chronicles,
and conversely that events given for the period before
1044 A. D. (the Pagan period) may have originally
been folk-tales later incorporated in the chronicles. Re-
cords earlier than two centuries ago are regarded as
of questionable accuracy by modern historians (Harvey,
1925, p. xvii; Hall, 1945, p. 1). The major problem of
the historians working with this material lies in sepa-
rating folklore from historical fact. The facts are fur-
ther handicaped by ethnocentrism on the part of the
chroniclers, as well as disregard of cultural change.
This last led to a characteristic portrait of Burmese
history as that of a stationary civilization. In spite of
these handicaps the chronicles are a mine of informa-
tion "no other country on the mainland of Indochina

Contemporary Accounts
Prior to the coming of Europeans in the 16th
century there is little mention of Burma in the accounts.
of the day. Ptolemy, the Alexandrian scholar, has the
earliest mention of further India in his second century
A. D. geography, describing the natives of the Irrawa-
ddy delta as cannibals (Hall, 1950, p. 7). There are
occasional references to the barbarians to the south
in Chinese histories and encyclopediae. The Arab
geographer Ibn Khordadzebeh in 844-48 A. D. gives
an account of routes and provinces with special refe-
rence to the Mon country, whose king had 50,000
elephants, and whose people produced cotton, velvet
stuffs, and aloes wood (Hall, 1927, p. 1).
The Arab and Indian Mogul traders of the Coro-
mandel Coast were superseded by the Portuguese in
the 16th century, who were in turn displaced by Dutch
and British traders. That these contacts were tenuous
and sporatic was due in largest part to the lack of spices
and the relatively small profits to be made from the
trade in slaves, gems, lac, and teak. The accounts are
filled with political intrigue, adventure, and loud laments
of unsuccessful economic dealings with the natives.

The best of the early accounts is that of the Vene-

Hakluyt's "Principal Navigations" which first appeared
in 1598. His descriptions of Pegu give a wealth of de-

historical interest” (Hall, 1927, p. 7). Fitch's journal is
a boiled down account of Fredericke's travels with only
a few original sections.
The Portuguese, operating out of Goa in southwes-
tern India, were primarily interested in the slave trade
of Arakan and in carrying on a holy war with the
Indian variety of Mohammedanism, the Musselmen of
the Coromandel Coast and Chittagonia. They readily
became mercenaries and one, Filipe de Brito y Nicote,
exceeded his authority to become King of Syriam from
1600 until he was impaled in 1613. Collis (1942) gives
a fascinating account of the period based on the Augus-
tian friar Sebastiao Manrique's travels which was publi
shed along with an extensive bibliography of Portu-
guese sources by the Hakluyt Society. The anonymously
written "A Brief account of the Kingdom of Pegu
(MacGregor, 1926) is typical of the Portuguese accounts
in recording mainly military action.
The major source of Dutch accounts is the Daghre-
gister of the Dutch East India Company which was

Modern Histories
The comprehensive modern histories of Burma can
be counted on the fingers of one hand. In order of
their appearance they are: Phayre 1883, Stuart 1910,
Scott 1924; Harvey 1925, and Hall 1950. The following
evaluations have been made of each: Phayre's pioneer
work was compiled mainly from Burmese sources and
lacked key data from inscriptions (Hall, 1945, p. 2).
Scott's effort is authoritative and written with literary
skill, but parts of the narrative are flippant' and
taken altogether it is a light book" (Temple preface in
Harvey, 1925, p. vii). Harvey's history follows Phayre's
closely but adds much sound detail particularly for the
earlier period" "So much valuable research, however,
has been done since that date that large portions of
this book also are now out of date, and a really authori-
tative work has yet to be written" (Harvey, 1945, p. 2).

Generally, the picture of Burma's past presented
in the historical studies is a static one of political ac-
tion in which individuals and groups succeed one
another in a continuous struggle for dominance. These
actors are shown as the major forces of history in a
haphazard sequence of events. Comprehensive history
in the sense of tracing the evolvement and interactions
of cultural patterns and civilizations as, for example,
the historian Toynbee (1934-39) attempts has not been
produced. Nevertheless, the framework of chronology,
necessary prelude to the greater goal, has been erected
for the more recent periods of Burma's history.

Antiquarian archaeologists may be regarded as
specialized historians pursueing the same objectives of
recording specific events of history, but utilizing a
different body of data. Antiquariar, archaeology has

been distinguished in practice from anthropological
archaeology. The former discipline has concentrated
on the monumental remains and fine arts of historical
sites emphasizing epigraphy as the basic tool, while
the latter's province has traditionally been with the
common remains of preliterate (prehistoric) cultures,
and stratigraphy has been utilized as the basic tool to
give relative chronology. Other differences are pointed
out below. The activities of antiquarians have been in
the fields of epigraphy, exploration, and the preserva-
tion of monuments.
It is difficult to determine the extent to which indige-
nous scholars of Burma utilized inscriptions in their
chronicles. The prominence given to stone slabs con-
taining ancient inscriptions in modern pagodas is in
part a reflection of the sacredness of Buddhist relics as
well as the necessity of retaining records of endowment
credits. However, the stones did serve other purposes.
King Bodawpaya, who died in 1819, gathered 600 stone
inscriptions at Ava for the primary purpose of deter-
mining the church lands which were tax exempt. These
stones and others came to the attention of Burmese scho-
lars, notably the Twinthintaikwun and the Monywe
Sayadaw who helped draw up the Hmannan Yazawin
chronicle in 1829, and to a degree discrepancies between
the records of the inscriptions and those of the chro-
nicles were noted (Luce, 1948, pp. 81-82). Whereas the
script of the earlier Mon inscriptions differed apprecia-
bly from the later written language, Burmese inscriptions
are readily translatable today. The "square Pali" script
is actually still in use in the Kammawasa ordination ser-
vice of the Buddhist order (Harvey, 1925, p. xvi).
Although the inscriptions contain mainly records of
endowments to religious structures, their primary value
to the modern historian lies in the correlation of names
and dates found on them-"....practically the only records
upon which it is safe to rely for anything further back
than two centuries ago are the inscriptions" (Hall
1945, p.1).

In the early 1800's Colonel Burney, British resident
in Ava and his brother copied old Burmese inscriptions
at Bodhgaya, and cooperated with the Burmese scho-
lars mentioned above in translating them. The epigra
phical knowledge of this period is summarized by James
Prinseps in the Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal
(Luce, 1948, p. 82). The German archaeologist Forch-
hammer amassed an extensive collection of inscription
rubbings in his explorations, but died in 1890 before.
they could be presented. His Chinese assistant Taw
Sein Ko published six "elephant" volumes (1892-1913)
the value of which was marred due to modernizing
of spelling, omissions and mistakes (Luce, 1948, p. 84).
The work was taken up by M. Charles Duroiselle,
superintendent of the Archaeology Department of Bur-
ma, and C. O. Blagden (1919-1936, 1921). From 1920 on G.
H. Luce and Pe Maung Tin (1923) collected rubbings at
the University of Rangoon. Both the University's and the
Archaeology Department's collections disappeared dur-
ing the Japanese occupation.
Organized archaeological exploration began in 1881
with the appointment of Forchhammer, then Professor
of Pali at Rangoon College as Government Archaeologist.
The work discontinued by his untimely death was re-
sumed in 1902 by the newly established Burma Archaeo-
logy Department. During the interim the Germans,
geologist Dr. Fritz Von Noettling, and Dr. Thomann
carried off archaeological treasures (including tiles,
carvings, images, and paintings) particularly from Pagan
to Berlin and Hamburg museums acts deplored as
vandalism (Luce, 1948, p. 85).

The Fields of the Social Sciences
A traditional division of labor between anthropolo-
gy and the other social sciences has been on the ba-
sis of Western vs. non-Western peoples anthropology
taking the latter group and the disciplines of sociolo-
gy, economics, political science, and psychology the
former. The division between anthropology and the
historical disciplines has been on the basis of literate
vs. non-literate peoples as mentioned above. Conse-
quently the historical phases of literate peoples readily
fell into the fields of history and antiquarian archaeolo-
gy, and the non-literate groups into the field of anthropo-
logy. However, the studies of current phases of literate
groups in Burma fell into the fields of both anthropo-
logy and the non-anthropological social sciences. Be-
cause these province barriers are being increasingly
over-ridden in Burma, as well as elsewhere, they are
becoming meaningless.
A division of somewhat greater stability has been
on the basis of purpose of study. Anthropological stu-
dies have had as a primary purpose contribution to
the theory of culture and society. The non-anthropo-
logical social sciences have had as a main purpose
contributions to the solving of practical problems.
However, as the non-Western, non-literate peoples in-
creasingly come in contact with modern cultures the
anthropologist, as specialist in this field, is called upon
to contribute data on practical problems of administ-
ration. In response to this need as well as the de-
mand for anthropological analyses of Western societies
a field of applied anthropology has developed. The
administrative records in Burma are in part based on
the concepts and methods of all of the social sciences,
and comprise a large body of data (Burma 1947, etc.).
Perhaps the most valid distinction between the
fields of the social studies is in their distinctive appro-
aches. Anthropology approaches social phenomena

The subject matter and concepts of social anthropology
and ethnology and sociology are nearly identical today.
It is interesting to note that the sociologist Herbert Spen-

to the data of Burma in his "Descriptive Sociology

Paw Oo, have recently taken advanced work in sociology
at Columbia University. Both have theses dealing with
the effect of British administration on the Burmese

In the field of education, research is currently un-

and the State Teacher's Training College to apply
progressive methods in secondary school education. The
research has been stimulated during 1950-51 by he
work of visiting Fulbright professors Doctors Adelain
Babbitt, Emmett Brown, and Harry Griffith as well as a
United Nations team of educational specialists. A tra-

Mi Khaing's colorful ethnographic portrait of a Mon
administrative class family (1946).
Anthropology
While the disciplines discussed above have been
restricted in aim and subject matter, anthropology is cha-
racterized by a virtually limitless interest in man for the
broadest possible understanding. This many-armed
giant is organized into five basic fields: physical anthro-
polopy - the study of man as an organism; archaeology
the study of past phases of cultures; ethnology - the study
of existing cultures and their histories (archaeology is in
part a phase of historical ethnology); linguistics - the
languages of man and their relationships; and social an-
thropology - the study of the nature of cultures and
societies. While the subject matter is diversified, the
aims of the science are unified through its theoretical
structure. To problems concerning the forces governing
men in societies anthropology has especially contributed
to the concepts of pattern and process in culture, and
their relation to the biological factors in man. Because

of its comprehensive scope in both theory and subject
modern anthropology has come to be recognized as a
coordinating discipline among the social and historical
sciences.
Each anthropological field seeks to coordinate with
the others in pursuing the basic aims of the science.
The data of their sciences is also embraced as it pertains
to man. Physical anthropology coordinates with biology,
anatomy, and the medical sciences; ethnology and social
anthropology with economics, sociology, human geogra-
phy, political science and history; linguistics with philolo-
gy and epigraphy; archaeology allies particularly with
history and geology and, of course, with antiquarian
archaeology, but utilizes all of the sciences which help
to explain man's past. The aid of radio chemistry is now
enlisted for dating organic specimen on the principles
of radio-activity. Applied anthropology coordinates
the data for application to current social problems.
It is difficult to think of any study involving man that
doesn't touch on anthropology--"the study of man". In
this wider sense the social studies in Burma have all
been contributions to anthropological problems. How-
ever, to date the studies in Burma called "anthropological"
have been mainly restricted to preliterate cultures, or to
the common culture of existing literate peoples-data
often referred to as "general accounts of the country"
and "prehistory".
There are interesting differences between the modern
conception of the science and that practiced a little over
a generation ago at the University of Rangoon (Taylor,
1922). Taylor's division of physical anthropology, cultural
anthropology, and linguistics are not in disagreement
since "cultural anthropology" embraces archaeology, eth-
nology and social anthropology. However, the purpose
of gathering data was for "classifying races, tribes, and
people", and to provide background information for
general comprehension and for the specialized social
sciences. This interest in encyclopedic taxonomy for its
own sake has given way to classifying as a means of

During 1950 the University of Rangoon carried out
a series of strata-pit excavations at sites in Syriam, Pegu,
Rangoon, Tenasserim, and Moulmein. Culture periods
for the past estimated 600 years of Burmese-Mon occu-
pation in Lower Burma have been defined (Orr, N.D.).
Unlike the work of classical archaeology described above
these excavations have been carried out in habitation
and refuse areas and the remains represent the common
culture. The majority of artifacts are those of the ceramic
industry including potsherds, tiles, and bricks. The
basic tool was that of stratigraphy in which the relative
age of the materials were determined by the super-
position of cultural strata rather than by epigraphy. The
approach is that of beginning with the known culture
of the present day and working backward in time-the
so-called "historical approach". The primary purpose
of the research was to define complexes of associated
artifact types representing cultural periods, by which
to erect a typological time scale for further intensive
definition of the culture patterns. It is expected that
this work will define the ecological aspects of past cul-
tures to provide necessary developmental data for
understanding current cultures in Burma.

In Conclusion
The study of man in Burma to date has been chara-
cterized by relatively extensive, but largely unorganized,
contributions by historians, antiquarian archaeologists,
and amateur anthropological observers. They have dealt
primarily with recent phases of the literate groups and
christianized tribes. Studies in the social sciences in-
cluding modern anthropology have been rare and uncoo-
rdinated, but are beginning to develop. The multiplicity
of facets of the phenomena of man in society necessi-
tates a variety of specialists. However, progress in the
social studies lies in largest part in the recognition of
the interdependence of these facets and thus in coo-
