THE BALUGYUN SAMPLE SURVEY, 1962. *
(1) Introduction
U Thet Lwin **
In Burma, at present, there is a large
amount of statistical data that is regularly
being collected and published mostly as
a by-product of the regular administra-
tion. This data provides a great deal of
information for the study of economic
and social conditions in Burma. But they
have also their limitations mainly because
the needs of administration do not always
coincide with the methods of scientific
study of these conditions. Therefore, there
is often a great need for additional infor-
mation based on more appropriate defini-
tions and for subjects not covered by the
regularly collected statistical data. One of
the great difficulties in the use of such
data is the fact that there is a considerable
time lag between the events studied and
the time, the information becomes available.
But in order to get more and more data
for effective planning of economic and
social developments in the country, it is
necessitated to collect up to data infor-
mation about the existing conditions and
trends of change in all parts of the
country. The most effective method for
getting such information is by way of
probability sampling.
The application of sampling techniques
to social and economic problems in Burma
is of recent introduction. In order to
expand the application of modern sampling
techniques, the experience with sampling
procedures in other countries is of great
help, but sampling work in Burma cannot
be based entirely on such outside experience,
as conditions in Burma
are different in
many respects. Therefore, the adaptation
of improved techniques practised elsewhere
requires considerable research and experi-
mentation with various sampling techniques
to meet the special conditions in Burma.
This survey may be considered as a
further experimentation with improved
sampling techniques in determining some
important aspects of socio-economic pro-
blems in Balugyun.
(2) The Island of Balugyun
The island is situated opposite the
town of Moulmein, in the river Salween
near the mouth where it flows into the
Gulf of Martaban. It lies between North.
latitudes 16° 38' and 16° 30' and between
East longitudes 97° 22' and 97 38' occupying
a total area of about 180 square miles.
It enjoys a tropical Southwest Monsoon
climate with an annual rainfall of about
190".
The inhabitants of the island are
predominantly Mon, although people of
most indigenous Burmese races are to be
found. There are in all 43 village tracts
with a total estimated population of about
80,000 living in about, 15,000 households.

(4) The Sample Design
The Sample Design adopted in this
survey is described by D.G. Horvitz and
D. J. Thompson, "A generalization of
sampling from finite universe", in the
journal of American Statistical Association,
1953.
In accordance to prior information,
the 43 village tracts were stratified into
five strata on the basis of their economic
activities. The actual selection of households
being included in the sample was effected
in two stages. At the first stage, a pair
of village tracts had been chosen from
each stratum without replacement with
probabilties proportionate to their sizes.
And at the second stage, in agreement
with the limited amount of time and
resources available, a sampling intensity
of two percent was so adjusted that the
resulting number of households expected
to be interviewed would fall around 300.
In computing the estimated sampling
variances, standard errors of estimates
and coefficients of variation, the formula
developed by J. Durbin,
developed by J. Durbin, "Some Results
in Sampling theory when the units are
selected with Unequal Probabilities”, in
the Journal of the Royal Statistical Society,
1953 was deliberately used; because the
solutions provided by Horvitz and Thomp-
son do not give satisfactory approxima-
tions in the cases met with in practice.

(9) Finance

is rather complicated and has to cover a
very wide field. First, because the economic
complex of a farm family enterprise com-
bines both producing and consuming activi-
ties. Secondly, the financial transactions
of a productive enterprise is highly inter-
linked with the existing economic and social
features of the area concerned. Thirdly, diffe-
rent methods of estimating rural income;
especially on the evaluation of (i) untraded

any, traded through barter, and (iii) pay-
ments and receipts in kind. Realising the
difficulties that have had to overcome
in exploring this field, an attempt has
