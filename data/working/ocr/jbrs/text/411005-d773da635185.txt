Burma is essentially an agricultural country. It is said that 70
per cent of the population are engaged in rice cultivation and trade. 3
Agriculture is concentrated in the alluvial lands of the delta and
the Irrawaddy, Chindwin and Sittang valleys. According to the
"Economic Survey of Burma 1957" presented in the Parliament by the
Hon'ble Minister of Finance and Revenue, on August 27, 1957, the
total sown acreage is 16,858,000 acres which is 88 per cent of pre-war
averaged. Of this 10,385,000 acres are devoted to rice; 1,466,000
acres to sesamum; 855,000 acres to ground-nut; 580,000 acres to
millet and 399,000 acres to cotton. The total yield of Burma's five
chief crops in 1956-57 is estimated to be 6,362,000 tons of paddy;
55,000 tons of sesamum; 195,000 tons of ground-nut in the husk;
81,000 tons of millet and wheat and 15,600 tons of cotton lint. 4

Now let me briefly deal with the five chief crops of Burma,
namely paddy, sesamum, ground-nut, millet and cotton :
Paddy, Oryza. Botanical writers have described some twenty
species of rice, the genus Oryza. But Benthem and Hooker assert
that five of these can be easily distinguished. Even these five,
they say, are generally viewed as varieties of but one species,
Oryza sativa, Linn. The chief forms are found in the East Indies,
though some are indigenous to Australia and most have been widely
cultivated from ancient times in the warmer regions of both hemis-
pheres. Actually, there are four recognized forms of Oryza, namely
(1) Oryza coarctata, Indus valley wild rice known in Burma as
natsaba ; (2) Oryza granulata, wild hill rice grown on dry soils at
altitudes up to 3000 feet; (3) Oryza latifolia, a tall sparsely branched
species found in Assam and Burma and known as daungsaba 7 in
Akyab and natsaba in Mergui and (4) Oryza sativa, the cultivated
rice.
In Burma Oryza sativa is divided into three main classes: kauk-
kyi or late rain paddy represented by sabanet, byat and meedone;

Sesamum, Sesamum indicum, is a seasonal plant cultivated for its
oil throughout the tropical regions of the world. Botanical evidence
leads to the supposition that the sesamum of sub-tropical agriculture
was originally a native of Africa where there are some eight or nine
truly wild forms. 8
In Burma there are two crops, the early rain crop and the late or
autumn crop. Early sesamum is sown broadcast in May and June
and is reaped in July and August, whilst late sesamum is sown in
September or October and is reaped in December or January. The
early sesamum seeds are the Thadunbyu (white), boketaung (red)
Shan nhan (large seed) and the late sesamum varieties are hnangyi-net
(black) and hnangyi-byu (white). The same kind of seeds is sown
for both crops.
The late crop is considered safer but the early crop
gives larger yield.

During the reign of the Burmese kings all the paddy
produced from the state lands ayadaw mye was stored in
royal granaries. There are two types of royal granaries
namely away kyidaw distant royal granary and ani kyidaw
near royal granary. 21 All these granaries housed diverse
kinds of paddy which are all classed as inferior. On the
other hand there is a kind of good quality rice known as
byat which is only meant for the royal family. Why is
byat classified as "good quality ice"? The proverb
(စပါးစကုဖြတ်၊ မယားတခုလပ် ) “ Of paddy, the bat is the best !
Of wife the divorced woman is the best!" will give the clue
to the answer.
The salary of Government servants were paid in paddy
or rice out of the royal granaries. So if a person speaks
or behaves in a manner above his station in life, we say
( ကျီတော်ဆန်စားပြီး ဖြတ်စကားပြောသည် ) “ He eats rice obtained from
the royal granary (i.e. inferior rice) but talks like one who
