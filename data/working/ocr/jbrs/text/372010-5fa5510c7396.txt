LOGICAL EMPIRICISM AND CARVAKA AND
BUDDHIST SYSTEMS OF PHILOSOPHY 1
by
K. N. KAR, M.A. 2
First of all I shall give, in brief outline, what logical empiricism
is. Our knowledge may be real or verbal. Empiricism believes that
all real knowledge arises from sense-experience and is validated by
sense-experience. In technical terms of logic, real propositions are
called synthetic and verbal propositions are called analytic proposi-
tions. All propositions derived from sense-experience and validated
by sense-experience are called a posteriori. A proposition is validat-
ed, really means here, that its truth or falsity is determined. Hence
propositions whose truth or falsity are determined by sense-experience
are called a posteriori. Propositions whose truth or falsity are deter-
mined independently of experience are called a priori propositions.
Hence the empirical position is that all synthetic propositions are
a posteriori and all a priori propositions are analytic. The old em-
pirical position of Hume was established and maintained mostly on
psychological analysis of the process of knowledge. The new em-
piricism known as logical empiricism is established and maintained
purely on logical grounds. Modern developments of symbolic logic
have been the main basis of its arguments. As opposed to the em-
pirical school, some other philosophers have maintained that a priori
synthetic propositions are possible. One view is that metaphysics
establishes such a priori synthetic propositions about ultimate reality
through sheer reasoning which is deductive. Modern developments of
logic have shown that logical propositions are tautologous. That is,
they are a priori analytic propositions. Hence deductive logic can-
not give a priori synthetic propositions. They further maintain that
as it is the essential characteristic of all propositions to be true or
false, there must be methods for the determination of the truth or
falsity of every genuine proposition. If there is no method for the
determination of a particular variety of so-called propositions, then
they are not genuine propositions. Synthetic a posteriori propositions
have a method for the determination of the truth or falsity of such
propositions. The method is that of verification by sense-experience.
All scientific propositions are so verifiable. All logical and mathe-
matical propositions are a priori analytic. They are shown to be true
by the fact that their contradictories are impossible. By the matrix
method also it can be shown that logical propositions are always true.
But there is no method which has found universal acceptance for the
determination of the truth or falsity of a priori synthetic propositions.
The genuineness of a proposition is determined by what will determine
its truth or falsity. As a posteriori synthetic propositions alone among
real propositions can have its truth determined, namely, through veri-
fication by sense-experience, it is asserted that verification by sense-

further maintain that the sense of a proposition is really determined
by what the proposition verifies. Hence according to them sense-
perception alone can give real knowledge. If reasoning be divided
into deductive and inductive, they maintain that deductive logic does
not give real knowledge. As for inductive reasoning, they do not
recognise the process as logical. Inference through generalization is
a purely psychological process. It is the repetition of the view of
Hume. Perception guarantees the truth of singular propositions but
it cannot guarantee the truth of universal propositions. The process
of generalization of universal propositions of unlimited instances from
singular propositions of perception is not logically valid. This was
the extreme position of the earlier forms of logical empiricism. But
some later forms have acknowledged the necessity of recognizing
generalisation as a valid process.
I shall sum up the whole position of the logical empiricists in
ordinary language. All real knowledge is from sense-experience.
Reasoning, meaning deductive reasoning, cannot give us any addi-
tional knowledge which is real beyond what is contained in the pre-
mises. Hence reasoning cannot be considered as a means of addi-
tional real knowledge beyond that of sense-perception. As we can-
not have sense-perception of God or gods, souls, the world beyond,
there can be no genuine knowledge concerning them. All meaningful
expressions in language are meaningful because they tell us what to
expect in our sense-experience. If an expression in language does
not and cannot tell us what to expect in sense-experience for their
truth or falsity then they are meaningless or senseless expressions.
As statements concerning God, gods, souls, other world, reality, higher
reality do not and cannot tell us what to expect in sense-experience
to know their truth or falsity, they are senseless expressions. Hence
there can be no genuine propositions about realms beyond sense-
experience. As 'This is yellow' and 'This is right' is not veri-
fiable by anything in the external world like ‘This is straight', morality
also is not expressive of anything objective and real. They stand for
certain subjective conditions and attitudes merely. Hence morality is
a purely subjective affair.
The views of the Carvakas have not been properly preserved. It
is only preserved in the accounts of other philosophical schools. The
account given here is principally based on the account given in Madha-
vacharya's 'Sarva Darsana Samgraha'. They recognize perception as
the only source of real knowledge. This is the fundamental tenet of
logical empiricism too. Carvakas reject inference as a valid source of
knowledge. In Indian logic, inducive logic had never been separated
from deductive logic. Though this non-separation leads in a certain
sense to the right appreciation of the value of reasoning as used in
actual thinking still the right appreciation of the distinctions of the
two distinct processes did not take place because of this non-separation.
The accounts about the Carvakas do not show the realization on their
part that reasoning which is not inductive, namely, which does not
involve generalization, is analytic and tautologous. They do not even
show the recognition of the presence of such reasoning. To them
every reasoning seems to involve generalization in the major premice.

They refute the validity of the inductive process of reasoning. This
they think is the refutation of all reasoning. In this refutation they
have not been surpassed by any of the modern empiricists. In the
syllogism the major premise gives the universal proposition. So the
problem of induction is the establishment of the invariable connection
between the middle and the major terms. How is it established?
Perception cannot establish it. The account says, ...; for although
it is possible that the actual contact of the senses and the object will
produce the knowledge of the particular object thus brought in contact,
yet as there can never be such contact in the case of the past or the
future, the universal proposition which was to embrace the invariable
connection of the middle and major terms in every case becomes
impossible to be known." (1)
The universal relation between middle and the major depends
also on the unconditionalness of the middle. In their words, "Again,
this same absence of a condition, which has been given as the defini-
tion of the universal connection (i.e., universal proposition), can itself
never be known." (2)

There are many more logical criticisms of importance given
against the logical validity of inductive generalization. Finally it says,
; and that in some cases this step (the step which the mind
takes in generalization) is justified by the result, is accidental just like
the coincidence of effects observed in the employment of gems, charms,
drugs, etc." (3)
The Carvakas further maintain that through inference people
come to different conclusions about them. Hence inference leading to
contradictory conclusions cannot be said to give knowledge about
them. Hence as reasoning is of no value in advancing knowledge and
as we cannot perceive by the senses, God or gods, souls, the other
world, the laws, we can not assert the existence of such things. The
earlier form of logical empiricism would wonderfully correspond with
this position. The logical empiricist says, "The man who makes in-
ductive inferences may be compared to a fisherman who casts a net
into an unknown part of the ocean-he does not know whether he will
catch fish, but he knows that if he wants to catch fish he has to cast
his net. Every inductive prediction is like casting a net into the ocean
of the happenings of nature; we do not know whether we shall have
a good catch, but we try at least, and try by the help of the best means.
possible." (4)
Both this ancient school and its modern counterpart are skepti-
cal about the logical basis of induction. The only difference will be
the logical way of putting the situation. The logical empiricist will
say that universal propositions are not genuine propositions because
they cannot be verified to be true by any perceptible set of singular
propositions. In logical language the universal propositions are not
truth-functions of any definite set of perceptible singular propositions
or in their terminology protocol propositions. Such terms as God,
gods, next life, etc. are also without sense because we cannot decide
what perceptible situations will determine their truth or falsity.

But this is suicidal even for science, not only for religion and
morality, because science itself claims to give us knowledge of the laws
of nature. As a view it is logically irrefutable. What they say con-
cerning induction is undoubtedly logically sound. But, life cannot go
on on this basis. So the body of scientific knowledge has to be justified.
So some of the later logical empiricists have tried to justify such a
body of scientific knowledge. For this purpose some logical empiricists
have joined hands with the pragmatists and some of them call them-
selves operationalists.

Buddhist systems of philosophy come out with almost the same
defence for inference. In the language of "Sarva Darsana Sangraha,"
; for such doubts alone are to be entertained, the entertainment
of which does not implicate us in practical absurdity and the like, as it
has been said, 'Doubt terminates where there is practical absurdity'." (5)
But is has to be acknowledged that the Carvakas, are right,
along with their modern counterparts in logical empiricism, that the
existence of soul, God, next-life and the like super-natural and trans-
cendental things cannot be demonstrated by any amount of reasoning.
This has been acknowledged by Kant and also by many Hindu ortho-
dox thinkers. Hence their logical position is irrefutable. The Hindu
orthodox thinkers tried to put the burden of proof on the Carvakas
by asking 'How do the Carvakas know that they do not exist?' But
I do not think that saves the position.
According to one Nyaya treatise mentioned by Hiriyanna, the
Carvakas did not deny inference altogether for purpose of life, but
denied that through inference God, soul and such transcendental things
can be demonstrated. (6) But this much is sure that by pointing out the
insecure logical basis of inductive generalization, the Carvakas and the
logical empiricists do give us the necessary corrective to view human
knowledge as only probable and relative. Science does not deal with
certainties but only probabilities. The logical empiricist says, "Gone
is the ideal of the scientist who knows the absolute truth. The happen-
ings of nature are like rolling dice rather than like revolving stars;
they are controlled by probability laws, not by causality, and the
scientist resembles a gambler more than a prophet. He is a better
gambler, though, than the man at the green table, because his statistical
methods are superior.” (7)
When I am mentioning Buddhist philosophy, I am referring
to the accounts of such philosophy as left in the records of the other
systems of Indian philosophy. As I have already indicated, they oppos-
ed the Carvaka view about inference and their denial of the validity of
the laws of nature, on the ground that such a view is in contradiction
to the practices of life. They distinguished universal laws of two
kinds, namely, causal laws and laws of identity. Nothing happens
without a cause. Only when a certain cause takes place, a certain
effect is brought about. If the cause does not take place, the
effect does not take place. Everything is conditional and relative.
This is the only theory of the dependent origination of things. The
logical empiricist demurs to such a view of causal determinism. He
says, "that the idea of a strict causality is to be abandoned, and

When the Buddhist philosophers talk of another kind of
universal proposition namely that of identity, they come to recognize
that some reasonings are very different from those which involve induc-
tive generalization in their major premise. It is in line with the
modern distinction of deductive and inductive reasoning and further
recognition of the fact that the former involves mere repitition. If
Indian logic had proceeded to develop on this line of distinction, it
would have come to recognize the difference between deductive and
inductive reasonings and to the realization that deductive reasoning
is tautologous. Unfortunately this development did not take place.
Strictly from the Buddhistic point of view, reals are particulars with
their individual characters (svalaksana). Reasoning uses universals
(Samanyalaksana). Universals are merely names and the product of
imagination. There is no identity of two things. There is mere simi-
larity. Hence reasoning distorts the nature of reality. So reasoning
cannot be more than working fiction. Dinnaga says that the whole
process of inference refer to what is imposed by thought and has no
reference to external reality. (9) Logical empiricists will only change
the word 'thought' to 'language'.
The Buddhists refused to dabble on certain questions on the
ground that answers to such questions are not finally determinable.
"Some things Potthapada, I have laid down as certain, other things I
have declared uncertain; The latter are those ten indeterminates that
you raised, and for the reasons given I hold them matters of uncer-
tainty." (10)
This view comes very near to the view of the logical empiri-
cist. The logical empiricist, maintains that every valid kind of know-
ledge must have tests for the determination of their truth and falsity.
If their truth and falsity are not determinable, they are not genuine
propositions. The Buddhists did not formulate their view in such
logical terms. But we can very correctly feel that such a view was
vaguely at the back of their mind when they asked people
not to think about indeterminable questions. Their logic was not
sufficiently developed to enable them to formulate their view in the
language of logical empiricism.
About the first cause the logical empiricist says, Could there be
a father who never had a child? Every one would ridicule a philo-
sopher who regarded this question as a serious problem. The ques-
tion of the cause of the first event, or of the cause of the universe as
a whole, is not of a better type. The word ‘cause' denotes a relation
between two things and is inapplicable if only one thing is concerned.
The universe as a whole has no cause, since, by definition, there is
nothing outside it that could be its cause. Questions of this type are
empty verbalisms rather than philosophic arguments. The conclusion
is Buddhistic but the reasons are based on modern logical researches.
The Buddhist philosophy is against any notion of substance. It is
called the doctrine of insubstantiality. We must not think of some

there is to it. The changes are according to laws. This is pheno-

some cases. The substance which lies hidden behind phenomena has

Logical empiricists and the Carvakas have no real place for
morality and religion. But Buddhism believes both in the reality of
moral life as well as in the reality of religious quest. In terms of the
Indian conception of human goals (Purusarthas) the Carvakas believe
only in artha and Kama (wealth and sense-pleasure) so do the logical
empiricists. But the Buddhists believe in dharma and Moksa in addi-
tion. Buddhism believes in other worlds, in rebirths as well as in the
reality of nivana. These are not matters which can be known by
sense-perception. Nor can they be known by reasoning. Buddhists
introduce super-normal perception as the source of such knowledge.
Through abhinna we can see and hear in the other worlds, we can know
other people's thoughts, we can recollect our own previous births, we
can know about other peoples previous births, we can be certain of the
emancipation already attained. (11)
In common with this Buddhistic view, that things beyond.
sense-perception cannot be known through any amount of reasoning
and that such phenomena can only be known by super-normal percep-
tion (abhinna), the Vedantin view is also the same. Super-sensible
reality cannot be known by any amount of reasoning because reasoning
takes us to varied conclusions, all of which cannot be true at the same
time. It can be known by yogaja perception only which is another
name for abhinna. But the Vedantins claim to know Brahman as the
permanent reality behind all phenomena by such means. Is this not
in variance with the Buddhist conclusion that things are without
substance? Hence in my humble opinion super-normal perception
also does not give any certain knowledge about super-sensible pheno-
mena. Anyhow, it in no way makes our knowledge about such things
more certain than reasoning. It is quite in place here to mention the
Mimamsaka criticism of yogi-pratyaksa. "As the external senses
cannot come in contact with the distant objects in time and space and
mind can independently of the external sense organs produce only
experiences of pleasure and pain, these experiences cannot be per-
ceptual." "The dependence of consciousness to the mind and the
senses was also fully appreciated by the Nyaya philosophy when it
denied consciousness to the soul when disjoined from the mind and
the senses. We are condemned to this predicament, namely, we can
not imagine any knowledge of objects which is not in terms of sense-
perception. Further we cannot think of knowledge except through
mind-body organism." (12)
In Indian Philosophy the reality of morality and religion has
been maintained by the Mimamsaka, without advocating Super-normal
perception. "Religion and morality are not matters of truth or know-
ledge. They are concerned with imperatives, that is action. If you
want this do this." (13) The logical empiricists may not be averse to
this view of religion and morality.

In summary we may note the agreements and disagreements of
these three groups. They all deny the reality of soul and God. The
logical empiricists and Carvakas deny the validity of inductive reason-
ing. The Buddhists do maintain the validity of inductive generalization
and reasoning. The logical empiricists recognize a separate kind of
reasoning namely deductive reasoning as logical but do not believe
that it can extend our real knowledge. The Buddhists and the logical
empiricists are both nominalists. Reasoning does not represent any
process of reality. The universals are mere names. Both resort to
pragmatic grounds to justify knowledge. The Buddhists believe in laws
not only in nature but also in moral sphere. The Carvakas and the
logical positivists are very sceptical about such laws. They are nothing
more than throwing stones into the unknown to obtain hits like a
gambler. These two also do not believe in the objective reality of moral
and religious goals. The Buddhists do believe in the reality of such
goals. But the Buddhists with the logical empiricists maintain insub-
stantiality of things. They also believe that reasoning cannot prove
anything about super-sensible reality. But the Buddhist believes that
by super-normal perception we can know things beyond nature. Thus
Buddhism avoids the sceptical conclusions of the Carvaka and the
logical empiricists.
