JOUR. BURMA RESEARCH SOC.
XXV, III, 1935.
BURMESE PSYCHOLOGY
Dear Mr Secretary,
25-7-35.
I have for some time wondered whether the Burma Research Society
might initiate an enquiry into the psychology of Burmese character and as
I have been put on to the Committee I feel justified in putting the suggestion
forward.
No doubt this is a delicate matter to tackle, but much has been written
on American and European psychology and I am sure an enquiry along the
same lines would be profitable. There must be many Burmese members of
the Society of right experience and critical capacity who could contribute,
and there are also many other persons who have for years sympathetically
observed.
A knowledge of the history and of the present day customs and reactions
as well as of the language and religion would be required, but partial
knowledge of one or more of these would be of use.
I remember that Alec Patterson who came out at the request of the
Burma Government to visit and inspect and report on prison and crime and
especially juvenil offenders made various suggestions e.g. that the excessive
brutality which often accompanies dacoity is to be traced to the conditions
under which people lived under the Burmese kings, when a powerful minister
never knew on rising of a morning whether he would be supreme or whether
he would be dragged out of the royal presence by his hair and handed over
to the executioners.
One thing which must strike observers is the existence of excessive
kindness-chnahde-with excessive brutality. There must be a psychological
explanation, and therefore a psychological treatment.
Again, so far as schoolboys are concerned in my own experience, a boy
may and bluster when detected in a fault but if you to say to him min-
it sh de he will say "yes" and be silent if it is no. Why is this?
Again, I am told that a criminal in Upper Burma if he is unsophisti-
cated will invariably make a confession upon arrest, though after he has
consulted a lawyer he will retract it. This is said to be due to the fact that
in olden days a prompt confession meant a speedy death (say, by beheading),
where unwillingness to confess meant a painful and lingering death (say, by
disembowelling and pegging out).
Again, what are the effects of present and past polygamy upon the
national character?
What incidents of peculiar behaviour have come to the attention of
members, and can a psychological analysis of these be attempted so as to
reveal underlying motives?
How far can the decay in manners which most elderly Burmese deplore
be traced to the official abolition of the shikko and what is likely to be the
psychological effect of an attempt to restore it in private life, or what
substitutes for it have been secured or can be secured in the training of the

It seems to me an enquiry along these lines by competent persons can be
undertaken and would be of practical utility as well as being an interesting
piece of research.
The efforts of the Council of National Education to promote the study of
"Civics and Morals" in schools is probably much more humanistic than
political in motive, and is I suppose intended to strike at corruption and
unreliability; but it is all in the air and there is nothing solid to get hold of.
So far as I know no serious study of Burmese psychology has been
attempted; yet some study of it, and comparison with other mentalities
Eastern and Western, would seem to be one of the necessary factors in any
attempt to hasten or direct the progress of Burma in any direction―
cultural economic or political. Towards such an attempt the Society's
Journal might contribute by the collection of materials.
Yours Faithfully
C. KENDRICK HUGHES
