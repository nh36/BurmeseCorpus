A DIPLOMATIC MISSION FROM BURMA TO AMERICA
Mindon Min is generally credited with having been one of
Burma's best kings; at any rate he was undoubtedly the most astute
among the Alaungpaya dynasty in his relations with the powers.
During his reign of a quarter century he was careful to maintain
correct dealings with the British government and its representatives
in India and Lower Burma, although it cannot be said that he was by
any means an Anglophile. Mindon's sincere efforts to modernize
his kingdom extended to the establishment of factories and industries
in the capital city, opening of river navigation and telegraph lines,
and education abroad of young Burmans, and the interchange of
foreign diplomatic missions.
More than fifteen instances of official intercourse between
Burma and foreign states during Mindon's reign are listed in the
Gazetteer. 1 While these were principally formal missions between
Mandalay and British Burma and India, envoys were sent to Britain,
France and Italy. There were return visits by accredited represen-
tatives from these states. Mindon at the same time took a great
interest in the fortunes of British arms in Africa, Asia, and the Crimea,
and proposals were made for embassies to Russia and Afghanistan.
While a resident of Burma, the present writer read, in Burmese
histories, accounts of a mission from the President of the United
States to the Burmese Court. Careful search in the standard Ameri-
can diplomatic histories, including the monumental work by Dr.
Bemis, failed to reveal any mention of American relations with Burma
beyond that already cited from the American State Papers. 2 A
chance reading of the Gazetteer brought to my attention the reference
therein to the mission, and search was begun in earnest for any source
material on this side. 3 Direct inquiry at the State Department and
the National Archives in Washington produced the documents
which are quoted in this paper.
Photostatic copies of the pertinent documents were secured
and they are being sent to the library of the Society in the hope that
they may prove of some value in reconstructing the interesting history
of the impact of Burma with the modern world. These papers,
which are used in full or in abridgement in this paper, are as
follows:-

Contrary to the Burmese accounts, the evidence is that the
mission to America was initiated by the King of Burma. As his
envoy to America the King chose Dr. Eugenio Kincaid, an American
Baptist Missionary who had spent more than twenty years in Burma. 4
Reasons for the choosing of an American instead of a Burman are
seen in the fact that the King had in February, 1856, sent an embassy
of his most trusted advisers in foreign affairs, including J. S. Manook
the Kalawun, to France, and plans for the removal of the capital
from Amarapura to Mandalay occupied the other ministers.
Kincaid's early experiences in Burma gave him an excellent
preparation for his later work as a representative of the Burmese
king. Arriving in Moulmein in November, 1830, he visited Ava
for the first time in 1833 during the troubled reign of Bagyidaw.
Shortly thereafter he visited Bhamo, Tavoy, Arakan, and other
parts of Burma. In 1843 he returned to America where he spent
the next seven years, returning to Rangoon early in 1851 just in time
to play a part in the second Anglo-Burmese War very similar to that
of Dr. Judson's colleagues in the opening scenes of the first war,
1824-1826. After interesting experiences in connection with Com-
modore Lambert's expedition to Rangoon, Kincaid removed to
Moulmein where he remained until September, 1855, at which time
a messenger arrived from Amarapura to inquire when he would
visit the capital city. 5

At the second interview, when the final instructions were given,

Ava is not given, but they arrived in New York on February 18,
1857, and by March 24 Kincaid made request to the Secretary of
State for permission to present his letter from Burma.

Three translations of this letter are available. The version
found in Patton does not differ essentially from the translation pre-
pared by Kincaid on April 14, 1857, and delivered to the Secretary
of State on the 16th. This translation was certified before a notary
public in Washington. However, important differences are seen
between Kincaid's translation and that made by U Po Mya of Cushing
High School at the request of the American Consul, Rangoon, in
1925. It is quite possible that Kincaid's translation incorporated
verbal or supplementary written instructions delivered with the
original letter in Amarapura in April, 1856. U Po Mya's translation,
which appears to follow very closely the original Burmese, is as
follows:
We, who are the Ministers and Generals of the King of Burma, the Over-
lord of all the Kings of the Orient, the most powerful Sun-rising Lord, the
Lord of Sadden Elephant King, the Lord of Many White Elephants, and the
Great Righteous Rufer; and we who are doing homage to the King by bowing
our heads to his Golden Feet, which are like the Paduma lotus Flower, write
this letter to the President and Ministers who are the rulers of both Washington
and the countries of the west. The powerful and Righteous Ruler who occupies
the throne and rules all the countries of the ancestors and who tries to administer
justice like the former Pralaung and the good kings of the past, wished to negotiate
a royal treaty between Burma and America like the royal treaty of the ancestors.
When the American Teacher, Mr. Kincaid, came under the Golden Feet he was
allowed to come in and go out of the Palace and to behold the Golden Face of
the King and to send in the petition without any obstructions. If the treaty is
made between Burma and America, and if it be lasting from sons to grandsons,
and from grandsons to great grandsons, there will be advantages both for traders
and common people. Seeing this advantage I send this letter to you through
Teacher Kincaid. The Generals and Ministers also seeing the lasting advantages
for the traders and common people in making the treaty between the two countries
hereby send this letter to the Rulers of America for the purpose of making the
treaty.
It is evident from Kincaid's letter to President James Buchanan,

President Buchanan's inquiry, What would be pleasing to
the King with reference to this letter?" brought this interesting
response from Kincaid, "I know from the many inquiries made by
the King and his Ministers that nothing could be more acceptable
than a small steam locomotive after the model of that sent to Japan."
Reference was made here to the model railway which was taken by
Commodore Perry to Japan in 1853. The Perry expedition was
quite well known among the British in Burma and it is entirely pos-
sible that the Burmese were aware of the American action in Japan. 6
Townsend Harris, American Minister to Japan under the treaty
negotiated by Perry, visited Siam while on his way to his post. He
presented the two Kings of Siam with a number of valuable scientific
instruments; but Mindon's presents were limited to "a number
of valuable national publications" which were delivered to Kincaid. 7
Having completed his business in Washington, Kincaid pro-
cured some "astronomical instruments for the Karen schools in
Rangoon," left his family in America, and sailed from New York
for Burma on September 5, 1857. He returned by way of Scotland,
Mrs. Kincaid's childhood home. Embarking on the Indus at South-
ampton the 20th October 1857, he travelled out by the recently
opened overland route and reached Calcutta within fifty days travelling
time from New York. On the Indus Lord Dalhousie and his two
daughters were fellow passengers as far as Malta. Kincaid records
of Lord Dalhousie, "He recognized me as soon as I came on board,
invited me to sit down, and we had a long conversation.".
Back in Burma, Kincaid spent nearly six weeks in Rangoon
and Prome with his fellow Americans one of whom, Dr. Mason,
offered to raise a battalion of Karen Christians to aid in suppressing
the Indian Mutiny. A month was consumed touring the villages
in the vicinity of Prome, "with a saddle for a pillow." Kincaid
left Prome the 12th February for his journey to Amarapura, where
he found preparations under way for the removal of the court to
Mandalay.
The King appeared pleased with the return of his envoy, and
issued a royal proclamation for the reception of the President's reply.
Upon the day appointed it was received with demonstrations of
respect. A splendid zayat was built for its reception, and it was
carried by royal procession five miles into the city. On March 20,
1858, Kincaid and Dawson were granted an interview by the King
in which he expressed his desire to have an American Consul reside

The first is found in connection with the instructions issued to Edmund
Roberts, an American diplomatic agent sent to southern Asia in 1832.
After the departure of Roberts from Boston, supplementary instruc-
tions were sent to await his arrival in Batavia. These authorized
him to visit, in addition to China, Japan, Cochinchina, Siam, and
Muscat, "the Birman Empire and the King of Atcheen."8 But
Roberts passed on without visiting Burma. The second reference
