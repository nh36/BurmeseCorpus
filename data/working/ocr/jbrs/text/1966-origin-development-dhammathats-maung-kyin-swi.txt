I. The Case for An Indian Origin of the
Dhammathats.
The points of similarity between the
Burmese Dhammathats and the Hindu Code
of Manu were noticed by Major Michael
Symes, when he visited Burma in 1795.
Symes met Father Sangermano and, having
got him to translate parts of the Burmese
Code into Latin, was struck by its resem-
blance to the Hindu Manu, of which he had
to
a Persian copy. In 1796 Captain Hiram
Cox, another Britisth Envoy, came
Burma and wrote a contemptuous remark
about the King of Burma, who in 1795 had
got some Armenians to make a Burmese
translation of Sir William Jones' "Institutes
of Manu". Cox said this was a useless
labour as, in the Burmese Dhammathats,
they had the famous Manu already. In 1852
Dr. R. Rost, the librarian of the India
Office, brought one of these codes to the
notice of European scholars, and pointed
out the resemblance to Hindu law.'
In 1860 Major Sparks in his "Code of
Burmese Law" called the Burmese Dhamma-
thats the Burmese version of the Laws of
Manu.2
Colonel Horace A. Browne,
Commissioner of Pegu, in his preface to the
Manoo Woonnana Dhammathat, published
in 1878, said that the Burmese Dhammathats
are mainly of Brahmanic origin and owed
little to the Buddhist scriptures. He said,
"The Buddhist law, properly so called, is
contained in the 'Pie-ta-gat-thoon-boon' (the
three baskets -- the Tripitaka) divided into
the Thoot-tan, Wie-nee and Abhiedhamma
(or Discourse, Discipline and Pre-eminent
Truth). Of these, the Wie-nee contains
many passages that lay down the law appli-
cable to the religious usages of the people,
but the rules that govern inheritance, marri-
age, divorce etc., among the laity, are
contained in totally distinct works known
generally as 'the Dhammathat' or 'the
Dhammathat of Manoo', which form no por-
tion of the Buddhist law. These works in
fact, as stated before, are more Brahmani-
cal than Buddhist, and the term 'Buddhist
Law' when applied to them is a misnomer.
Of the Dhammathats there are various
versions with various titles. They profess
generally to be based on the Pali text, but
contain also passages, which have evidently
been interpolated in later days to suit the
changing forms of society. One of their
most prominent characteristics is a total
want of systematic arrangement. Various
and often inconsistent provisions on
cognate subject are scattered here and there

Dr. Ba Han, Professor of Laws in
Rangoon University, gave the following
account of the origin of the Burmese
Dhammathat. “By about the 3rd. century
A.D., Indian traders had established trading
settlements in the coast towns of Thaton,
Prome, Pegu and Rangoon. The Hindu
settlers brought with them their sacred
books which are the source of their religion
as well as their laws. At about that time
Kanchi or Conjeeveram near Madras became
a great centre of both Brahmanical and
Buddhist learning. Archaelogical evidence
convincingly proves that southern Buddhism
was well established in old Prome by about
the 5th. century. Two gold plates with
Pali epigraphs bearing a close affinity with
the Kadamba script of the 5th. or 6th.
centuries A. D. were discovered in 1897 at
Maunggan village near old Prome. The
epigraphs on these plates contain well
known southern Buddhist formulae and the
ascription of praise to the Buddha as
described in Mahaparinibbana Sutta of
Digha Nikaya.20 Moreover, the Lopburi
Inscription, which is the oldest inscription
in Mon (Talaing) so far discovered, is in
the Pallava script of the 8th, century.21
"These pieces of indubitable evidence
lend colour to the Burmese tradition that
during the 5th. century A. D. Buddhaghosa,
the well known Buddhist commentator
brought the entire Buddhist scriptures with
the commentaries and Hindu law books
from Ceylon and India to Ramañña, the
country of the Talaings. Be that as it may,
by the 5th. century, Buddhism of the
southern school had doubtless been brought
across the Bay of Bengal to lower Burma.
The conquest in the 11th. century of the
Talaing Kingdom of Thaton by Anawrahta,
King of Pagan, is really the intellectual
triumph of the Talaings, who brought with
them mental ardours which produced the
flowering time of the Burmese genius in
many fields. A new era was inaugurated.
The Talaings had then already been under
Indian influence for some seven or eight
centuries. They had adopted as a model
for their laws Hindu law with necessary
modifications to suit their social conditions
and their religious faith. The Hindu
influence is clearly discernible in the Wagaru
Dhammathat. The very term Dhammathat
betrays its Indian origin. It is transparently
the adaption of the Hindu Dharma Shastras.
Manu is relied on as the final authority."
