A VERNACULAR HISTORY
BY
Maung Tha Aung and Maung Mya Din
Edited by H. R. Alexander
Editorial Note: -
Many years ago I was lent the following narrative by U Tun In.
Headman of Kangyi East Village Tract, Meiktila District. Some of
the characters mentioned were known to me.
At the request of the late Sir B. S. Carey to whom it was dedicated.
this story of the Annexation of Upper Burma was written in Burmese
by Maung Tha Aung, A.T.M., youngest brother of "Sitke" Maung Tun
E, soon after the occurrence of the events in which he took a prominent
part. It was translated into English by Maung Mya Din. All that I
have done is to correct the English in order to render it more suitable
for publication. I have retained the original sense and expression as
far as possible.
PART I
CHAPTER I
A GENERAL REVIEW OF THE COUNTRY AFTER THE
CAPTURE OF THE CITY OF MANDALAY
On the 7th lazan of Tazaung-mon, One Thousand Two Hundred and
Forty Seven, Burmese era, (13-11-1885), over ten thousand British
soldiers landed at the Gaw-Wein Shore at Mandalay, and surrounded
King Thi Baw's palace. Colonel Sladen and some of his Lieutenants
entered the palace and explained to King Thi Baw that they had been
sent out on a mission by His Excellency the Viceroy of India to escort
him to India. The King blushed at this, but he had no other alter-
native than to surrender to the British Mission without any offer of
resistance.
Having thus communicated the orders of the Viceroy, Colonel
Sladen and his party left the palace to rejoin the troops outside.
On the following day, the King and Queen were removed from the
palace to a building in the South Garden. At 3 p. m. the same day,
after taking charge of the ex-King's treasure, Colonel Sladen took King
Thi Baw and his family to the shore in a vehicle shaded by white
umbrellas as an emblem of royalty, leaving the City by the Kyaw-mo
Gate on the south. A number of his attendants were also taken with
him. The Royal party was then put on board the English steamer
which lay at anchor on purpose off the Gaw-Wein Shore.

The next day, the 9th lazan of Tazaung-mon, she steamed down the
Irrawaddy, reaching Rangoon on the 15th lazan idem. On the 6th day
of their arrival at Rangoon they were removed to a British liner, and

The ex-King and his family were kept there for some time, but after-

The general administration of the country was none the worse, at
first, for this sudden deportation of King Thi Baw, for most of the
officials remained loyal to the British and continued to hold their re-
spective charges as efficiently as ever, they only hoping that their ap-
pointments would be retained by their new Rulers. There were, of
course, some officials who being timid abandoned their old posts and
removed to places where the majority of their relatives resided.
The administrative functions of the old "Hluttaw" were confirmed
by Colonel Sladen, and the Kin-wun Mingyi and other members of the
Hluttaw," who were retained by Colonel Sladen, continued to exercise
their powers as before.

While things were thus going on smoothly an unhappy incident
occurred at Kyauk-taung, a frontier station near Pyinmana. The Thit-
kyeik Wun of Pyinmana Division proceeded to Kyauk-taung with his
followers to surrender to the British column stopping there, but the
officer commanding that column failed, according to the notion then
prevalent, to properly exercise his discretion, for instead of treating the
loyal servant who had come to him to surrender with his followers, he
tied him with a chain, and handled him rather roughly. This bad news
took wing to every Burman, and contributed to the disturbance of the
minds of many of the leading men who were preparing to submit to the
British Crown. Some of the officials lost confidence in the British and
removed to distant places leaving their respective charges ungoverned.
The result was that the dacoits and bad characters began to disturb the
country and brought about rebellions.
Lewun Maung San Dun of Pyinmana and dacoit leader Boh E
joined forces and set up at Taung-nyo Lema ready to make a stand
against the British advent. Maung Min Naing, Myothugyi of Yamethin,
collected a number of men at Yamethin to rebel against the British.

daing Prince, and his followers ravaged the country, making arrange-

at Kyauk-taung, the Myo-wun Maung Pyu had abandoned his post and
had fled to Mandalay, and the dacoits in the tracts of Pyaw-bwe,
Nyaung-yan and Hlaing-det were left unchecked. To add fuel to the

CHAPTER II
PACIFICATION OF THE MANDALAY DISTRICT
The "Hluttaw" authorities had up to then failed to exert any in-
fluence over the rebels and dacoits with all Colonel Sladen's retention
of their original powers. At length when it was found that its retention
was useless, the "Hluttaw" was abolished, and its members pensioned
off, they being unable to suppress even the petty local risings in Man-
dalay and its neighbourhood. Some time afterwards a declaration was
made by the Government by which the people residing within the town
of Mandalay were required to dis-arm themselves, and surrender all
their weapons to the British, failing which they would be dealt with as
political offenders. Myowun Maung Pai-Si of Mandalay was specially
appointed to assist the Government in disarming the people.
On this Khin Maung Lat, who was one of King Thi Baw's stewards
and son-in-law of Shwe-hlan-Myowun, and grandson of the Taing-da
Mingyi, persuaded the people of the town to defy the declaration. He
succeeded to a large extent in doing so, and after collecting some men
he went to one of King Min Don's sons, then a wearer of the yellow
robe, at a monastery at Mandalay. This son of King Min Don owed his
life to the "Thathana-baing" who rescued him from the death sentence
of King Thi Baw at his accession to the throne. When King Thi Baw

murderers were the men of the Burmese Prince at Zibingyi, and sent

They returned to Mandalay winners of a battle, but not totally driving
the rebels from their stockade at Zibingyi.
About this time the Taing--da Mingyi, who was held to be in great
measure responsible for the earliest risings, besides other grounds, was
deported to Cuttack.
After a time a party of servants of the Bombay Burma Timber
Trading Company were assaulted by the Burmese Prince's men on their
journey from Mandalay to Kyaukse. The assault took place near
Myitnge. Two of the Company's´men, viz., Messrs. Calogreedy and
Walker, were killed on the spot, while another, Mr. Gray, was wounded
and taken prisoner to the Burmese Prince at Zibingyi. The rebels and
the dacoits had thus become overbearing, and Colonel Sladen taking
serious offence at this bloodshed, marched a battalion to Zibingyi,
employing the Shwe-hlan Myowun as a guide. They were as before
met by the rebels about half way at Nwa-lan Hill. The rebels were
unable to stand long against the British soldiers, and they dispersed
after some hours resistance.
On this the Burmese Prince and his men fled to Myin-zaing in the
Kyaukse District, and rallied there. Mr. Gray had by that time been
slain.
The rebellious nature of Khin Maung Lat frightened his father-in-
law the Shwe-hlan Myowun, who, fearing that he would be deported
like the Taing-da Mingyi, requested Colonel Sladen to cause him to be
sent to Akyab in Arakan before he received a sentence of deportation*
He was accordingly sent to Akyab.

CHAPTER III
PACIFICATION OF THE KYAUKSE DISTRICT
After he was dislodged from Zibingyi in Mandalay District, the
Burmese Prince and his rebels made a raid into the Kyaukse District
and encamped themselves at Myin-zaing. His defeat at Zibingyi
reduced his strength, but he made no hesitation in recouping the same
by an addition of a force of several hundreds of men who were left in
disorder in the Kyaukse District by the Shwe-hlan Myowun, while re-
turning from Pyinmana to Mandalay at the news of the capture of King
Thi Baw. The Prince, who was stronger than ever, made a determined
effort to renew his rebellion from Myin-zaing. Thenceforth he became
known by the name of "Myin-zaing Prince" for his occupation of
Myin-zaing village.
Myin-zaing was afterwards considered to be an unfavourable posi-
tion from which to check the British invasion from Mandalay. They
therefore made a move from Myin-zaing to the town of Kyaukse. From
Kyaukse the Prince sent out a detachment and posted it at the village
of Ta-yok-su in Singaing Township, where they built a fort to resist the
English column marching from Mandalay.
The rebels, as has been said, were extended in magnitude day by
day, and the rising that was once trifling enough had now called for a
more efficient and careful blow from the conquerors. The country was
in a most disorderly state, and the dacoit leaders in the countries about
Kyaukse and Meiktila were rising in arms in conjunction with the
Prince. Moreover the English had but a scanty knowledge of the
country infested with so many rebels. It was therefore found necessary
to employ influential and trustworthy guides to help them in suppress-
ing the rebellion. The Kin-wun Mingyi was asked by the British to
find men who had influence over the country, and who were loyal to the
British for employment as assistants in the suppression of the rebellion.
The Kin-wun Mingyi could hardly fulfill this, most of the people then
believing that the Myin-zaing Prince would in the end succeed in his
attempt to regain the monarchy.
The Burmese Cavalry Officers, however, were most loyal to their
new rulers. The late Cavalry Minister (Myin-wun) U Pon-Naka, the
Hluttaw" Secretary U Mye, the Yatana-Theinga Myin-sa-ye Maung

assumed himself a ruler and gave situations to several of his "Bohs."

advent. Boh Khaing was placed at In-Yaung. Boh Myat Hmon, alias
Kyaukse-wun, was likewise given a situation at Kume. The Shwe-dah

Prince. Khin Maung Lat, the Prince's favourite and the originator of
the rebellion, was employed by the Prince as his counsel. The late
Maing-lon Myook Boh Mya Gyi was appointed a Mingyi-Thet-taw-she,
(Prime Minister), by the Prince, and was deputed to tour round these
Districts to sow the seeds of rebellion by means of seditious oratory.
The rebel Prince having thus decorated the dacoit leaders with
appointments and forged titles, set them up against the British and
posted them at different quarters of the country, each at the head of
several hundreds of fighting men, all of them covering a large area
south of Kyaukse.

The Sitke U Pon-Naka, who was attached to the Kyaukse District
by the British authorities, learnt that the Myin-zaing Prince's "Bohs,"
Boh Khaing and Boh Myat Hmon, were girding up their loins for an
attack upon Kyaukse, and he prevailed upon his foreign masters to nip
the rising in the bud. Whereupon the English Military Officer at
Kyaukse marched some of his men at night to Ywa-kaing-gyi where the
Prince was residing. Maung No, now a Myook, was employed as a
guide on this occasion. The British wished to approach Ywa-kaing-gyi
unexpectedly, so Maung No had to lead them by the most uncommon
ways through jungles so as to avoid a conflict with the rebels on their
way. They arrived at Ywa-kaing-gyi without meeting the rebels and
made a sudden attack upon the Prince. The Prince was unable to offer
an effectual resistance as he was taken unawares, and accordingly he
fled to the Shan States, and took refuge at Ywa-Ngan under the
Sawbwa.
At this Boh Chin and Boh Khaing joined forces with Boh Myat
Hmon at Kume, and continued to set up a rising there.
Meanwhile a Deputy Commissioner arrived at Kyaukse from Man-
dalay with fresh troops to suppress the insurrections in combination
with the Military Officer posted there previously. The newly arrived
troops were marched from Kyaukse to fight against the rebels occupy-
ing Kume. They halted at Kon-ywa, and there they made preparations
to start an assault upon the rebels.
Boh Chin got himself ready for defence and hid his men within the
masonry ramparts of the Shwe-bon-tha monastery. Boh Khaing and
Boh Myat Hmon made a stand at a group of pagodas near the site
where Kume bazaar now stands.
On the approach of the English troops Boh Myat Hmon discharged
a bomb shell at the right wing of the British file and wounded a loaded
elephant. At this signal the English started their attack and sent forth
volleys of musketry. The rebels were unable to withstand this and left
their place of defence within a few minutes.
An attack was then made on Boh Chin on the right. Boh Chin's
situation was very secure, the masonry ramparts of the monastery ser-
ving him as a good shelter. So, the English were obliged to halt at a
distance, and storm the fort with bombardments. This, however, did
not have the desired effect upon the rebels who were quite secure and
safe within the ramparts. The only alternative now left to the British
soldiers was to rush into the compound and try conclusions by means
of a close hand-to-hand fight. No sooner thought than done, and the
rebels were forced to quit the place after a few hours struggle.

CHAPTER IV
PACIFICATION OF THE MOGOK AND WUNTHO
DISTRICTS
The British column that was bound for Mogok by a steamer up the
Irrawaddy reached Tha-beik-kyin unmolested. About half way from
Tha-beik-kyin to Mogok, on reaching a defile, they were attacked by a
combined force of the Chiefs of Kathè, Kyat-pyin and Mogok. After a
conflict of short duration, the Chiefs gave way to the English soldiers
and fled into the jungles. The column marched on and occupied Mogok
without much trouble.
The Sawbwa of Wuntho, on hearing of the success of the British,
placed a number of his fighting men on the borders of his country to
check the British entry into his jurisdiction. This disloyal show of the
Sawbwa reached the English ear and it was decided to nip him in the
bud.
Accordingly they crossed over to the Sawbwa's country and
stormed the town of Wuntho. The Sawbwa got terrified at the magni-
ficence of the English troops and took flight into the jungles without
offering any resistance. He made, however, several attempts after-
wards to reoccupy his town, but without success.
The State of Wuntho was at last pacified by a declaration dismiss-
ing the disloyal Sawbwa, who then crossed over to China. Thenceforth
the inhabitants of the tract left their Sawbwa and submitted to the
British, and a permanent British Government was established at Mogok
and Wuntho.

CHAPTER V
PACIFICATION OF THE BHAMO DISTRICT
The detachment that was sent out from Mandalay to Bhamo under
the command of a Deputy Commissioner, reached the town unopposed,
and Bhamo was taken without having had to face any resistance. They
had, however, to face some troubles later in settling the affairs in con-
nection with the Kachin country that formed part of the District. But
in the end the Kachin Chief surrendered, and ceded his country to the
British. Besides this, there were the inevitable troubles of dacoits and
robbers, but these also died out gradually, and after a few years'
administration, the District of Bhamo was completely restored to peace.
CHAPTER VI
PACIFICATION OF THE SHWEBO DISTRICT
Maung Tun, one of those Cavalry officials who submitted to the
British at Mandalay at the earliest times, took a leading part in pacify-
ing the District of Shwebo. He was a Myin-sa-ye of the Yatana-
Theinga Cavalry (Shwebo Cavalry), and had much influence over the
people there. It is most surprising to note that there was no rising of
a serious nature in annexing Shwebo, a District that was notoriously
traditional for the harbour of Minlaungs in times past. The only trou-
bles that had to be met by the British were those of the dacoits and
robbers. They gradually ceased to exist. Its pacification may be said
to be at the credit of the Yatana-Theinga Myin-sa-ye Maung Tun. He
was appointed a Myook at Shwebo.
CHAPTER VII
PACIFICATION OF THE MINBU AND THE MAGWE
DISTRICTS
In this part of the country the dacoit leader, Boh Shwè, was the
kindler of rebellion. He assumed himself a ruler over the people there,
and went so far as to make use of guilded umbrellas, an emblem which
was bestowed only upon officials of high rank.

CHAPTER VIII
PACIFICATION OF THE YAMETHIN DISTRICT
Just before the capture of Mandalay, the English column that in-
vaded Upper Burma through Toungoo had gained ground up to a fron-
tier post called Kyauk-taing, south of Pyinmana. From there they pro-
ceeded to Mibayagon, where they were met by the Shan Myin-sa-ye's
troops. After a few hours resistance the Burmese made a retreat and
Mibayagon was taken by the British. Thereupon the Shan Myin-sa-ye
was executed by the order of King Thi Baw for having given up the
battle.
Meanwhile, Burmese reinforcements arrived from Mandalay under
the command of the Shwe-hlan Myowun. The fresh troops stopped at
Pyinmana and prepared to resist the British invasion. Subsequently King
Thi Baw's son-in-law, Let-thon-daw-Gaing Khin Lat, came to Pyinmana
at the head of another force. But with his arrival the news of the
capture of King Thi Baw reached them. So they retraced their steps
without meeting the invaders. The English troops therefore were left
at Kyauk-taing unmolested for some time. It was here that the Com-
mander of that column was said to have chained the Thit-kyeik-wun
who came to him to surrender with his followers. The incident, as has
been noted, was very unfortunate. Most of the Burmese officials of the
surrounding Districts who had been patiently watching the British
policy, and who had but two alternatives, viz., whether to surrender to
the British and assist them in governing the country, or to flee from

MAUNG TUN E AND HIS FAMILY
The history of services of Maung Tun E's family dates as far back
as the year four hundred and one, Burmese era, (1039 A.D.), when two
of his forefathers, Nga Mo Swe, (from his mother's side), and Nga Ta,
(from his father's side), were appointed by King Anawrahta of Pagan as
Myothugyis of Meiktila and Ta-byaw, which then became administered
tracts. In the year 401, B.E., King Anawrahta Saw of Pagan left his
city with a magnificent army to tour round the country in quest of ancient
buildings and constructions for repair. He went to Nyaung-shwe in the
Southern Shan States and built the Baw-that Pagoda at Bin-dein. On his
way back he touched at Hlaing-det and inquired of the people the site of
the Mok-htaw Pagoda which was one of the eighty four thousand pagodas
erected by Asoka the Great of Pataliput. He learnt from the inhabitants
that the pagoda in question was then in a ruinous state near the place where
the village of Ywa-gyi now stands, 3 miles north west of Thazi. He
proceeded to the spot and rebuilt the pagoda, and changed its name from
Mok-htaw to Shwe-yin-hmyaw.
He went on his journey and on coming to the spot where the village
of Kon-daung now stands, he took a rest for some time. At that time,
Maung Tun E's forefathers Nga Mo We and Nga Ta were hunters seeking
for their game in the vicinity of the Meiktila Lake. On hearing of the
arrival of King Anawrahta Saw at the place above quoted, the two com-
panion hunters proceeded to the King and presented him with some of their
trophies. King Anawrahta Saw in return appointed Mg Mo Swe a Myo-
thugyi at Meiktila and Mg Ta a Myothugyi at Ta-byaw, a town which was
called after him. King Anawrahta Saw included their names in the list
of his representatives who formed the machinery of the Government, a
record which was then called an "Ah-ya-dan Sa-yin" and was preserved
in the palace. Since then the names of Tun E's fore-fathers continued to
appear in the "ah-ya-dan sa-vin" without a break.

Don at Amarapura. Prince Min Don was a younger brother of Pagan

reasons of his disloyal disposition towards his brother the King. Mg Gwe
was charged with the task of evicting this disposition by admonishing the

At the fall of Mandalay, when the British proclaimed their sovereignty

HIS FIRST YOUNGER BROTHER SHWE-DAH BOH MG TUN YE
When Mg Tun E's first younger brother Maung Tun Ye came of age,
King Min Don called him to the palace and appointed him a "Shwe-dah
Boh" in token of his regard for the deceased Wungyi Maung Gwe. Mg
Tun Ye was employed in the palace as one of the body-guards of King
Min Don.
On King Thi Baw's ascession to the throne, Maung Tun Ye as an
interior Shwe-sah Boh was one of those who were given orders by King
Thi Baw to kill the male family of the late King Min Don, who were all
innocent. Maung Tun Ye felt repugnant with the brutal and unjust ways
of the despotic King, and resigned his appointment not wishing to serve
under King Thi Baw.
During the British annexation days, he rendered risky services to the
British Crown in company with his brother Mg Tun E and other brothers.
He was appointed a Myook by the British Government and was posted at
Hlaing-det on the Shan States border. He died from the effects of hunting
after the dacoits and rebels through the malarial tracts of the Shan States,
leaving one of his sons Mg Thee Hla who is now a clerk in the Deputy
Commissioner's Office, Meiktila.
HIS SECOND BROTHER PAÑATTAW MG THA DUN
On being made known that Mg Tha Dun was a grand-child of Maung
Gwe, King Min Don appointed him a "Panattaw," and he was made a
close attendant of the King. At the time of King Thi Baw Maung Tha Dun
was appointed an Akauk-ok at Mandalay. For his excellent services during
the British annexation days Mg Tha Dun became a Myook under the
British Government. After a full term of service, he retired a pensioner
without having any chance of being given a step in promotion. A few
years after his retirement, he passed away leaving one of his sons,
Mg Thaw Na, who is now a Sub-Inspector of Police.

HIS FOURTH AND THE YOUNGEST BROTHER
MY.INZI MG THA AUNG
Maung Tha Aung ended his monastic career after passing two
Patamabyan examinations by the lower grade. He first served under
Government as a Bye-daik Saye under Pin Atwin-wun in King Min Don's
time. During the reign of King Thi Baw he became a Myinzi of Kandaung
circle in the Shwe-pyi-yan-aung Ashe-let Cavalry. He was a recipient of
the title of Ne-Myo Theik-di. During the war between King Thi Baw and
the English; he was sent out at the head of a cavalry that was one thousand
horse strong, to defend the country against British invasion via Toungoo.
But on reaching half-way, the news of the surrender of King Thi Baw
reached him, and not wishing to rebel against the new Government, he
marched back his cavalry to Wundwin and proceeded to Mandalay with
his brother Mg Tun E and others to profess loyalty to the new Government
before Colonel Sladen. Colonel Sladen gave the brothers a letter of intro-
duction to the British column stopping at Myingyan, where they went and
brought Mr. Scott and his troops to Meiktila. After a long and strenuous
fight against the rebels and dacoits in co-operation with Maung Tun E and
his other brothers, Maung Tha Aung succeded his brother, Maung Tun E,
on the latter's death. He was a Myook and showed a marked influence
over the people he governed. He was rewarded with a Certificate of Honour
for his ability in confiscating one thousand two hundred guns from the
people of the Wundwin Township alone. He was later transferred from
Wundwin to Meiktila where he earned the title of A.T.M. He was
notoriously influential over his people, and his days are still remembered by
the country folk. He died in January, 1919, leaving his only son
Mg Maung who is now a teacher in the Government Anglo-Vernacular
School, Mawlaik.

was the greatest the British had to face in taking Upper Burma.
THE AHYADIN-SAYIN
It is a record of services of a family which came into existence during
the reign of King Anawrahta Saw of Pagan, in the 4th century Burmese
era. This record was preserved in the palace and kept up by the successive
kings. It remained to be called an Ahyadan-Sayin till the downfall of the
first Pagan monarchy during the reign of King Thi-ha-thu, when the great
Chinese invasion took place.
After the Chinese invasion, the Shans came into Burma and ruled over
the country temporarily with their capital at Pin-ya-myo. The Ahyadan-
Sayin was destroyed by the Chinese invaders, but it received the attention
of the then Shan King who caused it to be re-written from the very begin-
ning, after proper enquiries. Since then, the Ahyadan-sayin became to be
called a Sittan-sayin" for reason of its having had to be re-compiled after
enquiries.

This second compilation continued to exist until the fall of the city of
