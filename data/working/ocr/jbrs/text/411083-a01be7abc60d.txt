Introduction
Sometime in 1956, Sithu U Kaung, Chairman of the Burma
Historical Commission brought to the Geology Department, University
of Rangoon two pieces of sandstone rock, being chips of some of the
megaliths from the archæological site of the ancient city of Sri-Ksetra
(modern Hmawza area). U Kaung requested Professor Tha Hla to
visit the site and determine the petrological identity and geological
provenance of the megaliths. Owing to various commitments the trip
could not be undertaken immediately. In the meantime, U Kaung died
in the service of his country. The trip was undertaken in January 1958
over a year after the request was made.
Dr. Tha Hla and Dr. Nyi Nyi, palæontologist of the Geology
Department went to Prome on January 19th and returned to Rangoon
on the 24th. They spent four days in the field at Hmawza, Prome and
Padaung which lies some 12 miles to the SSW of Prome on the
western bank of the Irrawaddy. Before their departure for Prome,
they were briefed by Professor G. H. Luce of the Burma Historical
Commission who has undertaken the important task of writing the
early history of Burma. Professor Luce gave a detailed list of the
more important megaliths to be visited at Hmawza area with their
precise geographical field sitings.
The primary object of the excursion was (i) to settle the petrologi
cal identity of the megaliths, (ii) to determine whether the stones were
of Burmese origin, and (iii) to pin-point, if possible, the actual ancient
quarry sites from which the stones were extracted, should they happen
to be local stones.

Lithologically all the urns are made of marine sandstone. Indeed,
all the megaliths in the Thein are found to be made of the same or
similar sandstone. Superficial colours apart, the fresh unweathered
surface is greyish green. The green tinge is due to the presence of
mineral glauconite among the grains of the stone. The colour of
weathered glauconite grains is greenish while that of the unweathered
ones is black. At least fifty per cent of the glauconite grains are found
to be wethered in varying degrees. From a visual estimate under the
microscope, some twenty per cent of the grains is glauconite and the
remainder is quartz. The presence of mineral glauconite in the sand-
stone sees the marine environment 3 under which the sandstone was
formed. The cementing material is calcite (lime). Hence the urns, as
well as the other megaliths, effervesce vigorously with dilute hydro-
chloric acid. The texture, in particular the size of the grains, varies
from megalith to megalith. Some are coarser-grained than others.

Stone Frame, 20 Yards west of Sin-ma ko wun din (VI)
(chips collected)
Some twenty yards west of the Sin-ma-ko-wun-din mound, there
is a large stone frame, rectangular in shape. More than half is buried;
only a portion projects out in the air. The rock is again identifiable
with (I), (II), (III) and (V). Visual examination with the aid of a
pocket lens and application of dilute hydrochloric acid show that it is a
calcareous glauconitic sandstone.
However the stone is much more weathered than any previously
examined. Much of the glauconite grains have been destroyed by
chemical action. Hence the colour of the stone is no longer greenish.
The iron released from the breakdown of glauconite and also biotite,
must have been thoroughly hydrated. Hence the rust is not hematitic,
but limonitic. The colour of the rock is therefore buff or yellowish
brown, which is the colour of limonite. The rock is now soft and
friable. Despite this modification in colour and hardness by weathering
the original texture of the calcareous glauconitic sandstone is still
faithfully retained.
Stone basins from Peikthano Myo (VII)
(chips collected)
Inside Peikthano Myo, and close to the eastern limit of the Myo,
are found by the road side on the left (as one traverses from north to
south) two large stone alms-bowls' in fairly good, recognisable condi-

Rocks of the Ridge, Eastern Portion
The day was spent in studying the geology of the area between the
modern city of Prome and the old city. The party started the traverse
in the morning from the new Chinese cemetery, about a mile south-east
of the Central Railway Station, which was visited during the previous
day. To reach this new Chinese cemetery from town, one has to pass
through a series of cemeteries belonging to the various religious com-
munities-old Chinese cemetery, Christain, Muslim and Hindu cemeteries,
flanked on the right by Buddhist religious lands. From that point, the
route was more or less eastwards to Kyandawgon village. Leaving
the village, the route turned generally in a south-easterly direction.
till the party reached the Padagyi Pagoda. From the Padagyi, the party
traversed a distance of nearly 3 furlongs in the N.N. W. direction to
reach the Padagale Pagoda. From the Padagale, the party zigzagged
its way north-eastwards and entered the old city through the
Shethingan Gate and reached Kinmunchon village which is situated.
immediately inside the western wall of the old city.
The distance covered was roughly four and a half miles, allowing
for the zigzags. The country is hilly. The vegetation is indaing forest.
The permanent inhabitants are Buddhist religious recluses, the hill-tops
being occupied by monasteries and little pagodas. As a Buddhist
meditation centre in the country, this region is only second in importance
after the famous Sagaing Gyaung which is in Upper Burma.
The entire country rocks are soft yellow sands and sand rocks
(not sandstones) with pebble horizons. Occasionally the sand rocks
contain iron nodules. Silicified fossil wood, Dipterocarpoxylon
burmense Holden, is plentiful, being embedded in the sandy matrix.
The rock formation belongs to the Irrawaddian Series (Pliocene age)
which are fluviatile in origin. The outcrop of these Irrawaddian rocks
end just about where the western wall of the old city appears. Inside
the city the country rock is largely recent alluvium.
The above traverse is essentially an east-west one between modern
Prome and the western wall of the old city at Kinmunchon village.
From Kinmunchon (also known in its eastern extension as Kanbaungzu)
the party hiked southwards a distance of another 4 miles and visited
the Myinbahu Pagoda during the afternoon. The initial five furlongs
of the route after leaving the village lies entirely on the alluvium within

Rock from Okshitkon Hill - (A)
Coming down from Prome towards Shwedaung along the motor
road, the village of Ma-u Ywa was reached at about 3 miles due south
of Prome. The village is on the east side of the motor road. After
walking some 3 furlongs due east of the village across the ‘yah' and
paddy fields of flat land, the Okshitkon hill was reached. This hill forms
part of the western slope of the ridge (hills immediately to the west of
the old city), the topography is a little more rugged. The hills are
formed of hard bands of massive sandstones alternated with thick beds
of shale. Debris of hard sandstones are collected by the local people
for use as road metal for the Prome-Shwedaung motor trunk road.
Three specimens of sandstones were collected (labelled A 1, A 2, Aº3,
in the Department). The beds dip generally towards the N. E. and are
conformable with those (Akauktaung Stage) of the Prome foreshore.
In general lithology and texture, these rocks are identifiable with the
megalithic stones just as the Akauktaung Stage rocks are.

Several specimens were collected from the same bed and labelled B
in the Department. Lithologically the rock is identifiable with the
sandstones of the Upper Prome Series, the sandstones of the Prome

grained glauconitic sandstone. The fresh portion has the usual greenish
grey colour. The usual iron brown peripheral zone develops due to
hydration of the exposed surface.

At Sinmagyat Anauk village (E 502793, 85 N/1), which is on
Stuart's "Lower Prome Series", road-workers gave the authors two
large pieces of highly fossiliferous sandstone, stated to have been
quarried in the neighbourhood for road metal. The specimens are full
of molluscan shells of which Ostrea latirmarginata, Cytherea erycina,
Chlamys Trisidos (Parallelipipedum) can be recognised. The fauna is
typical of "Upper Prome Sandstones". These calcareous shelly
remains are embedded in greenish or bluish grey sand matrix.
The interesting fact about this find is that these stones match very
well with the fragments of the third stone basin from Peikthano Myo
(VII) described on p. 7. The fossils in the stone basin (or bowl)
fragments are almost identical with those present in these specimens
(C). Also the lithology is similar.
Conclusions
As a result of this field investigation, the following conclusions are
reached:
(i) The megaliths examined in the Sri-Ksetra area were
fashioned out of calcareous glauconitic sandstones of
marine origin. The texture is on the whole uniform and
fine grain-sized. No igneous rock is detected in the
megaliths examined.
(ii) The source of the stones
the stones for these megaliths are
undoubtedly local. The stones must have been drawn
either from (a) some horizon in the "Akauktaung
Stage" rocks exposed in the Prome city foreshore, or from
(b) the "Upper Prome Stage" rocks exposed along the
western escarpment of the Prome ridge between Mig-
yaung-ye (E 500832 - 85 N/1) in the north and Ma-u ywa
(E 505805 85 N/1) and its neighbourhood in the south,
or from (c) the "Lower Prome Series". rocks exposed
around the neighbourhood of Sinmagyat Anauk village
(E 502793-85 N/1).
