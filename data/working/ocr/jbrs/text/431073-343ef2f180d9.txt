by
Nguyen Dinh Hoa, B.A., M.A., Ph. D.
Department of Linguistics, Faculty of Letters, University of Saigon
(Republic of Vietnam)
A Vietnam Press dispatch of 19 September 1959, stated that during the past five years the
University of Saigon enrollment had gone up 218.5 per cent. For the same period that
institution has conferred 370 licentiates in law, 34 Advanced Certificates in law, 103 M. D.
degrees, 183 Diplomas in Pharmacy, 9 degrees in Surgery, 1,219 Certificates in Science, 37
licentiates in Science, 4 Advanced Diplomas in Science, 741 Certificates in Letters, 31
licentiates in Letters, 341 teacher's licenses and 5 Diplomas in Architecture.
Behind these figures are the stories of the development, struggle and victory of the
University of Saigon and those of its sister institutions.
The Republic of the Vietnam at present has three universities, located in Saigon, Hue
and Dalat * The first two are government supported and controlled while the third one
is under private auspices.
As the oldest institution of higher learning on the land the University of Saigon seems
to set the pattern of organization for its sister institutions to follow, although the latter
conceived under different circumstances will likely evolve in their own fashion.
1. The University of Saigon
While the two younger universities at Hue and Dalat were established only recently-
1957 and 1958 respectively - the institution which in now called the University of Saigon
actually has had various other names, and its history goes back not just to 1955, but to the
year 1917.

B
Present Organization and Administration
The University of Saigon is headed by a Rector assisted by a University Council
where all the following colleges or faculties are represented: Faculty of Law, Faculty of
Medicine and Pharmacy, Faculty of Science, Faculty of Letters, Faculty of Pedagogy and
Higher School of Architecture. With the exception of the Faculty of Letters, all appella-
tion are self-explanatory. In the Faculty of Letters "College of Arts" may be a more
precise name the subjects taught are Vietnamese, French, English and Chinese languages
and literatures, history, geography, philosophy, and psychology.
-
Each of those colleges or faculties is headed by a Dean, assisted by a Faculty Council,
an administrative staff and a teaching staff. Each of them has its own library, too. Unlike
their sister colleges in the United States, Vietnamese colleges are scattered around the city
instead of being grouped on a unified campus.
The present Rector was appointed by a presidential decree, and the deans are all
appointed by the Secretary of State for National Education, with the exception of the.
Faculty of Medincine.
Graduates of secondary schools, that is to say, holders of the baccalaureate (obtained
at the end of their 7-year high school career) are admitted to attend university courses.
There are no entrance examinations, since the requirement about the baccalaureate (tu tai)
is considered a sufficient guarantee that the entering student has had the necessary background
either in liberal arts or in sciences. Freshmen at the Faculty of Pedagogy, however, are
selected by means of competitive examinations, since throughout their three years of study
the students receive a government scholarship of 1,500 piasters a month, with the obligation
to servein public schools upon their graduation.
Normally, except for Medicine, Pharmacy and Architecture, it takes a student from three
to four years to obtain his licentiate (cu nhan), and the doctorate (tien si) requires approxi-
mately an additional four years.

Graduatuates of the Faculty of Law can become lawyers, magistrates, public adminisra-
tors or economists. The undergraduate program to the cu-nhan degree * includes courses
in Civil Law, Constitutional Law, Economics, Vietnamese Civil Law and History of Vietna-
mese Law. Post-licentiate studies lead to the Doctorate in, Private Law, Public Law and
Economic Sciences.
The Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy comprises actually three departments-Medicine,
Pharmacy and Dentistry. The Department of Medicine requires a pre-medical program,
under which, during the first year, the students must take courses in Physics, Chemistry and.
Biology ("P.C.B.") at the Faculty of Science. After passing this first year's examinations,
they go on to six years of medical education before they are allowed to submit their M. D.
thesis. The program in pharmacy lasts five years. The first of these five years is spent in
apprenticeship in a recognized pharmacy, and the following four years are devoted to course
work. The subjects taught are Botany, Chemistry (analytic, biologic, mineral and organic),
Cryptogamy, Hydrology, Geology, Hygiene, Drug Legislation, Mineralogy, Pharmaco--Dy-
namics, Microbiology, Toxicology and Zoology. The Department of Dental Surgery offers
courses spread over a period of five years and leading to the degree of D.D.S.
The Curriculum at the Eaculty of Science is varied and highly technical. Besides the
Physics, Chemistry, Biology (“P.C.B.”) program for pre-medical students and the two fresh-
man programs of Physics, Chemistry and Natural Sciences ("S.P.C.N."), and Mathematics,
Physics and Chemistry ("M.P.C.") for Science majors, there are courses in General Mathe-
matics, Rational Mechanics, Differential and Integral Calculus, General Physiology, General
Physics, General Chemistry, General Botany, Geology, etc.
In order to qualify for the licentiate (cu nhan) in Mathematics, Physical or Natural
Sciences, a student must obtain four appropriate certificates. Post-graduate studies may
lead to either the Diploma in advanced studies (after one year) or the Doctorate in Science
(after three or four years). The doctoral program can be in Mathematics, Physics or Natu-
ral Sciences.

tradition of the “cours magistraux", most university teachers deliver formal lectures, during
which the students try to take notes as copiously as possible. This habit undoubtedly
prevails in such colleges as the Law School or the Faculty of Letters, where theoretical
problems in law and jurisprudence, or strtements on, say, Vietnamese, French or English
literature have to be given to the students at dictation speed and in not unusual cases over
a microphone. In science classes as well as language classes, however, emphasis is laid on
the practical side: laboratory facilities acquired through foreign aid agencies are, being
extended to more students every day, and a class in practical English is as lively as in any
progressive, linguistically-oriented class in an American university.
Students used to sign up for a course (in law or in letters), pay their enrollment fees,
then stay home until the very end of the academic year, when they present themselves for
the examination.
If this final examination is based solely upon lecture notes and not upon extensive
outside readings, it certainly fails to give an accurate measurement of a student's potentiali-
ties.
Recognizing this, an increasing number of instructors are paying more attention to
the student's attendance record and his performance during the year (in various tests,
quizzes, book reports, class discussions - all innovations to the system), and final examina-
tions are no longer dreadful experiences - or ordeals upon which hang the whole future of
a young man.
The place of research in the overall university program at Saigon is becoming more
and more important as the institution gets older and thus has to provide to brighter stu-
dents opportunities for advanced work in their own fields of specialization.
E-Student Body
Behind the impressive enrollment figures (for instance, 2,060 in the Law School in
1959-60 as compared with 1,674 in 1958-9) lies the very problem of “outlets" for univer-
sity graduates. The problem of how to absorb them into various branches of national
activity has to be examined in the light of the high school population for the coming years
as well as the potentialities of industry, business, civil service, etc. in Vietnam. Planning is
imperative, if graduates of Law schools and other schoools are not to become so numerous
as to constitute a social threat as in India or Burma.
Selection among holders of the baccalaureate might be the answer, but people may
then denounce this solution as contrary to the university tradition whereby admission is
automatic upon graduation from a secondary school. At any rate the percentage (a little
more than 0.07) reveals excessive selectivity.

2. The University of Hue
A presidential decree of 1 March 1957, created the University of Hue, whose rector is
a well-known educator, the Reverend Cao Van Luan. The Government of the Republic of
Vietnam desired at that time to establish a centre of learning and culture at the very beauti-
ful former capital city of imperial Vietnam, close to the present demarcation line between the
Communist North and free South. But apart from the political impact of providing a show-
case for what dynamic, humanistic democracy can do the new institution, now two years old,
answers a true need for higher education in Central Vietnam. The Rector has succeeded in
attracting Vietnamese teachers from abroad - mainly France and the United States - while a
number of instructors from the University of Saigon also travel up to Hue once a month or
so to teach on a part-time basis.
When the University was officially opened in the fall of 1957, with four faculties-Peda-
gogy, Letters, Law and Sciences-it had an enrollment of 250 students, who enjoyed a modi-
cum of facilities including classroom building and dormitories scattered around the
city of Hue.
Being merely a beginning it gave courses only to a frashman class, who signed up also
for fine arts, midwifery and health and sanitation techniques.
Offering during the academic year 1958-9 included various certificates (Vietnemese li-
terature, British Civilization, American Civilization, Poilosophy, Mechanics, Physics, Chemisty,
etc.) open to sophomores. The student body in 1958-9 numbered 743, broken down as
follows: Pedagogy 182, Letters 173, Law 205, and Sciences 183. The fast-growing faculty
now has about thirty-seven full time instructors.
Unlike the University of Saigon, Hue University is using Vietnamese at the medium of
instruction in all its faculties, with the exception of course of foreign language courses.

The young university has undoubtedly great potentialities. The average age of faculty
members in Hue is lower than that of the instructors at the University of Saigon. The 10,000
volume library and the three laboratories (for Biology, Physics and Chemistry) have been star-
ted on a grand scale, with the assistance of the International Rescue Committee, the Asia
Foundation, the American Friends of Vietnam, etc. An economic research program is being
planned, and an institute of Sinology was set up in November 1959. Anticipation the 1,000-
mark in enrollment figures, the university publishes a review of high quality called Dai - hoc,
which prints articles by the teaching staffs of both Saigon and Hue universities.
In accordance with the twofold educational objective of the Republic, the new univer-
sity concentrates on further developing Vietnam's traditional values and at the same time
welcoming ideas and concepts from abroad. A faculty delegation was sent to Europe last
summer to arrange for photocopies of ancient documents presently held by the Librairie
Nationale in Paris and the Vatican Library in Rome. The valuable documents, written
either in Chinese or in nom characters, will be transcribed into quoc-ngu, the Romanized
script, thus enabling scholars to carry on reserch on early Vietnamese culture.
3. The University of Dalat
In 1958, the first private university in Vietnam was established in Dalat, the mountain
resort located approximately two hundred miles from the capital city. It is a Catholic-
sponsored university to be supported solely by private endowment. Started at the second-
ary level with most of the teachers coming from the French-run Lycee Yersin, it has since
expanded and added Letters to a predominantly Science curriculum. Under the able
leadership of its Rector Father Tran Van Thien, the building university situated in an ideal
site is gradually gaining its position as a respectable seat of learning maintaining high
standards. The language of instruction has been French in most sections, but in the
Teachers College the program of studies which eventually will be given in Vietnamese is
being implemented in coordination with the university of Saigon, Faculty of Pedagogy,
thus sharing the experience of organization and administration with an older teacher train-
ing college.
The 400-odd students enroll this year in Sciences, Letters or Pedagogy. Latin is given
emphasis in the Western classics program.
Publications
In addition to books and monographs as well as articles printed in various learned
journals, each faculty at the University of Saigon publishes its own bulletin or annals.
The Faculty of Medicine and Pharmacy publishes Acta Medica Vietnamica, The Faculty of
Science and the Faculty of Letters have their own Annals, and the Faculty of Law its
Review of Law and Economics, The regular publication of the University of Hue, Dai-hoc
(Higher Studies) appears six times during the academic year. Whereas the publications of
the University of Saigon are trilingual (Vietnamese, French and English), Dai-hoc contains
only articles in the Vietnamese language, its object being the “defense and illustration" of
the mother tongue. Hue University enjoys the obvious advantage of having its own press,

CONCLUSION
As the number of high school graduates keeps soaring every year (1099 at the end of
1954-5, it will be necessary to make plans to expand the physical plant of each college or
faculty in order to admit the knowledge-thirsty youth in their late teens.
Since the housing shortage is not so acute in Hue and Dalat as in overcrowded Saigon,
the number of dormitory facilities in the latter city cannot be too rapidly increased. Present
accommodations in Saigon are provided at two hostels one for male students and one for
female students which make up the Cité Universitaire where some 420 are housed. Student
lodging and living conditions are the constant concern of the University administration as well
as the National Union of University Student and the National Committee for World University
Service (W.U.S.). Although the rooms at the main University hostel are corwded, meals
provided at its mess hall are adequate and reasonably priced. The tuition fees are only
nominal, and most students can easily find part-time employment in town. The students
at the Faculty of Pedagogy, who receive full scholarships, make up only about nine per cent
of the total enrollment, and a small number of worthy and needy students in other faculties
also obtain small scholarship awards.
Classroom space, equipment and textbooks in either of the three universities are by no
means plentiful, and plans are being carried out to build more classrooms, obtain more equip-
ment and produce more educational materials. All these projects can be easily achieved,
with the material help of friendly nations: the U.S. Operations Mission is contributing a
Medical Centre, complete with a 500-bed teaching hospital; the Colombo Plan is assuming
the cost of additional science laboratories, and the French Cultural Mission is donating books
and periodicals. Human resources are even more sorely needed, however, if institutions of
higher learning in Vietnam are to fullfill their noble mission of making enlightened and re-
sponsible citizens and shaping the future of the land in the most humane fashion possible.
