EMBRYOLOGY IN CONCRETE.
One of the problems of Anatomy Departments in tropical
lands is the fact that the completion of almost any construction
is the signal for a grand attack by either climate, earth-quakes,
white ants or creatures of the boring breed. Embryology models are
no exception to this rule and although the Zeigler series of models
in hardened wax satisfied most requirements I have now been
informed that as Professor Zeigler is dead these models are no
longer on the market. For this reason I have turned to ordinary
cement and sand as a possible medium as it is one of the few
materials that white ants reject as an article of diet. The other I
am told is kerosine oil-but this statement need not be taken too
seriously.
The idea started from nothing more inspiring than a drain in
the University Sanatorium. I was struck by the remarkable
smoothness of the surface of the work which I attributed to the
original use of very fine sand followed by years of heavy rain, such
as occurs in South Burma. After a prolonged search I found the
man who had done some of the original cement work at the
Sanatorium. He turned out to be a Punjabi living in the" wilds
of Kambe and grown old in the service of concrete. So assisted
by the Medical College Artist we evolved a method of making
embryology models.
MATERIALS AND EQUIPMENT.
,,
A board sloping at an angle of 30-40° is used for the work.
Rough paste-board on which to make the outline. An iron rod to
act as a central support. A small diamond shaped trowel-like
implement fixed on a 9" wooden handle. The " trowel ,, is
made of block tin, 1" long by 1" wide with a rod extension to fit
into the handle. Pieces of lath-wood of various shapes to assist
in the working up of the model. Hog-hair brushes or brushes of
strong fibre-actually we use the top of a coconut. These are used
for shaping the curves and making the work more even. A
quantity of cement of good quality and of fine sand. A few
pebbles for polishing, water, a cloth or two and a few towels. We
obtained our sand from the shores of the Irrawaddy, probably
mixed with the delta-mud of the years.
THE METHOD.

posterior measurement and about 3"-4" maximum thickness. A
piece of rough paste-board is fixed with drawing pins, to the

sloping work-board. A pencil outline of the model is then made

The iron rod is then placed vertically along the
the centre of the
outline below which it extends for a few inches so as to act as a
candle for the finished models. The upper end of the rod does
not extend above the head end of the model. A mixture of cement
and fine sand is kept ready. Good cement is required and the
proportions used are cement one, fine sand three. This
mixture is now applied layer by layer until
until the required
size and thickness is reached. The model is curved, shaped
and excavated with lath-sticks and the little trowel. The fibre
brush assists in getting the required roundness. Should the model
be not completed in one day, criss-cross lines are made on its
surface so that the next layer will adhere properly, when the
work is again started. For the surface layers a quantity of lime
(1-4) is mixed with cement and sand. Between periods of working,
the model should be covered with a damp cloth. After the model
is completed on the rough it should be sprinkled with cold water
and kept moist by partial emersion in a bath of cold water for two
or three days. This has a hardening effect. It is now ready for
polishing. This is effected by going over the whole work with a
smooth pebble until a smooth surface is obtained. It is then
carefully cleaned with a towel or cloth. The process of painting
requires little comment. Enamel paint is the best and the colour
known as stone grey is useful for whole embryos though different
workers will doubtless use the wide range of colours available for
different models and sections. The finished embryo is suitably
kept in a glass case with a sloping background of black in the
case of a stone-grey work.
