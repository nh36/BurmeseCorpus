A debtor who could not repay his debts, Yajna-

ditor. Manu Samhita provides that "if the debtor be
of the same caste as the creditor or belongs to a
caste inferior to that of the creditor, he shall pay
off the debt by bodily work." Brahmanical
influences helped to exempt Brahmins from the obli-
gation to work off the debt by personal labour.
The Dhammashastras treating of limitation of action
laid down twenty years as the period beyond which
immovable property would become irrecoverable by
the true owner out of possession and ten years in res-
pect of movables. But a debt did not become barred
till after the third generation on the debtor's side.
"
Earlier law givers of Burma appeared to have
borrowed extensively from these provisions in the
Hindu Dhammashastras, adapting them on occasions.
to different conditions obtaining in the country. Thus
the obligation to repay the debt due by a person
deceased was extended not only in the descending
line but also to the ascendants for two generations,
but such obligation was made to depend either on
the descendant or the ancestor having had the
benefit of the estate of the deceased or on such per-
son having made himself personally liable by some
form of suretyship for the deceased, either at the
time the debt was contracted or at any time there-
after. Thus, Wagaru Dhammathat has: Sons should
pay the debts incurred by their parents; but those
sons do not obtain inheritance
from their par-

parents." On the lapse of ten years from the con-

in dynasties. And when the debt had became due
for repayment, "whether the debt be a large or

The comparatively early introduction into this system
of law relating to debts of the principle that in certian
circumstances a debtor could obtain a discharge of his
liabilities without repayment of his debts is startling.
Manusara Shwemyin (cir. 1768 A.D.) which claimed to
be a recension of a Dhammathat of 1629 A. D. has a
reference to the law af discharge of debts other than by
repayment, the process being described as

1.74th. The law when the debts are swept away, or for
relief for insolvent debtors.
Oh excellent king! regarding the sweeping away
of the debts. If a person has incurred debts beyond
his means of paying, and his family are unable to
assist him, or if he has no family, he shall make a
petition to the king, (who will say,) on conditions
give him an advance, or the king may give him an
advance and let him go in a trading ship. In three
years the king may take back the advances. This he may
do in accordance with the laws of Theng' ha-wooto; but
if the debtor has not abilities to warrant the king acting
in this way, or if he has no strength, he may call the
creditors together, in the presence of rahans and
respectable men, and having caused the debtor to put
on white garments, make an invocation, and say that
he gives release from misforturne in this life to his
wife and children, and request his creditor to grant
him a release; and having poured over him clear water
from a bramin's shell, make rejoicing with music; this
is called "sweeping clean the level." If the debtor's
luck shall change and he shall after this become a
thatay, (wealthy man,) the creditors shall have no
claim against him, and even if he offer them their
duties; it is improper in them to take it; the matter
has become one in connection with a future state.
The monarch alone, and no one besides, bas the power
'to do this.”
"75th. The law for breaking up the collection of debts,
or surrendering to creditors.
Oh excellent king the breaking up of the collec-
tion of debts is this: One out of many creditors shall
not take the effects of a debtor at valuation, but the
creditors and the debtor shall come before the head
man of the village, or some man of wealth and res-
pectability, not being a debtor, and having got him to
buy the effects at a price fixed; the amount shall be
divided amongst the creditors according to the amount
of their claims. This is the custom of poor people,
and is called breaking up the collection of debts.
If after they have been thus broken up and divided,
the debtor shall, by a change of fortune, become a
man of known substance, the creditors shall have no
claim against him; let him be free. But if with a
view of preventing the debt against him being carried
on to the next state of existence, the debtor on becom-
ing such shall offer to pay the creditors, they may
receive all that he will give them."

To appreciate how revolutionary these provisions
were at the time of their first introduction into the
Burmese legal system in 1629 A. D., one has only to
recall that in England in 1705 A D., the Statute 4 An-
ne C. 17 for the first time enabled a bankrupt trad-
er if he surrendered all his property and otherwise
conformed to the requirements of bankruptcy law, to
obtain a certificate of conformity, which protected him
from arrest for his debts. But the statue provided
that if a bankrupt wilfully omitted to surrender him-
self to the Commissioners and to submit himself for ex-
amination or if after voluntarily surrendering himself
omitted to make a full discovery of his property and
to deliver it to the Commissioner, he should suffer as
"a felon without the benefit of the Clergy." The death
penalty thus provided was reduced only in 1825 by 6
Geo. 4. C. 16 to one of transportation for life or for a
term not less than 7 years or imprisonment for a term
not exceeding 7 years. It was, again, not ill 1838 that
the benefit of a certificate of conformity was extended
partially to non-traders; the certificate was a protec-
tion against arrest but the debtor's after-acquired pro-
perty remained liable for his debts. Till 1861 A.D. the bias
was on the punishment of fraudulent debtors, the relief
to insolvents having but a secondary place in the
scheme of bankruptcy legislation in England.
The Roman Law as a prototype for the rules enun-
ciated in the Dhammathats in relief of insolvent debtors
appreared at first sight possibilities. Portuguese nation-
als are known to have been settled in Burma prior to
1600 A. D. and it may be suggested that from these
settlers, jurists obtained some information of the Roman
law relating to debts. But a closer examination of the
Roman law shows that the rules relating to discharge of
insolvent debtors to be found in the Burmese Dhamma-
thats cannot be traced to Roman influences. The early
process of manus injectio by which the debtor addictus
was assigned to his creditor bound to work for the
latter till the debt was satisfied finds a parallel in the
early Burmese law but this parallel is clearly fortuitous.
The Rule of Hindu Law is also similar in this respect.

Bonorum Venditio did not exempt a debtor's after-
acquired property from being seized in satisfaction of
such portion of the debts as may remain unsatisfied.
Cessio bonorum merely protected the debtor from arrest
and did not effect a discharge.
Where, then, did Burmese jurists obtain the germs
of the principle, which by 1752 A. D. in the Manugye
had developed into the two provisions earlier reprodu-
ced? The text of section 74 of the Manugye, volume
three. appears to provide the solution in its reference
to (1) "Then'ha-wooto". The four "Theng'ha-wooto"
or more exactly "Sangahavattu" were according to
Buddhistic learning the duties of the ruler in his
dealings with his subjects. The king was exhorted, inter
alia, to provide such of his subjects as stood in need of
assistance, capital wherewith to earn a living; the advan-
ce thus made was not recoverable till three years had
elapsed; and no interest was to be charged on the sum
advanced.
To Burmese jurists, almost all of whom received
their education in religious institutions and not a few
of whom were members of the Buddhist religious order,
it was but a short step from such moral exhortations to
legal obligations binding on the ruler. Thus Dhamma-
vilasa has, "No interest is exigible on loans made by
the king to his subject." Manuyin, again, states, "The
king shall recover the loans he has made to subject by
such instalments as the means of the debtor permits."
Compare with this, a provision in the same dhammathat
where the dealing was between two subjects:" If a
citizen has taken a loan from another, the debtor shall
repay the loan by the date originally stipulated for the
repayment. It is not open to the debtor then to say,
"I cannot discharge the debt." Thus it was that the
King, when petitioned for assistance by a debtor unable
to pay his debts, if satisfied that granting the assistance
by way of a loan to the debtor would be ineffacious (or
as the Manugye has it "if the debtor has not abilities to
warrant the King acting in this way"), would call upon
the creditors to agree to the debtor being granted a
discharge of his liabilities.
