The total area of the Union of Burma is 266244
square miles or 170,396,000 acres. Of this the area of the
frontier States is 104375 square miles or 66,800,000 acres.
Hence the area of Burma proper is 161,869 square miles or
103,596,100 acres. Of the total area of Burma proper
40.4 million acres are suitable for agriculture, 199 million
acres are reserved forests and 43.2 million acres are not
suitable for agriculture. No records are available for
classifying the 66.8 million acres of the frontier States. Of
the 40.4 million acres suitable for agriculture in Burma
proper, 17.4 million acres are cultivated, 3.8 million acres
are left as fallow and the remaining 19.2 million acres are
not cultivated at all. Let us now consider the population

Therefore in Burma there is no pressure of population
on the land as there is in Japan, India or Ceylon. For
every person in Burma there is also about one acre of
reserved forests from which all the building materials and
firewood are obtainable. Besides, from the mouth of the
Naaf river in East Pakistan to Victoria Point there is a
long coast line of about 1200 miles which offers all the
natural advantages for deep sea fishing. There are
also big water sheds which could be harnessed for
the production of cheap electric energy. As for the
mineral resources of Burma, I think up to now only the
surface has been scratched. Therefore I am justified in
saying that the natural resources of Burma are immense.
If all the available manpower of Burma exert to its fullest
capacity to develop the natural resources mentioned above,
every eighth family of the two million families in Burma
should live in well built wooden houses, have an income of
K. 800.00 per month and also own a motor car.
There should be then at least 250,000 private motor
cars in this country.
As things are now, there are not more than 25,000
motor cars owned by private individuals at present. To
raise the standard of living of the citizens of the Union of
Burina there should be a well-balanced development of the
natural resources of Burma.

The majority of the people of Burma are consumers of
fish and ngapi. Therefore we should increase the produc-
tion of fish and also improve the quality of our ngapi. Let
me now consider the fish position at present. Before
World War II the average price of fresh fish in Rangoon
and other big towns in Burma was at most about fifty
pyas per viss or 3 pounds. In May 1945 when the
Anglo-Americans reoccupied Burma, the price of even
common fish such as Ngayant, Ngakhu and Ngabyima
rocketted upto about fifteen kyats per viss. Even today
we are still paying K. 4 to K. 5 per viss of fish inspite of
the coming into existence recently of fishermen's co-opera-
tive society. I consider that the only way of bringing
down the price of fish is to increase the output of not only
fresh water fish but also deep sea fish. To be able to
increase the output of fish our present method of catching
fish should be supplemented by an electrical method.
This method was first discovered by Professor Kreutzer
of the University of Freiburg in Germany a few years ago.
According to Dr Kreutzer and his collaborators, fish of
the usual commercial varieties reach the state of electro-
narcosis when subjected to voltages between head and
tail of, say, 1.5-5.0 volts and they will be killed if
subjected to greater voltages for a time which is long
enough for electrolysis of certain substances in the body
to take place. Further it has been found that fish are
responsive to the polarity of electric fields and when
free to move tend to arrange themselves so that the head
points in the direction of increasing potential.
Thus fish could be caught by placing two electrodes
in the water and putting a varying positive voltage on one
electrode. The positive voltage on this electrode (anode)
would cause the fish to point towards it. The varying
electric field along the spine of the fish would cause the
tail muscles to contract and relax, moving the tail and
propelling the fish into a net near the anode. In actual

Let me now consider the ngapi position at present.
There is no need for me to emphasize that rice and ngapi
are staple foodstuffs of the people of Burma.
Our poor
cultivators live almost entirely on them. Therefore it is
extremely desirable to improve the quality of ngapi which
is consumed daily throughout the length and breadth of
this country. The best way of doing this is by setting up
a ngapi research institute either at Mergui or Samanaing
village in the Delta. In this institute the proteins and
amino-acids of all kinds of fresh water fish and also all
kinds of deep-sea fish obtainable along the Burmese coast-
line should be studied. The best method of producing
palatable and wholesome Seinsa ngapi, Yegyo ngapi, Damin
ngapi and ngapigaung also from the right kind of fish
should be thoroughly investigated. The degradation pro-
ducts of the proteins in each kind of ngapi during storage
should be exhaustively studied. The most satisfactory
method of manufacturing nganpyaye or ngapi sauce from
ngapi also should be thoroughly investigated. In fact there
are more than a hundred and one problems on the chemistry
of ngapi which await solution. Therefore I would suggest
that the ngapi research institute should have sufficient
facilities to enable some of the post graduate chemists
from the University of Rangoon to pursue a course of
research leading to the degree of Master of Science or
Doctor of Philosophy of the University of Rangoon. I
look forward to the day when ngapi of the best quality
would be abtainable at a low price throughout the length
and breadth of this country.

I have mentioned a little earlier that our agricultural
products, forest products and minerals earn practically all
the foreign exchange which the country needs. Thus it is
extremely desirable that the Government departments
which supervise the production of these commodities should
be adequately staffed to increase the output further. Let
me now give a bird's eye view of the staff of these depart-
The Agricultural Department has a technical staff
of one director, thirteen deputy directors, eighteen assist-
ant directors and ninety eight senior agricultural assistants.
All these add up to 130 persons. It means that for every
134,000 acres of land under cultivation, there is only one
technical supervisor. Therefore I consider that it is
extremely necessary to double the technical staff of the
Agricultural Department. As the Veterinary Department
is an offshoot of the Agricultural Department, let us examine
its staff. There are all together 213 veterinarians consist-
ing of one director, nine deputy directors, nine veterinary
superintendents, fourty-four veterinary inspectors and
150 veterinary assistants. It means that we have only
one trained veterinarian to look after every 20,000 cattle.
Therefore it is very essential to increase the staff of the
veterinary Department also. The Forest Department has
a staff of 594 trained foresters consisting of one chief
conservator, six conservators, forty-two deputy conser-
vators, 52 extra assistant conservators, 131 rangers
and 359 deputy rangers. I consider that the Forest
Department has a fairly adequate staff to look after our
forests but many specialists will be needed for the new
forest research institute. The Geological Department has
four geologists, one chemist, one mining adviser and chief
inspector of mines, one assistant inspector of mines, one
junior mining engineer and one statistician all together
adding up to ten members only. This staff of ten is too
small to supervise the developing of immense mineral
resources of this country. I consider that the sooner we
can at least treble the present strength of this department
the earlier we shall be able to intensify the mineral pro-
duction drive in this country.
