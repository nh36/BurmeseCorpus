BOHMU BA SHIN
1914-1970
By Nai Pan Hla
on
The death of Bohmu Ba Shin,
January 7th, 1970, is a great loss to the
scholarly world and the whole nation of
Burma. He was a man of outstanding
intellectual abilty as teacher, writer, soldier,
religious leader, epigraphist, historian and
student of iconography. He worked not
only competently but also with integrity,
courage and devotion.
I knew him intimately for the last
fifteen years.
We first met when we were
introduced by Professor G. H. Luce at the
latter's residence in Rangoon in 1955.
Bohmu Ba Shin was then serving as-chair-
man of a sub-commission of the Burma
Historical Commission. When I became
a member of his sub-commission I was
assigned to collect source materials on Mon
culture. Since then we met very frequently
at Professor Luce's office, where we worked
mostly on epigraphy. We read rubbings
of inscriptions, and sometimes went to
Pagán together with Professor Luce to
read ink inscriptions on walls in the dark
interior of temples. The last time we
travelled together was in 1962 when we
worked at Taungbi Mòn Temple near
Pagán.
Bohmu was a Burmese of Indian
ancestry, born at Pyinmana in 1914. His
knowledge of language was remarkable.
He was well acquainted with almost all
the ethnic languages of Burma, whether
belonging to the Mon-Khmer group, the
Tibeto-Burman, the Tai or the Chinese,
as well as several Indian languages. He
wrote and spoke English fluently, which
gave him easy access to international
scholarship. He was an authority on Old
Burmese and Old Mon. He had a reading
knowledge of Pali; and though he was a
devout Muslim, he knew the Pali Buddhist
texts well and was a sympathetic student
of Buddhism.
JBRS, LV, i & ii, Dec., 1972.
'Bohmu' was his pen-name. He was
also called Saya
also called Saya (Acariya) Ba Shin by
most of his old pupils, many of whom
now occupy high positions. He was a
pupil of Professor Luce from 1935 to 1940,
working with him in the Department of
Far Eastern and Burmese History of the
Rangoon University. In 1940 he was
appointed Assistant Lecturer in that depart-
ment. In World War II he joined the
Burma Defence Army, and played a pro-
minent role in the Resistance Movement
against the Japanese. After the war he
served in the Army of Independent Burma,
in which he eventually rose to the rank
of Colonel (Bohmu). The History of Burma
(in Burmese) which he wrote for the Army
published on Independence Day, January
4, 1948-was prescribed as a text-book by
the Department of Education for use in
high schools. Later on he served as mili-
tary attaché in London, where he had the
opportunity of meeting many international
scholars.
In 1955, when the Burma Historical
Commission was founded he was appointed
a member. After retiring from the Army
because of ill health, he joined the Com-
mission as a full-time Compiler in 1960.
One of the tasks entrusted to him was to
write a detailed history of Burma from
1300 to 1752, but his activities covered a
much wider historical and archaeological
field. He was a very well-read man; he
travelled far and wide; he conducted much
scientific research; and he took a very
active part in collecting source materials
for the Commission both at home and
abroad. He wrote many scholarly articles
some in Burmese and some in English,
and submitted numerous research papers to
the Commission.

Bohmu Ba Shin
Together with Messrs Jean Boisselier
and A.B. Griswold, Bohmu Ba Shin served
as an editor of the Essays Offered to G. H.
Luce by his Colleagues and Friends in Honour
of his Seventy-Fifth Birthday (two volumes
Artibus
Asiae Supplementum XXIII,
Ascona, Switzerland, 1956). To this pub-
lication Bohmu con.ributed an article on
'Buddha Images of lai Yuan Types Found
in Burma. Other contributors on Burma
subjects include U Lu Pe Win, Jean Perrin,
H.L. Shorto, Daw Thin Kyi, U Khin Zaw,
Mon Bokay, Robbins Burling, Chen Yi-
Sein, Robert and Betty Morse, Jean
Rispaud, and U Wun; while the artist
U Nan We painted a picture of the
Sabbaññu Temple at Pagán as a tribute to
Professor Luce, which is reproduced as the
Frontispiece of Volume II.
Bohmu Ba Shin collaborated with
Professor Luce on a long study of the
Myinkaba Kubyauk-gyi temple of Rāja-
kumar at Pagan (built in 1113), published
in the Bulletin of the Burma Historical
Commission, Vol. II, 1961, rp. 277-416.
It contains a detailed examination of the
historical background of the temple, its
architecture and its Jātaka series; the texts
of all the glosses found in the temple, with
translations; charts of variant Jātaka titles
in old Pagán, studies of Old Mòn and the
Austro-Asiatic languages, the orthography
of Old Mòn inscriptions, and much else
besides. Bohmu was responsible for the
preparation of plates and plans, a com-
parative chart of Sanskrit, Pali, and Old
Mòn, with notes of reference to Old
Burmese. For this effort Professor Luce
wrote: 'My colleague, Bohmu Ba Shin,
has given invaluable help, not only in
checking my readings (mostly done 30
years ago), but also in the first reading of
many new glosses, overlooked by me.'
In a recent article on the Wetkyi-in
Kubyauk-gyi Temple at Pagan, which is
103
dedicated to Bohmu Ba Shin's memory,
Professor Luce refers to him as 'one of
Burma's finest scholars, epigraphists and
historians.'
Among the many excellent works by
Bohmu, the one I like the best is his book
Lokahteikpan, an Early Burmese Culture in
a Paga'n Temple (Rangoon, 1962). (See a
review of this book in the issue of Artibus
Asiae (pp. 228-233) Vol. XXXIII jointly
written by me and A.B. Griswold.)
Bohmu rendered much help to the
Department of Education as well as to the
Arts and Science University of Rangoon in
the field of history, culture and literature.
As an authority on Old Burmese ortho-
graphy, he was one of the active members
of the Committee on Burmese spelling.
I was for Mon and we always sat together.
We also worked together on the com-
parative study of the Mon and Burmese
versions of the Rajadhirit (Rājādhirāja),
which gives much interesting information
on the Middle Period of Burma history.
He was proud of his assignment and
worked extremely hard. He would come
to the office at 8 o'clock in the morning
and leave late in the evening. He said the
quiet morning time in the office was the
best part of the day, for he could write
with freedom of thought without interrup-
tion from other official business or visitors.
He also guided and supervised many senior
and final history-major students in the
preparation of their theses.
Bohmu Ba Shin did not live to enjoy
a ripe old age. He was still young for
death, but death is inevitable for every
man. Even our Lord Buddha, the Exalted
One, could not avoid it or stop it. Anicca !
Dukkha Anatta! May the God of Islam
protect Bohmu Ba Thin in a Perfect and
Eternal Heaven !
JBRS, LV, i & ii, Dec., 1972.
