I
INTRODUCTION
Few words in the English language
are fraught with as many meanings or
have been subjected to as many interpre-
tations as the term 'law' has been 1 To
some, law is a divinely ordained rule or
set of rules for human action. To others,
law is the recorded wisdom of the wise
men of old who had learned the divinely
approved course for human conduct. Thin-
kers like John Austin conceive of law as a
body of commands emanating from the
sovereign authority in a politically or-
ganised society, addressed to the members
of that society and which are generally
obeved. There is also the conception
of law as a body or system of rules im-
posed on men in society by the dominant
class for the time being in furtherance
of its own interest.
The late Roscoe Pound, the well-
known American legal philosopher, has
identified no less than twelve different
conceptions of law. Under such circum-
stances, one might be tempted to ask
whether it is possible to say in precise
terms what the Burmese concept of law
really is. Would it be wise to attribute
•
a particular concept of law to a nation
as a whole, however small the size of
such a nation may be ?
My answer to both of these questions
is 'yes'; and the reason is that the jurists,
the philosophers, the judges and all those
who have contributed to the formulation
of various legal concepts are, in a sense,
representatives of their own people. For.
in the words of Sir C. K. Allen, "every
man, be he genius or simpleton, is a
product of his age. The great concei-
vable intellect can work only with the
material which its environment vouchsafes
to it, and can express itself only in lan-
guage, and only about things, which are
intelligible to contemporary minds
Among judges and jurists some are con-
servative and reactionary, some are in-
novating and long-sighted, some narrow,
others liberal; but none can escape the
actual circumstances in which he lives or
attempt to apply the laws of Utopia to
existing society..." And yet, we can-
not ignore the influence of individual
minds. Great historical figures like Cae-
sar, Napoleon or Lenin, by sheer force

Just as the Dhammathats, which form
the main basis of Burmese Law, drew
their original inspiration from Hindu
Dharmashastras, So also the Burmese
concept of law was originally derived
from the same source, namely, the Dhar-
ma. This is not to be confused with
divine law or law of God. The term
Dharma represents the Hindu conception
of a moral order coeval with the world
and to which every man had to adapt
the conduct of his life if he was con-
cerned with his salvation and progress.
This moral order is not governed by
arbitrary rules, dependent on individual
feelings, but has been established
for ever in a body of immutable rules,
which operate exactly as physical laws
of a cosmic world, acting their part
mechanically, punishing transgressors au-
tomatically, rewarding those obeying them
by the same mechanical process. It is
the collection of these eternal, transcen-
ding rules, that is called Dharma, and it
was revealed to Brahma, the Self-Existent
Being, to Manu, a semi divine being, and
from Manu to ancient sages, who in their
turn made it known to mankind through
abridged versions called dharmashastras
or treatises on the Dharma. It was the
religious duty of every man to know the
Dha ma and to strive to follow its dictates
if he would avoid disgrace and ruin.55

A Burmese scholar has made the

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION
Few words in the English language has
as many meanings given to them as does
the term 'law. A well-known American
legal philosopher, Roscoe Pound, has
listed at least twelve different conceptions
of law. The title of this Paper might,
therefore, raise doubts in the minds of
those who see it as to whether it would
be wise to attribute a single conception
of law to a nation as a whole. The
answer to this may be found in the fact
that jurists and philosophers, who had
contributed to the formulation of legal
principles are considered to be the re-
presentatives of their own peoples and
are, therefore, spokesmen for the people
on matters of jurisprudence and legal
philosophy. Thus, the consensus among
our Burmese judges and jurists, both
ancient and modern, could properly be
