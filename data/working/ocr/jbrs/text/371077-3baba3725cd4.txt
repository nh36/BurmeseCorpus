I. Introduction
One of the most active fields of research in the scientific world of
today is that of radio. Every year many billions of Kyats are devoted
to inventing, developing and producing new devices for radio, television,
radar, electronic computers, servo-mechanisms, proximity fuses,
telemetering and continually improving present equipment. For
instance in television alone, the successful transmission of colour
pictures by radio waves, called for many million man-hours of research
work to devise new methods, standardise procedures, design new
instruments and transmitters and receivers, test them and develop them
for mass production.
This research in television engineering, aimed at the transmission
of color pictures, brought in its wake, subsidiary discoveries in optics,
colorimetery, electromagnetics, transistors and is just another example
of how all research is interrelated and how an advance of knowledge in
one area can contribute to advances at many other points along the
frontier of science.
This report therefore is for the benefit of research investigators in
all fields of science and technology, to acquaint them with the applica-
tion of radio and the techniques we have learnt in the field of radio, so
that the search for new knowledge may by accelerated. This cross
fertilisation of ideas between the different scientific groups is a most
wholesome and invigorating experience for all those who share in the
process.

III. The application of electronic circuits in Radioactivity
Research
Another area in which extremely small quantities have to be
measured is in radioactivity research. During the decay of a radio-
active atom, one electron or alpha particle may be given off and the
instruments must be able to detect this single particle which carries a
charge of less than one hundredth-millionth of a coulomb. This may
be done in a scintillation counter using the photomultiplier tube
developed originally for use in television but now serving many other
useful purposes or in a Geiger Counter with its associated electronic
circuits (e.g. Higginbotham Circuit).
IV. Radioisotopes and how they aid Agricultural and
Biological Research
Electronic means are now available to detect a single radio-
active atom. This opens up fascinating possibilities in research concerned
with the growth of plants and trees and animals and their diseases too.
One method employed has been to get a radioactive isotope of carbon
(or the element under study) prepared in an atomic pile and to put it in
the food of the plant or animal. This radioactive isotope then can be
followed by a Geiger Counter and its subsequent career fully described.

VI. Employment of Radiosonde In Meteorology Research
Meteorologists need information about the temperature, humidity,
pressure and winds at all levels of the atmosphere in order to conduct
research and correlate their theories with actual phenomena. For this
research, radio comes to their help. By attaching a portable radio
transmitter to a balloon so that temperature, humidity and pressure
sensitive elements can modulate the radio frequency at an audio rate,
the meteorologist can receive on his radiosonde equipment on the
ground, the conditions prevailing at the various portions of the atmos-
phere through which the balloon is travelling. By the use of radarsonde
equipment he can in addition get information on the prevailing winds
at various levels of the atmosphere. Radar has also been utilised by
research meteorologists at M.I.T. to "see" through lower layers of cloud
to investigate the structure of cloud formations and to "follow a storm
front" for hundreds of miles.
Radar has been put to quite a different use by food technologists.
They have developed a radar cooker which can boil a pot of rice in
minute (normal cooking time 10 minutes) and roast a large hunk of
meat in 2 minutes (normal roasting time 4 hours) and bake a big cake

VII. The Stroboscope, Tool For Applied Mechanics Research
The stroboscope, an electronic time flasher invented by M.I.T.
Professor Edgerton to produce very short duration light flashes (one
millionth of a second) at controlled rates is now used by many research
workers, notably those in applied mechanics. The utility comes about
in this way. Suppose we have an engine wheel revolving at several
thousand revolutions per minute and wish to study its behavior with
ordinary light we can only see blur - but by flashing a stroboscope at
the right rate, the wheel is illuminated once every revolution for a very
short duration and to our eye it appears to be standing still and we can
examine every detail on it clearly. Hydraulic engineers, mechanical
and electrical engineers and physicists all find various use for the
stroboscope in their investigation of fast moving pnenomena.
VIII. Radioastronomy Discovers New Worlds
Radio has opened up new vistas for a very old science namely,
astronomy. Since ancient times man has looked with his unaided eye,
then with the optical telescope, into outer space and has kept
discovering more and more stars and planets. Very recently a whole
new group of "invisible" stars have been observed by astronomers
using radio telescopes. These stars are so "cool" that they do not
emit any visible light but they do emit radio waves and can be observed
by pointing a highly-directional radio antenna at them and measuring
the radio frequencies on a receiver.

X. FM Radio Transmitters For Research Into Atomic
Structure
The glamorous field of research nowadays is atomic fission.
The spectacular results of the atomic bomb and its tremendous
implications have no doubt helped to build up public interest in this
field. The devices used in atom smashing also have contributed in no
small measure to the news-making ability of this branch of science.
Whenever a new multi-million volt cyclotron goes into operation it
makes the headlines. What does a cyclotron consist of in principle?
To put it in a rather simple way, it is just a circular path with a device
designed to speed up a bombarding particle to an energy of several
million election voltes and then shoot it out at a target in order to
disintegrate the atoms in the target material. The device which
performs the speeding up is FM transmitter, very similar to those
used for FM radio broadcasting. So we see here again how radio
plays a vital role, in yet another area of research.

XI. Conclusion
-
We have just taken a quick survey - it has not been possible within the
limits of a few pages to go into any details. But sufficient has been
said to indicate the wide scope of modern radio and the various uses
to which radio may be put.
One particular use of electronics has not been mentioned so far
but which may one day solve the fundamental problem of research
itself. This is the application of radio circuits to large automatic
programmed "thinking and calculating machines". So far we still
call these as automatic computers but the day is fast approaching when
these machines will no longer be just computing machines but will be
able to "think" (i.e. take decisions, learn new techniques, and
correct their own mistakes) which is more than what some human
beings seem to be able to do!
