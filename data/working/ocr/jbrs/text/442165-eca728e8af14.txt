The first railway line (from Rangoon to Prome) was sanctioned for construction
in 1874, and by the end of 1914 Rs. 215 millions had been invested in 1599 miles of
Burma railways by the State and the Burma Railway Company.'
One would expect
considerable repercussions on Burmese economy from such a volume of investment,
although the progress in railway mileage was much slower than in other Asian countries
such as Japan.
Before the construction of railways, the steamers of the Irrawaddy Flotilla
Company formed the only mechanical means of internal transport, and the condition of
roads was also unsatisfactory. Apart from cart-tracks there were only 815 miles
of roads in Lower Burma in 1873,³ and in 1875 the Irrawaddy Flotilla
Company possessed a fleet of ten steamers and twenty flats. ↑ Although this
Company transformed the condition of transport along the Irrawaddy river during the
late 1870's through its extended fleet of steamers, its beneficial effects were confined to
places along the river bank, and high cost of cart transport to the river banks hindered
the reclamation of arcas further inland. 5 This backward state of inland transport
meant wide regional price differences. In 1874, for example, paddy brought down from
Prome in the Irrawaddy Flotilla Company's steamers rose by fifty per cent in price by

The railway construction in Burma was initiated by the State, which continued
to provide a large portion of the railway capital even after the railways had been leased
to the Burma Railway Company in 1896. It would therefore be of interest to analyse
the reasons behind the State's interest in railways. Were railways constructed mainly for
their strategic value, as they were attributed to be so in Tailand 3 and India? How far
was the State's interest in railways inspired by economic and non-economic factors? An
analysis of the State's motives will enable us not only to discuss such questions of
importance, but also to appraise the success or failure in realising the original intentions
after the extension of the railway network. The remaining portion of the paper will be
divided into two parts, dealing first with the Government motives and secondly with
the economic effects of the railways.
II
Bearing in mind the warnings against a naive acceptance of altruistic expressions
by British Statesmen, * the motives expressed by various authorities responsible for
Burma railways may be grouped under three broad headings--social, economic and
military. 5
However, it must be admitted at the outset that they often overlap and
are hard to demarcate from one another.
(1) Social and Welfare Motives:
The British justified their colonial pretensions by considering it their duty to
improve the social and spiritual, as well as the economic, conditions of the people they
governed. We can detect some traces of the desire to spread “the civilizing influence"
of British rule. It was believed that the Rangoon-Prome railway would facilitate and
enhance “the free interchange of ideas and communication" between British and
Independent Burma, ª which would thereby benefit both countries. "The employment

The Government also stressed the welfare aspects of railways. It intended
to relieve the misery of the poorer class of the population in congested
districts of Upper Burma, by providing railway facilities for them to migrate and settle in
adjoining areas of potential development.2 During the 1890's, when the deficiency of
rainfall caused scarcity and distress in many districts of Upper Burma, the Thazi-
Myingyan railway was constructed as a "famine" relief work. Although the Burma
Famine Conference of 1897 recommended the adoption of railway construction and other
major irrigation projects as relief measures in future famines,4 no other occasion arose
requiring such use. It must however be noted that the construction of the first railway
line was commenced in 1874 "mainly with the view of affording works for immigrants
from Bengal", which was ravaged by a severe famine in 1873 and 1874. In all subse-
quent schemes of state-assisted emigration from India to Burma, which were undertaken
during later Indian famines, Indian immigrants were employed with greater success on
public works and as coolies, although the main aim in all of them was to settle the
Indians on wastelands of Burma.6 From a wider point of view, we may say that the
construction of Burma railways was linked, either consciously or unconsciously, with
the Indian famine relief measures, both by giving employment to Indian immigrants and
by assisting the shipments of surplus rice to India.
The rapid economic progress of British (or Lower) Burma between 1852 and
1874 was achieved without efficient means of transport. It was therefore believed that
the railway construction would enhance the welfare of the people not only by quicken-
ing the pace of this process," but also by encouraging a wider development of the
the resources of the country.

The main railway lines of Burma were intended to strengthen the efficiency and thereby
reduce the cost of military and police forces, in regard to the defence of frontiers and
the maintenance of Law and order. The military motives may be discussed from two
viewpoints, namely, (i) purely military and strategic aspects, for defence against external
aggression, and (ii) political and administrative aspects, for internal administration and
for improving the political relations with neighbouring states.
(i) With regard to the first aspect, it must be noted that the British in Lower
Burma did not conceive of any real danger of formidable attack from Upper Burma,1
and yet this possibility was never disregarded. Thus in soliciting the approval of the
Indian Government for the Rangoon-Prome railway and its proposed extension to
Allanmyo, the Government of Burma put military expediency as the first objective of
the line, and the development of trade as its second aim.2 If by an unfortunate
change", wrote Sir Ashley Eden, the Chief Commissioner, "Upper Burma was to become
disturbed... and we have to move troops and stores to the frontier in any number or
quantity, the saving from the existence of the Railway would probably nearly equal the
entire outlay in [its] construction", 3 Later on, the "Army Organisation Commission",
which was entrusted with the task of planning the military lines of communications in
the whole of British India, named the Rangoon-Toungoo line, as "one of the lincs
which ought to be undertaken at once". In preparing the project of this line, the
Government of Burma admitted that the military reason "would form the almost sole
reason of the line, if it were construcied at present", although there existed "indefinite
capabilities of developing the area traversed by it. Hence, although the first two lines
constructed before 1886 (from Rangoon to Prome and Toungoo) were expected to
develop the areas through which they passed, they “are wanted in a great degree for
military purposes. This is specially the case in respect of the line from Rangoon to
Toungoo."6

III
We now come to the interesting, but more controversial, part of the paper,
namely, the economic effects of railways on Burmese economy. The paucity of proper
statistics on income, prices and output precludes any accurate quantitative statements
in this section on the economic effects of railways. Even if more adequate data were
available, it would still be impossible to relate the extension of railways and the economic
development of Burma in causal terms. Our task is therefore less ambitious, confining
ourselves to qualitative assessments³ and the examinatton of the extent to which the
original intentions of the State were realised after the railways had been constructed.
(a) Railways and social and political changes:
Since social and political factors were important motives behind the State's
investment in railways, we shall briefly try to asset the effects of railways on social and
political structure, bearing in mind that these effects took some time to reveal them-
selves, and that there cannot be any general agreement about their merits.

(c) The effects of railways on industrialisation, trade and agriculture :
It is natural that in the early stages of railway construction, an underdeveloped
country should depend on foreign technicians and materials. But this dependence could
be reduced at later stages as local industries for railway materials developed, as in the case
of Japan. It would therefore be of interest to consider whether the railways fostered or
retarded the growth of industries in Burma.
Naturally some of the capital raised in the London Money Market was spent on
procuring railway equipment from Britain.2 Thus, for example, out of a cumulative
capital of Rs. 64.4 millions which the State had invested in railways up to 1892, Rs. 15.1
millions (or about twenty-three per cent) were spent in Britain, mainly on iron and steel
components of railways. In addition, owing to the absence of local railway fuel apart
from wood, large quantities of coal for the railways were imported from Bengal and
Britain. The fact that nearly all railway equipment and fuel were imported limited the
benefits of the railways in promoting new local industries.

With respect to "non-railway" industries, no accurate assessment can be made in
the absence of output statistics. Just as the railways might have facilitated the penetration
of the internal markets by imported manufactured goods² which displaced traditional
handicrafts, notably hand-loom weaving," so also they played an important part in the
offsetting trend of spreading rice-and saw-mills into the interior of the country. Even in
the decline of indigenous handicrafts the railways were not the main reason. The reasons
are to be found partly in the changes of taste which followed greater Westernisation, and
partly in the free trade policy which permitted the full competitive powers of factory
products to be felt. The railways merely quickened a process which was the inevitable
accompaniment of increased economic contact with the outer world.
One of the main motives of the State in constructing the railways was to open out
virgin territories to cultivation and settlement, by providing a new means of transport
away from the navigable rivers. The railways undoubtedly provided a great impetus to
expansion of cultivation in areas away from the rivers, by raising prices of paddy at the
internal centres to the Rangoon level, which was itself moving upwards due to the increased
world demand for rice. The extension of cultivation for export in turn enabled the
cultivators to buy more imported goods, whose prices in their localities were also reduced
by lower transport cost. The encouragement given by the railways to extension of
agriculture and trade is shown by the tremendous rise in land revenue associated with
settlements along the railways, and also by the increased volume of various commodities
carried by the railways.

IV
The section on motives has revealed that the Government wanted railways for a
number of reasons social, economic and military. As is general in a case where several
motives are involved, it is very hard to arrange the Government motives in order of their
importance. Perhaps the railways were constructed mainly for military motives, [followed
closely by the economic. However, the predominance of military considerations in the
railway projects is partly explained by the fact that the authorities responsible for the
early railway projects were military personnels attached to the Public Works Department.
Because of their training and experience, they would naturally see things more from the
military point of view. Moreover, in a country where railways were designed to open out
virgin tertitories, the immediate reasons for construction would be other than the prospects
of direct returns. Although much emphasis on "indirect returns" is to be found in most
of the railway projects, they are not susceptible to meaningful measurements, and thus the
military considerations became prominent as the immediate motive. "It must be at once
admitted", reported the Executive Engineer of the Mu Valley Railway, "that this extension
will not pay commercially for some time to come, and the reasons therefore for recom-
mending its construction are mainly political. But, at the same time, it would attract
settlers, extend cultivation, open up markets and greatly facilitate administration."1
In the section on the economic effects of railways, the paucity of statistical data
precludes any accurate quantitative statements. However, literary and other evidence
enable us to arrive at some broad conclusions. The railway seemed to have produced
two notable effects. The first of the beneficial effects was the alleviation of “famines” in
the dry zone area. The second was the encouragement given by the railways to agricul-
tural extension. However, even in the latter, we must guard ourselves against exaggeration
because the railway mileage before 1914 was too small to exert any influence over a large
part of the agricultural regions. Moreover the credit for agricultural extension in most
of the deltaic areas must be given to other factors such as protective embankments
constructed along the Irrawaddy and the Sittang, the extended steamer services of the
Irrawaddy Flotilla Company, and so on.
