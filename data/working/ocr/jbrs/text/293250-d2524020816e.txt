ADMIRAL LUARD.
Admiral Sir William Garnham Luard was born at Witham, in
Essex, on the 7th April 1820. He entered the Royal Naval College
at Portsmouth in 1833, and in 1835 was appointed a midshipman
on the frigate Actaeon. He served on the South American station
and then the China station, where in 1841 he was promoted to the
rank of Lieutenant for his services during the China War. He
subsequently served on the Cape station, and after a second period of
service on the South American station was in April 1848 sent to the
East India station as First Lieutenant of H.M.S. Hastings. On the
29th September 1850 he was promoted to the rank of Commander
and was posted to the command of H.M.S. Serpent, a twelve-gun
sloop with a complement of one hundred and twenty-five men.
When in the last weeks of the year 1851 Commodore Lambert
was ordered to Burma to negotiate with the Burmese authorities,
the Serpent formed part of his squadron, and apparently stayed in
the Rangoon River off the town throughout the course of the some-
what hectic diplomacy which characterised Lambert's visit to Rangoon.
During this time, Commander Luard was engaged in surveying
the River, in the course of which work he contracted sunstroke and
was in consequence seriously ill. Perhaps in consequence of this
illness he does not appear to have been called upon to take any active
part in the negotiations between the Commodore and the Myowun
of Hanthawaddy; his participation in affairs did not begin until
after the declaration by the Commodore, on the 6th January 1852,
of a state of blockade of the coast of Burma. After the issue of this
declaration the Serpent was ordered down the River, where she
anchored off the Bassein Creek to complete her watering prior to
taking up position off the island of Negrais to enforce the blockade
of Bassein. Commander Luard thus escaped the clash of arms
which occurred on the 10th January.
The Serpent anchored off Diamond Island on the morning of
the 16th January, and on the 17th stopped a junk which was making
for Bassein. On the following day Luard decided to continue his
surveying, and went off in his gig for that purpose; his boat was
fired on by a stockade, but without effect. On the 20th the Serpent
passed through the western passage (the Hainggyi channel) between
Negrais and the mainland and anchored off a small village; the
object of this move appears to have been to continue the work of
surveying the coast. There was a threat of an attack that night
when at about 8 p.m. a party of soldiers was seen to move from the
stockade to the shore, bearing torches; but nothing came of this.

After nearly a week of taking soundings and enforcing the blockade,
the Serpent went to Diamond Island on the 26th, but on the 4th
February returned to Negrais once more. As she passed Pagoda
Point, seven shots were fired at her from a stockade, at about 2 o'clock
in the afternoon; the Serpent returned the fire, landed an armed
party, destroyed several stockades, and took possession of some
boats and supplies. On the following day, the 5th, at about 9-30 p.m.,
she was again fired on, this time from a stockade near the village on
Negrais Island, but the garrison deserted the place during the night
and when on the next morning a landing-party went ashore, it was
able to burn the stockade without resistance.
These events were reported to the Commodore by Commander
Luard in the following terms-
"I have to inform you that, on the 4th instant, I
left Diamond Island, in order to return to the anchorage
inside Negrais; and whilst entering the Western channel,
the stockade on the mainland opened fire upon the ship,
which I returned with one broadside of shot and one of
shell, and afterwards sent the armed boats under the charge
of Lieutenant L. W. Hammet, to destroy the stockades,
which he effectually accomplished. The two upon the
hill, mentioned in my last letter, were supported by four
others, lower down, almost entirely concealed by the jungle,
and might have been defended against a much superior
force.
"Three small guns, a large quantity of rice, and shel-
ters for about 500 men, were destroyed with the stockades ;
four war-boats, and one large cargo boat loaded with rice
and stockading tools, were also brought off, and have since
been destroyed.
"In the morning, after having examined the south end
of Negrais, and the opposite shore, without finding either
of the stockades marked down in the old chart, I proceeded
up the river, and, on opening the village of Negrais, per-
ceived that a stockade had been built in our absence. I
immediately anchored, and sent an officer to enquire if the
stockade also had orders to fire upon us. He returned.
bringing off an interpreter, who stated that they were quite
ready to fight. The stockade, however, was so completely
under our guns, and so full of men, that being unwilling to
cause such a great loss of life as must have ensued, had we
commenced firing, I sent the interpreter back to his chief
to tell him I would give him till sunrise the next morning,
by which time, if the stockade was not pulled down, I should

The determination of the Governor of Bassein to avoid conflicts
appears to have prevented any further acts of war. The Serpent
remained undisturbed off the mouth of the Bassein River until after
the formal declaration of war, when she proceeded to the Rangoon
River to join the expeditionary force. Then, on the 4th April, she
went up the River with the East India Company's steamers Tenas-
serim and Phlegethon, carrying three companies of H.M.'s 18th
Regiment. The ships anchored for the night off the Bassein Creek,
and on the morning of the 5th Commander Luard, with the Serpent
and the Phlegethon which had also been placed under his command,
and one company of the 18th, proceeded to the stockades at De Silva
Point which were taken and destroyed.
When the actual attack on Rangoon began, on the 11th April,
the Serpent passed up the river under the gun-fire of the stockades
along the waterfront, and anchored above Kemmendine to capture
the war-boats there and prevent any fire-rafts being sent down against
the shipping. In his report on these events Rear-Admiral Austen
wrote―
66
Commander Luard, of H. M. Sloop Serpent, has
been employed many months in these waters. A more
zealous and indefatigable officer is not to be met with, and,
relying upon his ability, he has frequently been placed in
charge of responsible duties. With the assistance of the
Honourable Company's steamer Phlegethon, commanded
by G. T. Neblett, Esq., he destroyed the De Silva stockades.
When the action on the 11th of April commenced, he
passed along the line of fire in the Sloop under his command,
to above Kemmendine, for the purpose of preventing the
removal of the war boats; and, whilst on this service he
was attacked by a very important stockade, and an Officer
and seven of his men were wounded, but he ultimately
succeeded in silencing it. Before the commencement of
the war he took great pains in surveying the coast and
river, and his information was afterwards of great use."
In August of the same year the Serpent was ordered home to
be paid off, and on this occasion the Admiral addressed Luard in
the following terms-
"In addressing to you this day your sailing orders to
proceed to England to be paid off, it is no less a duty than
a pleasure to place upon record the high sense I entertain
of your services during the two years and a half you have
served under my command. My having promoted you
from H.M.S. Hastings must have shown you that your
zeal in the service and your conduct on board my Flag Ship
was fully appreciated by me, and I am happy to be able

The Admiral's Secretary wrote at the same time in reference
to the Admiral's letter-
"There is no one who was in the Irrawaddy at that
time who can have a doubt but that the fearfully hard
work you had there in carrying on surveying operations
during a burning sun nearly brought you to your grave,
and I do trust when the letter I allude to and the prominent
manner in which the Admiral brought you forward in the
Rangoon Despatch has been brought under the notice of
the Board, that they will give you that promotion which
the Admiral so confidently believed you had worked out
for yourself before you left his command."
The promotion so confidently predicted by the Admiral and
his Secretary was not immediately forthcoming, and it was not till
March 1857 that Luard received his captaincy. He did not
return to the East India station, though in 1860 he was in the China
station in command of the Conqueror, and there earned the Com-
panionship of the Bath and the Legion of Honour for his services
during the difficulties with the Japanese. He received his flag in
1875, was promoted Vice-Admiral in 1879, and Admiral in 1885. In
1882 he had been appointed President of the Royal Naval College,
Greenwich. In the Honours List of the Diamond Jubilee in 1897 he
was awarded the K.C.B. Admiral Luard, despite his long and
strenuous service, lived to an advanced age; his death occurred,
as the result of a carriage accident, on the 19th May 1910, at his
home in Witham, Essex, at the age of over ninety years.
