The population of Thailand like
that of Burma is not ethnically homoge-
neous. Discounting for a moment the
more moderm foreign immigrants, the
Chinese and to a lesser extent Indians, a
variety of ethnic elements is discernible in
the composition of the Thai population.
The Thais of southern Thailand, free from
Chinese blood are dark-skinned compared
to the northerners and often have curly
hair and broadish nostrils which are often
vaguely upturned. The peninsular(southern)
Thais cannot be entirely free from the
bloods of Semangs and Sakais, remnants of
the earlier inhabitants of the Malay Penin-
sula region. (Compare the Semangs and
Sakais with the Salons of the Mergui
Archipelago.) A very beautiful and popu-
lar Thai story, 'Sang Thong' (golden snell)
describes how a legendary Thai princess
marries an ugly Ngoc (the Thai term for
a Semang or a Sakai) of the forest, much
to the consternation at first of her royal
parents. After various tests and trials, the
Ngoc turns out to be a man of sterling
qualities, both intellectual and physical-
a hero, a golden prince in the garb (shell)
of the semi-civilized Ngoc. The story
embodies the Thai nation's conception of
ethnic assimilation to attain which inter-
marriage as a contributary factor is employ-
ed as one of the means. In the faces
of modern Thais, differential ethnic features
of the Khmer, Mon and Chinese, ranging
from mere strains to purity can be obser-
ved. But everybody is a THAI, complete-
ly assimilated.

When the Shans (Tais) migrated
from southern and southwestern China to
what is known to-day as Thailand, there
were already well-established Mon and
Khmer kingdoms in the region. The
Zimme (Chiengmai) region which now
constitutes Northern Thailand was the
Mon kingdom of Haripunja when a
branch of the Shans first settled there.
The old Mon capital, Haripunjai_( =
modern Lamphun) was captured by a Shan
prince, Meng Rai in 1281 when he esta-
blished the independent Thai state called
the Lanna Kingdom which comprised the
Upper Menam valley. Two other indepen-
dent Thai states in the Middle and Lower
Menam valky regions, namely, the Sukho-
thai and Ayudhya were founded during
the 13th and 14th centuries A. D. respec-
tively in supplantation of the Khmer
administrations there. When the early
Thais extended their political authority
into the upper portion of the Malay
Peninsula, people of Malay stock became
part of Thailand's population. Thus the
heterogeneous basis of the Thai popula-
tion was inherent in the very circumstances
under which the Thai state took its initial
shape some seven hundred years ago.
