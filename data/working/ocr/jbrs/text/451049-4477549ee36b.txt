In the post World War II period, especially after the launching of the Eight Year
Plan for development in 1952-53, the government expenditures and receipts have
increased sharply. The public domestic payments amount to approximately 50 percent o
the Gross Domestic Product at current prices. In relation to the public domestic receipts
the public domestic payments have been consistently above by more than 11 percent of the
current GDP over the period 1954-55 to 1959-60. The Union Government's receipts and
expenditures (current and capital) have risen from a level around 16 percent of the curren-
GDP to that around 25 percent. The government operations, therefore, are a single
most important factor determining the economic trends in the economy.

Using ex ante terms the condition for a stable level of aggregate expenditures may
be stated as equality between the government's cash deficit with the private sector and the
intended (or desired) excess of private savings over investment at the initial level of income,
plus the intended net imports that the private sector would wish to undertake at the initial
level of income. In other words, the surplus on private account in ex ante terms is called
the "intended surplus on private account".
Increasing aggregate expenditures imply that the government's cash deficit should
exceed the intended surplus on private account. The excess may be termed Expenditure
Gap. The increase in aggregate expenditures will tend to exceed, and will be a multiple
of, the expenditure gap if it is maintained over successive periods.
The main variables that determine the size of the expenditure gap may be identified
from equation (5) as level of public domestic expenditure, level of public domestic receipts'
excess of intended gross private saving over investment, and the level of intended net
private imports. Given the level of public domestic receipts and the level of the intended
surplus on private account the expenditure gap will be determined by the level of public
domestic expenditures. To avoid inflation these crucial variables of equation (5) should
be so adjusted by the fiscal authority as to create an expenditure gap that would generate
aggregate expenditures equal to the total value of Gross Domestic Product at constant
prices.

at constant prices to be five percent per annum. To maintain price stability the aggregate

In order to make this little price of theory an effective tool of fiscal policy numerous
difficulties arise. First, to determine the ratio between the expenditure gap and the
aggregate increase in expenditures, a number of factors have to be considered. On the
consumption side, the resulting increase will be determined by the multiplier effect. On
the investment side, the resulting increase in expenditures will depend upon induced
investment, that is, how private investment responds to an increase in total demand. The
response will depend not only on the initial increase in expenditures but also on resulting
changes in money supply. The effects of changes in money supply continue to be felt
even though the effects of initial expenditure gap will taper off in future periods if the
rate of increase of expenditure gap is discontinued. The effects of changes in money
supply are continued to be felt also because of structural reasons and, especially, the dicho-
tomous character of the economy - a rural subsistence production sector and the urban
sector which restrict inter-sectoral flow of incomes. In respect to the response of private
investment to increase in total demand it is not only the magnitude but also the rapidity
of response which is of great importance to policy making.
In an economic organisation, such as that of Burma, where institutional factors -
various import, exchange, price, and distribution controls - - restrict the true propensities
to consume and import no meaningful estimates of the multiplier and induced investment
are possible. Because of the complexity of the problem it is not possible to predict the
extent to which the expenditure gap will increase aggregate expenditures: Besides in a
developing economy a given ratio of expenditure gap to total expenditures may be correct
for one year yet quite a different ratio may apply to another time.
Given the ratio of expenditure gap to total expenditures it still remains to determine
what increase in total expenditures is permissible in order to avoid inflation. It may be
argued that the increase in total expenditures generated by the expenditure gap should
match the increase in output valued at constant prices. An increase in total expenditures
more than the increase in real output will create an inflationary gap. In a developed
economy, under full employment, increase in output will be due to increased productivity,
and the marginal capital/output ratio may be determined. But in a developing economy
the increase in income may not be easy to estimate. In such an economy there is no

The full employment assumption is not valid for a country like Burma where a
considerable number of people remain unemployed or underemployed. The expenditure
gap, to the extent it draws such people to productive employment would increase output
rather than cause inflation. In this case the desirable increase in the total level of expen-
ditures would be greater than what it would be under full employment. But much will
depend on how responsive the idle resources are to increases in demand, to what extent
bottlenecks and shortages will develop and so forth. In Burma certain cultural factors
also impede such responsiveness. The problem which the creation of expenditure gap
then involves is the extent of price rise which would be permissible with a given increase
in real output, the areas where such price rises may occur and so forth. These factors
can not easily be appraised but they must be kept in mind by the policy maker when
deciding on the desired increase in total expenditure the level of expenditure gap, and the
required level of taxation in order to avoid inflation.
Foreign Exchange Earnings and Net Private Imports
The current level of foreign exchange earnings and their reflection in the contribu-
tion of public enterprises, especially the SAMB, have no direct bearing on the size of the
government's cash deficit with the private sector; and hence no bearing on the need for
taxation. The size of the cash deficit and hence the need for taxation are the same whether
the government obtains its funds by drawing on balances, borrowing from the Union Bank
of Burma, or from SAMB.
Foreign exchange earnings, however, bear indirectly on the need for taxation.
More foreign exchange makes more imports available. If we take private imports as a
residual item after government imports, then higher foreign exchange receipts enable the
private sector to finance a higher level of imports. A decline in foreign exchange earnings,
therefore, makes it necessary to restrict private imports. The restriction of drivate imports
reduces the intended surplus on private account. This requires a corresponding reduction
in the government deficit with the private sector, and hence a corresponding higher level
of domestic taxation or a lower level of public domestic expenditures.
The structure of Burmese economy is such that a substantial cash deficit of the
government with the private sector is needed to maintain the level of total expenditures.
Its main function is to act as an offset against the surplus on private account and also to
provide for an adequate growth of money supply as output increases. The most significant
item in the surplus on private account is the net private imports. Burma is dependent to a

Expenditure Gap and Changes in Money Supply
A cash deficit of the government with the private sector will give rise to a correspon-
ding increase in the privately held money supply, whether it is financed out of SAMB
contributions or by borrowing from the Union Bank of Burma. The release of the SAMB
earnings for government spending acts in the same way as receipts from private exports on
the money supply of the economy. To the extent the deficit is financed by sale of bonds
to the non-bank public, the deficit will not increase privately held money supply. However,
sale of bonds to the non-bank public is not yet an important factor in Burma.
Net imports by the private sector involve the purchase of foreign exchange from the
Union Bank of Burma and thus result in a corresponding reduction in the privately held
money supply.
An excess of intended saving over planned investment will effect changes in money
supply depending on the velocity of money in the private sector. If the private sector's
velocity of money is one then changes private money supply will equal the difference
between the intended saving and planned investment in the private sector.
If the velocity
of money in the private sector is not one but constant the private expenditures will change
directly and proportionately with changes in money supply. However, total expenditures
will not necessarily vary directly and proportionately with money supply. This will be
true only if government expenditures were a constant ratio of private expenditures. In
other words even if valocity of money in the private sector is constant the income velocity
of money need not be constant.
If the income valocity of money is constant the cash deficit of the government equal
to the intended surplus on private account! will keep the total expenditures constant.

A change in total expenditures will be possible only if there is a corresponding change in
money supply. Thus, an excess in the cash deficit above the intended surplus on private
account (i.e. an expenditure gap) will give rise to an equal increase in the privately held
money supply and the increase in total expenditures will be equal to the expenditure gap.
However, the assumption, constant income valocity of money, is not a realistic one.
If changes in income valocity are allowed, a situation of constant total expenditures need
not necessarily be one in which money supply remains constant. An expenditure gap may,
but need not, be accompanied by a corresponding increase in private money supply.
This is not to deny that changes in private money supply, implicit in the expenditure
gap, are important. They are infact very important in Burma where holding of cash
balances is not a widespread practice. But changes in money supply are not necessarily
equal to the expenditure gap as bank loans to private borrowers, savings deposits, etc.,
also enter the picture, although in relatively small amounts.
Expenditure Gap and Development
Expenditure Gap (or deficit financing) is now a well recognized fiscal tool to combat
unemployment in advanced economies. A situation of unemployment in developed
economies is attended with excess capacity and the main purpose of an expenditure gap
is to increase aggregate demand. An expenditure gap, for the purpose of achieving full
employment, may be created by a combination of increased government expenditures and
reduced taxation.
However, the fundamental role of expenditure gap in an undeveloped economy is
to develop resource and capital capacity. A large part of unemployment in such econo-
mies is not due to lack of aggregate demand but because of relative shortage of capital to
fully emyloy other factors. In an "undeveloped setting" large expenditures are needed for
capital formation. Creation of large expenditure gaps will result in strong inflationary
pressure especially because of the long "production lags" and high capital/output ratios.
Long production lags are inevitable since capacity has to be developed before output can
be produced. High capital/output ratios can not be avoided where heavy expenditures
are made on Social Overhead Capital at the initial stages of development. Therefore, in
order to mitigate inflationary pressure, the fiscal policy in an undeveloped setting must
not combine increased expenditures with overall reduction in tax yield as in a full-employ-
ment-policy. On the contrary tax yield must be increased to mop up the inflationary gap.
Measures taken to increase tax yield should not be such as to adversely affect
incentives to work and to produce or to become actual additions to costs. The effect of
such tax measures would be to increase the inflationary pressure through "cost push price"
type of medias.

without imposing reductions in prices and wages in other sectors.

A note of warning may be given here against a possible mistaken notion that since inflation
can be controlled by raising tax yield, expenditure gaps can be increased to any level.
Rising of tax yield is not without limits. These limits are set by inefficiencies in tax
administration, by political opposition to the raising of tax rates and introduction of new
taxes or expansion of the tax base, and by adverse effects of taxation on incentives. Besides,
however flexible a tax system may be it would not completely siphon off the inflationary
gap generated by the expenditure gap since a part of the increased incomes flows into
channels which can be siphoned off only through shiftable taxes, or taxes that become
actual additions to costs. A partial effect of such taxes is to raise prices and hence to
aggrevate inflationary pressure. Increased expenditures in these channel may be supported
by a fall in liquidity preference which might offset the deflationary effect of taxation on
income.

Expenditure Gap Policy of the Government
During the fiscal years,1
1947 thru 1952, the government maintained overall
budget surpluses. The surpluses were maintained inspite of the low revenue potential of
the war-crippled economy and high military expenditures because of insurgent activities.
The explanation for the large budget surpluses, during the years 1947 thru 1952,
lies in the large government receipts from the rice exports in a period of boyant world
market for rice.² With a system of fixed price paid to the cultivators for rice, and a state
monopoly of rice exports, the government was able to draw a third to half of its annual
revenue from foreign rice sales. A second contributing factor for the budget surpluses
was the relatively low level of developmental expenditures, during this period.
After the inauguration of the Eight Year Plan for economic development in 1952-53
government expenditures began to increase sharply. Along with large increase of expen-
ditures on capital formation expenditures on capital formation expenditures on social
welfare and current account (administrative) also witnessed sharp increases.
The Cash Consolidated Budget3 shows for the years 1954-55 to 1959-60 that the
domestic payments of the government have been in excess of government receipts in all
these years, thus, giving a cash deficit of approximately 680 million kyats on the average
every year.

and so did domestic receipts, domestic payments, and cash deficit. Between 1955-56 and

government liberalized its private import policy, and the excess of private imports over

caused a negative expenditure gap of K 208 million. The private money supply, hence,
should have fallen to K 208 million, but because of the increase in bank credit expansion

Gross domestic product, in current and constant prices, increased in 1956-57 but
diminished in 1957-58. Could these changes be related to the large expenditure gap in

Structure of the Burmese Economy and Inter-Sectoral Flows
A sectoral approach to the impact of the public expenditures requires identification
and analysis of at least the broad sectors of the economy. Inflationary pressures may arise
because of reasons which restrict flows of goods or incomes between the various sectors of
the economy and thus may cause inflation to be localized in some particular sectors where
money incomes rise.

Broadly speaking the economic structure of Burma is a dichotomy between
a large rural sector and a smaller urban sector covering a few large towns and
some smaller ones. The rural sector includes a subsistence production sector
whose demand for food and other goods is satisfied very largely within the sector through
a system of barter exchange. There is very little inter-dependence between this sector and
the urban sector. Hence neither monetary nor real flows between them are of any signifi-
The other part of the rural sector is the commercialized rural sector whose agricul-
tural production, largely rice, is oriented to the demand of the urban sector and the
foreign export sector. The output of this sector forms the single largest source of nation-
al income contributing about 40 per cent to the total output. As an item of export its rice
is again the largest, contributing approximately 70 per cent of the total exports during the
post World War II period. It is the contribution of this sector that makes Burma an
"export economy.' The demand of the commercialized rural sector, especially for food
products, is satisfied largely from production within the sector. A small portion of
demand is supplied by the output of the urban sector and by imports which also flow
through the urban sector. There is thus some inter-dependence between the commerciali-
zed rural sector and the urban sector.

Export Generated Inflation
In an export economy the export surplus forms the main determinant of national
income, and as such also the largest potential source of either inflation or deflation. In a
period of rising world prices, the expanding export proceeds increase national income and
prices and, though more slowly, the demand for imports. Because of the lag between
rising exports and induced imports, and the government's usual desire to build up foreign
exchange reserves during an export boom, the period of lag is generally marked by an
export surplus and mounting reserves. In a country, like Burma, where the domestic
money supply is based on foreign exchange holdings and can be legally expanded to some
multiple of these holdings, the export surplus may lead to a serious inflation. The domes-
tic currency equivalent, or a multiple, of these new exchange holdings, enters the economy
without being matched, at least in the short run, by any increase in domestic production.
The inflation may be further aggravated by import controls, as in Burma.
1
If inflation is to be avoided the domestic currency equivalent of the export surplus
must be sterilized either by a voluntary increase in savings or by a governmental taxation
and a budget surplus.2 In Burma the export surplus is absorbed most effectively by the
SAMB which drains off almost the whole of increased export earnings from rice. However,
it is still necessary for the government to sterlize the domestic currency counterpart of new
foreign exchange reserves which are now received by the SAMB. This counterpart is
transferred to the government through tax on the SAMB's income and rehabilitation con-
tribution. The SAMB in Burma thus serves as an effective institutional arrangement to
check an export generated inflation.
However, the SAMB receipts from rice exports, being made over to the government,
enter the government expenditures and create inflationary pressures.³ The introduction
of SAMB into the system merely changes the channel of money flow. The new export
earnings instead of flowing through the rural sector now flow through sectors wherein the
government makes the expenditures, which, in large part in the past, were made in the
urban sector.

The increased government expenditures in the urban sector create excess demand
since the elasticity of domestic supply there is low at least in the short run. In addition
the import controls restrict supplies from abroad. The prices in the urban sector rise,
and rise more sharply, for imported goods relative to goods of domestic production which
latter constitute mainly necessaries. The inflation is also largely localized in the urban
sector and does not spread out to the rural sector on account of not much interflow
between the two sectors. The urban sector's demand for rural output is relatively inelastic
because it is a demand for necessary goods. Besides, since the price of rice is fixed by the
SAMB the increased income of the urban sector does not flow out to the rural sector via
a higher price of rice.
The inflationary spread will be somewhat different if the government expenditures
are made more largely in the rural sector. The excess demand in the rural sector will
partially spread over to the "superior” goods of the urban sector and to imported goods.
In this case the dependence of the rural sector upon the urban sector would tend to
increase.
The SAMB policy of maintaining a fixed price of rice needs one further considera-
tion. The fixed price of rice which is weighted more heavily than other prices acts as a
strong stabilizer on the general price level. However, the fixed price may contribute to
inflation if it is much below parity with other prices,¹ and thereby adversely affect incen-
tives for larger output. If mobility between the various sectors is great, reduced incentives
in agricultural production may move labour to other sectors where its increased output
may offset the reduction in agricultural output and also the effect of the differential in
weighting of the two outputs. Thus, the general price level may remain the same. But
if such a mobility of labour and other resources between sectors does not exist, the reduced
incentives in agriculture might force a choice of liesure against work. If this happens, the
aggregate supply would diminish, and if the fall in aggregate supply is more than the fall
in aggregate demand it would contribute to inflation.

Sources of Finance for Public Capital Formation and Inflationary Impact
The sources of finance for capital formation may be listed as follow: (1) Current
budget surplus (2) Monetary expansion (3) Drawing down foreign exchange reserves (4)
Reparations and (5) Net foreign borrowing including foreign loans and free gifts and aids.
These five sources of finance may be classified into Current Finance to include current
budget surplus and monetary expansion; and into Non-Current Finance to include
drawings from foreign exchange reserves, reparations, and net foreign borrowings.
Out of the five sources of finance only the current budget surplus is non-
inflationary. The other four sources have varying degrees of inflationary impact. Monetary
expansion unmatched by growth of real output is purely inflationary. Drawing down of
foreign exchange reserves, reparations, and net foreign borrowings have both the inflatio-
nary and non-inflationary aspects. The proportion of these financial sources which is
spent on consumer imports is non-inflationary whereas the proportion which finances the
imports of capital goods has an inflationary impact in as much as domestic complementary
resources have to be financed in local currency. If these complementary resources can
not be financed out of current budgetary surplus recourse has to be taken to monetary
expansion which is inflationary.
The contribution of each source of finance may be examined from table 9 for
the
period 1952-53 to 1959-60. The total public capital expenditures have
been financed 62 percent by current finance out of which 22 percent has been
through money creation which is purely inflationary. The non-current finance
contributed 38 percent of the total capital expenditures. This source of finance again
has very high inflationary impact depending upon the ratio of domestic
complementary resources to foreign resources, which is something like 5:3 in Burma.
In conclusion, both the public capital expenditure pattern and the sources of finance
for capital formation had high inflationary impacts.

The average rate of increase in total expenditures has been 5.99 percent while that
of current expenditures has been 6.43 percent. This gives an average rate of increase in
capital expenditures of 5.55 percent. In Burma, the government expenditures are the
principal factor for the increase in money supply, which has been increasing at a much
higher rate (about 10 percent) than the increase in real GDP (4.6 percent), and therefore
largely contribute to inflation.
However, the interesting fact to note about the above ratios and rate of growth of
expenditures is that current expenditures have contributed more to inflation than capital
expenditures. This would suggest that for purposes of stabilization government policy
should be directed more to controlling current expenditures than restricting capital expen-
ditures. Capital expenditures, since they increase output are not so inflationary in their
impact as the current expenditures which do not contribute to output directly. However,
capital expenditures can be as inflationary because of production lags which occur between
the time when the expenditures are made and the output actually comes out in the market.
The policy of the government to reduce capital expenditures after 1955-56 is quite
understandable in view of the worsening position of the foreign exchange reserve then. A
prompt remedy was needed. Such a remedy was available in the curtailment of capital
expenditures, a large proportion of which was being made abroad to meet the foreign
costs of the capital program, and in the restriction of private imports. The government
applied both. Both these policies, however conducive they might have been for the
immediate improvement of foreign exchange position, so far as stabilization is concerned
have different effects.
Restriction of private imports leads to a reduction of surplus on private account
which, if taxation or non-bank borrowings are not increased correspondingly, would be
inflationary. As regards curtailment of capital expenditures it would be directly con-
tributory to stabilization, and yet, it may be inflationary if new production is, thereby,
reduced more than the reduction in expenditures.¹

whether Burma has a capital formation program or not.

the desirability and the possibilities of reducing and controlling the current expenditures,

Large amount of money, especially in the hands of certain business sections, and

foreign goods, import of which is restricted through an import license system.
Under such a situation the effect of enhancing the custom duties would be to
increase prices further rather fall on the seller or the importer as the conventional theory
of incidence would expect with inelastic supply curve. Two cases, one of consumer
imports in general, and the other durable consumer goods may be taken to illustrate the
point.
For consumer goods imports in general, the supply curve is perfectly inelastic at the
point of import. However, the supply curve is not perfectly inelastic below this point.
In other words the speculators can withdraw the supplies from the market and thus raise
the price further. The withdrawn supplies would be released slowly in the market. This sort
of speculation can be easily practised with advantage by the sellers if the restricted import
is much less than what the market demand at the original price would be during the
period between the first and the second issue of import License.
In the case of durable consumer goods there is one more consideration. The
enhancement of customs duty, especially if it is expected to be a permanent one, raises the
value of the durable good. The good becomes relatively a more valuable asset to hold.
In more technical language, the marginal effeciency of holding this asset increases. Apart
from this, durable consumer goods represent items of higher and modern standards of
living for which there is a growing demand from the educated and the city elite. The
increasing demand for such goods may be supported by liquidation of other now lesser
valuable assets to hold, by cutting down consumption, or by a fall in liquid holdings of
individual.
Conclusion
In conclusion, the inflationary pressure in Burma appeared in periods when large
expenditure gaps were created through excessive public expenditures and restriction of
imports. The expenditure gaps have been necessitated because of developmental and
other expanding administrative and welfare expenditures. The present revenue structure
of Burma is inadequate to siphon off the inflationary gap. Therefore, to control the in-
flationary pressure, especially in a development setting, the revenue structure needs to be
expanded and modified.

A significant feature of the inflationary is that it is largely localized in the urban
sector especially in the import goods market. This particular aspect of inflation is con-
tributed by the dichotomous character of the economy - a rural sector and an urban
sector-between which there is very little interflow of incomes and goods. The excess
demand generated by the public expenditures in the urban sector does not spread out
sufficiently to the rural sector. The spread-out would be more active from the rural
sector into the urban sector. Therefore, to spread the effect of income generation to the
whole economy a much larger share of public expenditures should be made in the rural
sector than has been done hitherto. The increasing rural incomes would induce invest-
ment expenditures in industry by the private sector, provided proper investment climate is
created.
The current expenditures of the government have been increasing at a much faster
rate than other expenditures, and current revenue. It is, therefore, highly desirable to
explore all possibilities of reducing and controlling the current expenditures. This would
not only mitigate inflationary pressure but also leave a larger current budgetary surplus
for capital expenditures.
New indirect taxes (or enhancement of rates) such as customs duties, sales taxes can
have price raising impacts. Therefore, a careful study of the impact of such measure on
the market should be made before introducing them. Such a study can only be made by
a thorough analysis of the existing pattern and behavior of the market and the likely
developments under the impact of proposed measures. Conventional text-book precepts
and theories cannot be much relied upon for policy matters as they are drawn under sim-
plified assumptions much removed from reality. They can be our guides provided we are
constantly aware of their limitations.
