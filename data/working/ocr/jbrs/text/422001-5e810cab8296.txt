A. THE MANUSCRIPT
by
Suzanne Karpeles*
National Archives of India possesses a manuscript with the title "Embassy
to Cochinchina. 1822 "which The Indian Government Record describes
as “a curious relation of an Embassy to Cochinchina, which would have been
sent in 1822 by the King of Ava. It also reveals numerous details concerning
the way of living of French people residing in this area. The Embassy was
composed of G. Gibson, head of mission, and a certain number of natives.”
There are still two other documents relative to the same manuscript.
One is The Journal of an Embassy to the Courts of Siam and Cochinchina by
John Crawfurd, London 1830, vol. 11, p 409 441, Appendix A. The
other is a letter from Captain Burney (Indian National Record, Foreign
Dept., Secret Proceedings vol. p 320 - 424, 1824, No. 17.)
-
John Crawfurd thought better to add “a succinct report in appen-
dix" to his book. "The original, written by Gibson, is full of grammatical
faults and mispellings, which makes the manuscript not only improper to
a transcription but almost unintelligible without his explanations and
personal commentaries." That is why John Crawfurd made a summary
of the manuscript, conserving as much as he could, the way of expressing of
the author.
The document from Burney reveals that it was on his request that
Gibson wrote his journal. One copy was given to Burney and he was
obliged to undertake a "very laborious task" of rewriting it entirely by
himself in order to send it to the "Honourable Governor". He added that
without his consulting Gibson every day, he would not have been sure of
what the latter really meant.
Looking attentively at the manuscript of the National Archives of India,
we can discern four different hand-writings. The figures marking the pages are
written with a pencil. The text is entirely written in ink, with corrections and

B. THE AUTHOR OF THE MANUSCRIPT
Up to now the few informations we have on Gibson come from the
Journal of Crawfurd (J. Crawfurd. op. cit Vol. 11, p. 410 - 411,), the letter
of Captain Burney, his papers (The Burney Papers, printed by order of the
Vajirañana National Library, Bangkok, 1910, Vol. p. 745) and from an
essay devoted to a French naturalist P.M. Diard.
According to these sources, Gibson's mother was from Madras,
and his father was an English man. Gibson himself lived for many years
in Burma where he occupied many important posts and had good knowledge
of geography, customs and languages of Burma. But one cannot say the
same of his knowledge in English. Yet, in spite of this, John Crawfurd
described him as "a person very perspicacious, knowing Portuguese, Hindus-
tani and Telougou."
According to Gibson's manuscript, we believed he went to Cochinchina
for the first time in 1789, where he met some influencial Frenchmen. Doctor
Diard, a French naturalist, was so fascinated by Gibson's great knowledge
of customs and people in India that they soon became friends, and Gibson
knowing nothing about the invasion of Burma by the British, invited his
friend, Dr. Diard, to accompany him to Pegu.
Captain Burney pointed out that Gibson understood well the
principles of geography "being often required by the King of Ava to draw
maps of the country." Moreover, in Burney's Papers, it was stated
that “Mr. Gibson's knowledge of the country allowed him to give the
most useful informations.”
Those appreciations made by the British, explained the reasons according
to which they assured themselves of the services of Gibson who served as
interpreter in the British Administration and died of cholera while the
British army was invading Prome.

1. The Collection of Laws and Customs of the Great South (Empire of
Vietnam)
(Extracts from the part devoted to the Ministry of Rites concerning
foreigners.)
During the fourth year of the reign of the Emperor Minh-Mang
(1823), Burmese messengers came to the citadel of Gia-Dinh with a Royaj
letter and presents from their king who wanted to offer his submission.
The Emperor Minh-Mang ordered: "having had a long trip by sea
those messengers must not be rejected inconsiderately. The governor of the
province of Gia-Dinh will receive them courteously and provide them with
lodging." The Emperor also authorized the Head of the delegation to receive
every month 10 ligatures, his assistants 8 ligatures each, the three members of
his suite 6 ligatures each, and 3 ligatures for each of the 40 servants. Moreover,
each of them received every month, a bushel of white rice. In his decree, the
Emperor declared: "the king of Burma has sent an Embassy to the province
of Gia-Dinh, with a Royal letter and many gifts to offer his submission.
The governor of the province of Gia-Dinh had the letter translated and
sent it over to me. I noted that the letter presented some improper terms.
In short, the letter made known that previously the Kingdom of Burma has
always been desiring to be in good terms with us, that twice during the reign
of the Emperor Gia-Long (1802-1819) an Embassy has been sent but
could not reach our country. Welcoming the news of my ascending the
throne, the king of Burma seized this opportunity to send this letter,
asking earnestly that we break our relations with Siam. This reminds me of
the time when my Father was still in the Kingdom of Siam of which the
previous King had once sent reinforcement troops to help my Father. But
Siamese officers were bad men. Everywhere they went they plundered. Our
nationals lost their confidence in them and soon these troops were attacked
and beaten by our enemies (Tay-Son). Since then, the brothers of the
previous King of Siam, being afraid of the power of the enemy, did not
send any more reinforcement troops.
My Father, gifted with wisdom and prudence, realized that it was useless
to ask for Siamese help, and on this very night, he decided to sail home by
sea. Later on, his suite followed him and he began to accept new partisans.
Everywhere he went, the enemy were beaten and he was victorious. Finally,

One decision from the Imperial Court authorized to offer compensation

Three pounds of cinnamon bark of Thanh Hoa.
Five pounds of cinnamon bark of Nghe Aw.
Ten pounds of cinnamon bark of Quang Nam.
One hundred pieces of gauze.
One hundred pieces of silk.
One hundred pieces of coloured silk.
One hundred pieces of raw silk.
One thousand pounds of sugar.
The Ambassador will receive a dragon embroidered satin dress, with a

dress and eighty ounces of silver; the five members of his suite will receive

ounces of silver.
A gold ring and a red precious stone are the only Burmese gifts accepted.
All other gifts will be sent back to Burma.
The day before their departure, the Governor of Gia-Dinh will receive
the Embassy and entertain them with a theatrical show. Moreover, he will
give them three month's travel allowance paid in cash and rice. Furthermore.
one officer with soldiers will escort them to their country.

The letter made known that up to now, their Kingdom has always been
desiring to establish friendly relations with our Empire. Twice during the
reign of Gia-Long (1802-1819), the Burmese Embassy failed to reach our
country. Greeting the good news of ascending the throne of Emperor
Minh-Mang, the king of Burma sent him a letter in which, he asked the
emperor to renounce his alliance with the Kingdom of Siam.
To discuss the matters, the Emperor summoned his ministers and
among them were Nguyen Duc Huyen and Tran Van Tinh. He said:
"Burma and Siam are mortal enemies. Now, we know why Burma is coming
to offer her respectful submission. We have already concluded an alliance with
Siam. If we also accept an alliance with Burma, certainly Siam will lose its
confidence in us. It would be preferable to give magnificent gifts to Burmese
messengers and send them back to their country."
Nguyen Huu Tan and Nguyen Van Hung did not agree:"for a certain
time we do not want to receive foreigners. But when they came, we always
treat them well. It's none of our business to know whether Siam and Burma
are enemies or not."

The Emperor declared: “I am thinking of my Father when he was a
refugee in Siam. The previous King of Siam, in order to help him, sent to him
reinforcement troops. But Siamese officers were bad men. Wherever they
went, they plundered. Our nationals lost their confidence in them. Beaten
by the enemy, Siamese officers ran away. Since then, being afraid of the power
of the enemy, they did not think any more of sending other reinforcement
troops. My Father gifted with wisdom and prudence knew for this reason that
time will come when he does not need any more help from Siam. He decided
then to sail home, called back his old partisans and welcomed new arrivals.
Everywhere he went he defeated the enemy who asked for submission and
finally he took over the whole Empire of Vietnam. Never did he borrow
from Siam a bow or an arrow. Not only the people and subjects of my
Empire recognized the power of Heaven (my Father) but even foreigners
paid tribute to his military ability. Yet my Father did not forget that Siam.
had been good to him and moreover, Siam is our neighbour. That is why
my Father authorized Siam to establish relations with us. I follow the ancient
rule; I will not listen to any other advice and, with my own will, I will not
break off our relations with Siam. If ever Siam accused us of some misdeeds,
there will be war. If Siam is right in waging war with us, she will be
victorious with the help of God. As to us, we shall have what we deserve.
In my opinion, we have no reason to denounce our alliance with Siam.
The Burmese proposition is unacceptable but, since those messengers have
been crossing the sea to come to our country, and since they have been
suffering from fire (Gibson: one of the vessel carrying the messengers
took fire), we must, in compensation. give presents to their King and
themselves."
GIFTS GIVEN TO THE MESSENGERS
Twenty three pounds of cinnamon, one hundred pieces of gauze, ordinary
silk, white silk, raw silk and one thousand pounds of sugar. One hundred
pounds of silver to the Head of the Delegation, eighty to his assistant,
and to both of them a dragon embroidered satin dress, and a belt; to
each of the five members who composed the suite sixty pounds of silver,
a black velvet dress and a belt; to each of the forty servants four pounds
of silver, a red wool dress and a belt.
A minister was assigned to write a reply to the King of Burma. Our
ministers asked the Emperor to accept a gold ring and a red precious stone
amongst the gifts sent by the king of Burma in order not to hurt those
messengers' feeling. The Emperor agreed. Our ministers Nguyen Van Uan
and Hoang Trung were assigned to escort the messengers back to their
country with our war ships and to provide them with six month's food and
expenses. After fulfilling their duty, they came home.
