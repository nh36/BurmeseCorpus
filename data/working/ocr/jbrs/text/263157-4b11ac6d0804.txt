Venous Patterns in the Superficial Cubital Region
of the Upper Limb in Orientals
It is well known that veins are the subject of variations mostly due to
developmental anomalies. Many of these variations are of academic interest
and a few are important to the clinician and the surgeon. Tropical
medicine gives a special interest to the various peculiarities of the cubital
veins partly on account of the enormous numbers of intravenous injections
and transfusions given and partly on account of the frequency with which
they become associated (primarily or secondarily) with tropical disease. In
view of the above it seemed worth while to examine a number of orientals to
discover (a) if there was any special distribution which was more common in
orientals (b) whether the knowledge obtained would indicate any change in
the intravenous technique.
108 students were examined. These were drawn from the Constituent
Colleges of the University of Rangoon. The following races
represented-Burmans, Chinese, Indians and Indo-Burmans.

METHOD
In each case the arm was well swung, then immersed in a tank of hot
water and then a band was tightly passed about the middle of the arm.
most cases the veins stood out well and classification was a matter of no
great difficulty.

There are four main types of distribution of the veins of the cubital
region (as described by Gray, 25th edition). No figures are available as to
which type is the more common in a particular race.
TYPE I
In type I the median antibrachial vein joins the basilic vein. The
cephalic vein continues upwards and gives out a tributary (median cubital
vein) to join the basilic above the last mentioned junction. Also the
accessory cephalic vein joins the cephalic vein in this type. Further additional
tributaries (un-named) pass between the cephalic and the basilic vein.
TYPE II
Instead of the antibrachial vein joining the basilic vein as in type I it
joins the median cubital vein. Usually there is no accessory cephalic vein
in this type.
TYPE III
This is practically the same as in type I except the accessory cephalic
vein is absent.

In this type the median antibrachial vein divides in the region of the
elbow into two tributaries, one (the median cephalic vein) joins the cephalic
vein and the other (the median basilic vein) joins the basilic vein. There is
no accessory cephalic vein present.
Other variations occur but generally speaking they involve (a) the
accessory cephalic vein which may be present or absent in any of the types
mentioned or (b) the median vein of the forearm may be absent and (c)
the smaller unimportant tributaries are liable to irregularities in number and
position.
INFORMATION OBTAINED AND ITS BEARING ON
INTRAVENOUS OPERATIONS
Type I. Having regard to the proximity of the brachial artery, the
lateral cutaneous nerve of the forearm and the inedial cutaneous nerve of the
forearm, it appears that the order of safety for injections is as follows (a)
basilic vein (b) cephalic vein (c) median cubital vein. In practice however
the median cubital vein is chosen as it is the most fixed and prominent.
With careful technique there is no danger but should the needle go too deep
the brachial artery may be pierced.
In type 11 the order of safety is (a) median antibrachial vein (b)
basilic vein (c) cephalic vein and (d) median cubital vein.
In type III the order of safety is (a) basilic vein (b) cephalic vein (c)
median antibrachial vein and (d) median cubital vein.
In type IV the order of safety is (a) basilic vein (b) cephalic vein (c)
median antibrachial vein (d) median cephalic vein and (e) median basilic
vein.

REMARKS AND CONCLUSION
The venous patterns of the superficial cubital region have been examined.
Variations occur and have been demonstrated. The commonest types in
these orientals are III and IV.
Certain well known dangers in intravenous operations have been
mentioned. The basilic vein is separated only from the brachial artery by
the lacertus fibrosus in its lower part and only by the thin deep fascia of the
arm in its upper part.
A study of the relations of the veins to neighbouring structures
demonstrates the proximity of two important cutaneous nerves (medial and
lateral cutaneous nerves of the forearm) and pressure on these nerves (apart
from irritative drug action on the tissues) explains the extreme pain resulting
from escape of fluid into the tissues during the operation of intravenous
injection. The investigation leads one to the conclusion that a careful
