NEOLOGISMS IN ENGLISH AND BURMESE*
Dr. K. Forbes §
Language develops and changes most
rapidly in the matter of vocabulary. New
words are continually being added to the
existing stock; many existing words ac-
quire new meanings; other words, less
noticeably, gradually fall out of use and
become obsolete. When new words enter
the vocabulary, or existing words under-
go semantic change they constitute neo-
logisms. This paper will be mainly con-
cerned with only one of these two pro-
cesses, namely the introduction of new
forms into the language. For the pur-
pose, I have used material as nearly syn-
chronous as possible, which seemed de-
sirable on general grounds, and especial-
ly because the types of neologism which
occur in any language, and the relative
frequency of one type or another, may
vary at different periods. My purpose was
first to present a general overall picture
of the ways in which the English and
Burmese vocabularies respectively are being
enlarged, and secondly to compare the
methods used in these two languages which
are unrelated and have very different pho-
nemic and morphemic systems. It will be
seen that the fundamental differences in
the character of the two languages has
affected also the types of neologism that
may occur in each.
While care has been taken to include
all the types of neologism which occur
in each of the two languages, the examples
given by way of illustration generally of
necessity only constitute a selection from
the total material collected, though the
relative frequency of various types has been
broadly indicated in the text. As far as
English is concerned, the ground has been
covered in a general way before. Therefore
the English material will be discussed more
briefly than the Burmese material, for I
know of no previous treatment of Burmese
neologisms.

1.1 Loanwords from other Modern
Languages
Although the English have at all periods
of their history been very prone to adopt
words from the languages of other peoples
with whom they came in contact, there
seem to be very few recent loans from other
modern languages. There is sputnik from
Russian, anorak from the Greenland word
for 'warm jacket', espresso from Italian,
couture from French, along with culottes
and assemblé. Most of the other recent loans
are terms denoting cultural institutions in
particular countries (e.g. Israeli kibbutz,
Russian komsomol) and are not really part
of the general vocabulary. Thus English
does not seem to have borrowed much from
other languages in the post-war period
this is a contrast with the large number of
loans which entered English at earlier
periods. The paucity of very recent borrow-
ings is rather to be expected in view the
present general political position of the two
major English-speaking countries, England
and America. Through the loss of her
colonies, England has lost much of the
close contact with other peoples which
would facilitate borrowing.

1. 3. Loantranslations
Loantranslations may involve either se-
mantic or morphological innovation
both at once. There may be only a se-
mantic innovation (semantic imitation) as
when in the Middle Ages English calling
acquired the meaning of "profession" after
the Late Latin vocatio which denoted both
the act of calling and secondly "profession".
Neologisms of this kind, which involve
only a semantic innovation, do not in a
sense concern us in this paper, but have
been mentioned as an example of another
less obvious but not less interesting and
important kind of foreign influence.

1.5. Onomatopoeia
In English onomatopoeic words are
marked by centain characteristic sound
patterns, which are more or less vaguely
felt to be pleasurable and suggestive of the
sense. New forms can be coined in accor-
dance with these patterns, and tend to be
readily accepted by others. For example,
by 1) repetition of whole syllables, or 2)
English onomatopoeia may be characterised
repetition of syllables with variation of one
vowel or consonant. There are a number
of recent neologisms with one of these two
patterns: e.g. type 1) housie-housie (a game),
the never-never (in the vulgar phrase 'to buy
on the never-never' i.e. on instalments);
type 2: fuddy-duddy (an old-fashioned per-
son), boogie-woogie (a dance), walkie-talkie
(sc. radio, colloquial for 'a portable radio-
telephonic transmitting and receiving set'),
hifi (an abbreviation of 'high fidelity'), ping-
pong (a tradename now widely used as a
synonym for tabletennis, morphologically
a reduplicated form based on the stem ping,
and comparable to sing-song). These recent-
ly coined onomatopoeia show that this is
still a living type of formation in modern
English. Such forms are particularly
characteristic of popular speech, and are
not found among new learned compounds..
