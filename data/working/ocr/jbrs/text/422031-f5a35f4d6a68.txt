INTRODUCTION
I. HISTORICAL BACKGROUND
Historically speaking the Karen people of Burma were discovered by
Western scholars only recently. They remained more or less unknown until
American Missionaries discovered them by the first quarter of the 19th
Century. Then because of their ready acceptance of Christianity in the hands.
of American Missionaries they came to be known throughout the Christian
world. Today Karen is a name known to apply to several distinct tribes
Burma united by a bond of having a common language. Within this racial
group are three main tribes namely, Bghai, Pwo and Sgaw. Under each main
tribe are again many smaller groups, speaking differing dialects.
From available evidence modern scholars incline to the view that the Ka-
rens are not aborigines, though they entered Burma earlier than the Burmans
and the Shans, but later than the Mons. It is generally believed that some-
where in northern China was their original home from which, they gradually
moved southwards into Burma. They did not come in great hosts, but rather
trickled in through the cracks and fissures of the physical and probably
human barriers surrounding the Country in the North-east.
Most of the Western scholars were under the impression that the Karens
were severely oppressed by their superiors and that they were totally dependent
upon the despotic government of the Burmans. Actually, under certain despot-
ic Burman Kings, the Karens were not particularly oppressed; the oppression
they suffered, such as heavy taxation and forced labour, was general
throughout the country, and affected the Burman and Mon population as well.
It might be that in certain administrative areas, however, the local officers
for personal gains would be more oppressive than the others.

Karen population rose to 1.3 million in 1931, an increase of approximately one
million in 60 years or roughly 50% in every decade. This high rate of increase
could not be due to natural causes alone; part of the increase should be
attributed to larger area covered by the successive censuses and also to more
complete enumeration from one census year to another which eventually
increased the accumulated total. But it is remarkable to notice that during
the period under review the Karens were able to maintain the same proportion
in relation to the country's total, which means that the rate of increase of the
Karen people tended to be proportional to the increase for the whole country.
Although no complete census was taken after the Second World War
it seems quite reasonable to assume that the rate of increase for the Karen
population remain roughly the same as the pre-war period. The estimated
total population of the Karens in the present decade would be approximately
between 1 and 2 million.
In general distribution the Karens are found to be limited to lower Burma,
south of 20 N latitude. They are unevenly distributed; the highest concen-
tration in terms of proportion are found in the hilly areas bordering the
southeastern frontier of Burma, while at the same time they are also found
in appreciable numbers over the lowlands of lower Burma, forming minori-
ties. Except for Thaton and Salween districts they do not form a majority
in any of the remaining districts of Burma. From the Karen hills their propor-
tion decreases southwards down the Tenasserim coast and also westwards
into the Irrawaddy delta. Along the Valley of the Sittang the Karens are
found as far north as Yamethin district while along the valley of the Irrawaddy
they are found as far as Prome district. Towards the south they are found
in appreciable numbers as far as the southern most district of Mergui, which
seems to be the southern limit of their distribution. Eastwards, however,
they are found beyond the boundary of Burma in northern Thailand. Of the
three main tribes the Bghais are still confined to the hilly areas east of Toungoo
district, whereas the majority of the Pwo and Sgaw tribes are found on the
plains of lower Burma. On the whole the Karen population is predominantly
rural in character, and in spite of living in mixed villages, they tend to confine
to their own quarters or "Asus" and therefore they fail to amalgamate
completely with the Burmans.

III. HISTORY OF THE FORMATION OF THE KAREN STATE
Before the British rule in Burma the Karens never seemed to have
developed any political organisation of their own other than the village.
Under the British rule, however, they endeavoured to unite themselves into
a national group quite early in the history of their development. Partly owing
to the necessity for security and partly fearing a division between the Christians
and non-Christians, representatives from different places met and formed the
Karen National Association known as "Daw K' Lu", meaning the whole
nation. The basic aim of this association was to consolidate the Karen people
through the promotion of the economic and educational interests of the
people. Later, however, this association became the official organ of the
Karen people. Although the initial progress of the Karens in the field of
organisation was very promising, in the long run they could not keep pace
with the majority group. Nevertheless they cooperated with the Burmans

and under communal representation were represented in the British Burma
government. During the British regime they did not develop any political
party of their own, the Karen National Association was more or less the
official body representing the Karen people.
During the Second World War, the Burma Independent Army, while
trying to set up civil administration, came into clash with the Karens in
Myaungmya and Papun areas. These minor clashes between the two racial
groups did a lot of harm to the Burmo-Karen relationship. Apart from these
minor clashes, however, the Japanese regime passed away quite peacefully.
When the War came to an end and the British returned, the Karen National
Association, which undergone a change of name to the Karen National
Union, refused to accept the Attlee-Aung San agreement and boycotted the
the Constituent Assembly convened to draw up a constitution for Burma.
But a new political group known as the Karen Youth league decided to co-
operate with the Burmese and filled up the scats allotted to the Karens in the
Constituent Assembly. Although the constitution drawn up by the Assembly
included provisions for the formation of a Karen State. the majority of Karens
under the Karen National Union were not satisfied with the conditions
attached. The K.N.U. later demanded a Karen State to include not only the
Irrawaddy and Tenasserim divisions but also Insein and Nyaunglebin
subdivisions. The areas demanded were too much for the majority group
to concede. Nevertheless the Karens under K.N.U. stood by their demands
and tried to consolidate their positions. This created misunderstandings
and later tension arose which eventually precipitated an insurrection in
December 1948. After a lapse of about four years the Constitutional
Amendment Act was passed and eventually the Karen State came into
being comprising the Salween district and Thandaung, Pa-an, Hlaingbwe,
Kawkareik and Kya-in townships.
IV. HOW FAR COULD THE KAREN STATE EXTEND?
Since the Karen State embraces only 1 of the total Karen population
within Burma one of the probable solutions is to consider whether the State
could possibly be enlarged to include the remaining Karens left outside.
Except for Thaton and Einme townships (Myaungmya district) all the
areas having Karen majority have already been included within the State.
But there are areas with substantial Karen population laying adjacent to the
Karen State boundary. The areas east of the Sittang in Kyaukkyi and Shwe-
gyin townships have substantial Karen population: except for urban areas
and settlements near the river the population in the remaining areas is predom-
inantly Karen. Therefore the interior areas east of the Sittang should be
considered for inclusion in the Karen State. Further south, since the popula-
tion east of Martaban range in Thaton district is predominantly Karen,

VI. HOW DOES THE PROBLEM ARISE?
The general opinion of the Burmans would attribute the Karen problem
mainly to British rule. In other words they would attribute the Karen question
mainly to the "divide and rule" policy of the British. Even though the British
Government might not deliberately create divisions among the natives with
a view to playing off one group against the other, its rule brought about
changes which tended in that direction. During the British rule the Karens
were uplifted and they developed into a strong national group. This brought
them into direct competition with the Burmans in all the fields of human
activities.

When the British came into Burma there was a clash of political interests
between the Burmans and the Karens. The Burmans regarded the British as
imperialists trying to deprive them of their freedom, whereas the Karens
regarded them as liberators from Burman oppression. The help given by the
Karens to the British fighting against the Burmans and the retaliation by the
Burmans on the Karens created a new enmity between the two racial groups.
One of the factors which hindered friendly relationships between the
Burmans and the Karens is because they still remember too much of history.
Some of the Karens still look back to the days when their ancestors suffered
persecution from their former rulers, which hinders them from associating
with the Burmans of today. Though the underlying cause of the Karen
question may lie deep in history the present problem, relating to their dissatis-
faction over political arrangement, is largely the result of the difficult situation
brought about by the geographical distribution of the Karen people. Unlike
the other minorities, such as the Chins or Kachins, the Karens do not reside
in an area which they might call their own, but they live side by side with the
Burmans in most of the districts of lower Burma. Yet they retain their own
language and racial individuality and would like to have a state of their own
covering a greater part of lower Burma.
.
Instead of intermingling with the Burmans, if they had confined themselves
to a geographical area of their own, the problem of delimiting a

them might not have arisen. On the other hand, even though they have lived
side by side with the Burmans, if they could have submerged their racial
individuality and allowed themselves to assimilate with the majority group,
the desire for a separate state might not have arisen.
CONCLUSIONS
The actual problem now is that the Karen people are not satisfied
with the present political arrangement, which provides them with a small
state. On the other hand, the Central Government is willing only to the
extent of providing a state to include areas with Karen majority, adjacent to
the Salween district, on the condition that in choosing a state the Karens
forego the minority rights, namely special representation in Parliament.
Having chosen the state, three-fourths of the total Karen population, now
residing outside the Karen State, have no Karen representation in Parliament.
The privilege of having a state is enjoyed by only one-fourth of the total
Karen population of Burma. This political arrangement is, therefore, not
compatible with the geographical distribution of the Karen people. So what
would be the best association or arrangement that could provide all the
Karens with special minority rights in the Union and within the limits of the
Constitution.

History has taught the Karens to distrust the Burmans, and to be cautious
in their association with them. Mainly to safe-guard themselves against
oppression or exploitation by their more advanced neighbours, they wanted
a territory of their own with the power to look after their own interests and
manage their own affairs. The desire to have a state of their own may be justi-
fied by the reasons given, but the Karens have to remember that the facts of
geography link them with the Burmans. They cannot escape from the reality
that they are living side by side with the Burmans. In such circumstances it is
not feasible to carve out a separate territory for them including all the Karens.
Although no clear-cut solution to such a problem is possible, certain
adjustments could be made which might be advantageous to both the racial
groups and thereby ease the tension between them. The present political
arrangement is not beneficial to all the Karens, while at the same time the
Central Government is not willing to grant further concessions, save within
the limits of the Constitution. Arrangements should, therefore, be made to
enable all the Karens to enjoy equal privileges. Probably the best arrangement
for benefitting all the Karens, would be on the model devised for the Chins.
Instead of a state, like the Chin Hills Division, the Karens' areas would be
retained as a Karen special division. This arrangement would not only
safeguard the hill areas, inhabited by the Karens, against exploitation by
their more advanced neighbours, but also retain minority rights for the
Karens in the Union.
Except for the giving up of the state, which in reality is no more than
surrending a dignified name, the Karens as a whole would enjoy many
advantages from such an arrangement. Technically the Karen people will lose.
nothing except legislative rights by giving up the state. As certain fundamental
rights of the minority peoples are safeguarded, it is not likely that the Karen
would be subjected to exploitation, even if they had no legislative rights.
One of the advantages is that Karens would have greater representation in the
Union Parliament. At present only twenty two Karen members represent the
Karen people in the Parliament. Before the formation of the Karen State,
under minority rights forty four Karen members represent the Karen. If
the State were given up for Special Division, this privilege would be restored.
In that case both the Karens of the Tenasserim Division and those of the
Delta would be represented in Parliament by Karens. More representation
might lend more weight to the expression of their views or their demand for
special privileges in Parliament. Besides better representation, the internal
affairs of the Karen areas, especially local administratation, schools and
cultural institutions, would be in charge of a Karen cabinet minister. It would
be his duty to protect the backward Karens from exploitation by their more
advanced neighbours.

Another advantage would be the better chance of developing the Karen
areas. The revenue collected in the present Karen State together with the
contribution from the Central Government is just enough to run the admini-
stration of the State. So the Karen State is not in a position to finance any new
project to develop the area. If the Karen State were relegated to a special
division, then all the expenditures would have to be borne by the Central
Government. As the Union Government is in a stronger financial position.
than the Karen State, there would surely be a better chance of developing
the Karen areas.
Actually most of the Karens living at present in the Karen State are not
advanced or politically minded enough to know the present situation of the
country. To them it makes no difference whether they are living in a Karen
state or in a special Karen division.So long as they are not interrupted in their
daily occupations, they are not likely to take heed of the development going
on in the country. Still practising primitive agriculture, they spend most of
their time in their “taungyas", trying to get enough food to last them until
the next harvest. It is of extreme importance, however, that the local officers
in charge of the administration should understand their ways of life and
culture. If they were under a special division, a Karen minister would be in
charge of the internal affairs, and he would see that only local officers, able to
understand the Karen people, were appointed. In such circumstances, there-
fore, it is not likely that the Karens living in the present Karen State would
object to the arrangement.
If the Karens, because of an extreme nationalistic attitude, were reluc-
tant to give up the idea of having a separate state of their own, then the next
best alternative would be to retain the State and enlarge it as far as possible
so as to make it more viable. The possibility of extension of the Karen
State to adjacent areas was found to be limited to some of the interior areas of
Toungoo, Thaton, Amherst districts. If the suggested arrangement were
carried out to include these areas most of the Karen areas in the Tenasserim
division would be included, and possibly more revenues for the government
would be provided. Even then it is still doubtful whether sufficient revenues
could be collected to bring about a budget surplus adequate to finance
further developments. Nevertheless, this alternative arrangement would
be better than the present arrangement, where some of the Karen areas
are outside the State.

half of the total Karen population of Burma would still be left outside the
“enlarged " Karen State. Nevertheless, such a bold concession would provide
unmistakable evidence of the sincerity of the Burmans' desire to give the
Karens their full share in the national life, and would help to win the confi-
dence of the Karens.
Finally, it is regrettable that the Central Government is not prepared to
grant statehood as well as minority rights to the Karens. If the Central
Government were to be liberal as to provide the Karens living outside the
Karen State with minority rights, and at the same time retain the present
state, such a concession would help greatly towards the solution of the
present Karen problem. Although it might not fulfil the aspirations of the
Karens, it would be the nearest approach that the Central Government could
make towards to fulfilling the demands of the Karens. But some of the Bur-
man leaders dislike the idea that the Karens in the Delta should have special

So long as the Karens remain dissatisfied they are a potential danger to
the peace of the Union. The Karen people are by nature peaceful, but
under certain circumstances they can cause a great deal of trouble, such as, for
example, the insurrection in 1949. When no arrangement was made by the
British Government to provide them with a State, and when the State provided
by the Burmans was not up to their expectation, they started an insurrection.
Though most of them gave up the idea of achieving a state by force of arms,
there is still a good deal of dissatisfaction with the present arrangement.
A few of them even remained “underground" and refused to accept the
authority of the Union Government. There are reports of their collabora-
tion with the Communist group, who are also fighting the Government. This
may be either the personal preference of the individual leader or a conven-
ient arrangement as a last resort in the fight against the Government. At
present the Karen people as a whole are not inclined towards Communism.
But there is a danger that they may become so in the future, if they are
promised a bigger state or a better concession by the Communist group.
According to Saw Tha Din the Karens perfected communism years ago,
but they did not find it practicable.
