I. Languages of the Negritoes All are
precious, for they still exist. In the
Philippines, though there are plenty
of Negritoes, all their languages,
I believe, are lost. On the Burma side
we have ANDAMANESE on the islands,
and SEMANG (barely 500 miles dis-
tant) on the mainland. My old teacher,
Dr. C. O. Blagden' could find little
or nothing in common between these
two sets of languages. He knew Semang
au fond. Andamanese, though spoken
only a hundred miles North of Car
Nicobar, shows no sign of Austroasiatic
influence. Contrast Semang, which is
full of it, while still remaining an
independent language. - The inference
is that the Andamanese reached their
islands, before the Mon-Khmers (as
a result of discovering the technique
of Wet Rice Cultivation) swarmed all
over the deltas of Further India from
Tongking. This happened (I guess)
about the 3rd millenium B.C. At that
time Negritoes must have been wide-
spread in Burma. Chinese texts of
the 8th-9th century A.D. still speak
of them near Mogaung in the North
of Burma, as well as on pirate islands
North of the Straits of Malacca; and
tales of them are still rife among the
Nagas and other tribes on the Burma--
India and East Pakistan borders.
Negrito features are still noted among
the Hill Karens. A number of words

Late in the 2nd millenium B.C.
(the date is a guess), the Southern
YÜEH (=VIÊT), under pressure from
the north and east, drove out the
Malayans from their coastal homes
in South China, and overran the rice
lands of Tongking. This caused a large
exodus of the Wet Rice-Cultivators
up the Red River to its source, and
across the north of Burma into Upper
Assam, where the KHASI settled in
the Brahmaputra valley; while the
main body of MUNDA moved on to
grow their rice in the Gangetic Delta.
Subsequent invasions-of the Nagas
and Tibeto-Burmans from the North
East, and of the Indo-Aryans from
the North West-have pushed both
Mundas and Khasis south into the
hills.

III. Indonesian languages - From about 500
B.C. to 500 A.D., after their expulsion
from the coasts East of Tongking, the
MALAYAN peoples took to their
ships and became the Sea-Vikings of
the South, raiding and settling where
they could on the coasts or islands
between the mouths of the Red River
and the Irawady (not to mention
Madagascar). They excelled in metal
work: the words for Gold and Silver in
Khmer etc., and for Iron in Mon and
other Burma languages, are Malay.

guages, of course, are distantly allied:
but apart from this, there are

KAREN, which forms a language
family by itself, had its old home in
the Southern jungle-covered hills
between the Southern and the Northern
Mon Khmers. The Hill Karens were
influenced by both, especially the
Southern Mon-Khmers. But also
they caught the first impact of the
Tibeto-Burman invasion from the
north, about the beginning of our era.
As a result, Karen is now a mixed, but
highly organized language: half Pre-
Tibeto-Burman, one third Tibeto-
Burman, plus an important admixture
of Mon-Khmer.

IV. Pre Tibeto-Burman or Mixed Lan-
guages The MRO or MRU of North
Arakan (not to be confused with the
Nomads of Tibet. Most of them were
squeezed south into Ssu-Ch'uan (up
the T'ao and down the Min Rivers,
East of the Amne Machin Range). But
some broke west along the Nam-shan
Mts. north of Tibet. So possible
contacts between South and West
cannot be ruled out. I think it was
the great height and barrenness of
the North East Tibetan plateau beyond
the Ko-Ko Nor which forced those
remarkable people of the T'ao Valley
and Pan Shan hills to move, when
they had to move, either west or
south (The Chinese Hou - Lan - Shu
lends some support to this view).
The Burmese language itself is rather
nearer to Classical Tibetan than to
Archaic Chinese; but is pretty close
in basic vocabulary to both. The
tail end of the south migration, still
called CH'IANG, is now North West
of Chêng tu, the capital of Ssu -
Ch'uan. From that point a variety
of escape routes were possible: (i)

In language the connection of the
Burma and Lolo Groups is so close
that they may even be called one
group, connected not only by common
vocabulary (which is largely true of
all the Groups, Sak, Chin, Kachin
and even Karen), but also by a con-
siderable degree of tonal corresponden-
ce (which cannot be said of the other
Groups). Many of these Tibeto-Chi-
nese languages today are monosyllabic,
and at first sight invariable, dead
stumps of sound. But the deeper they
are studied in historical depth - and
this applies even to Chinese, the more
signs of life appear, especially in their
pronouns and verbs. Professor Eugé-
niè Henderson's book on Tiddim Chin'
proves how similar, in grammar
if not in vocabulary, even the Tiddim
Colloquial language is to Indo-Ger-
manic, with its conjugations, moods,
tenses, pronominal suffixis to verbs
etc. Much the same applies to all
the northern dialects of Chin. And
such ‘Pronominalized groups' extend
east across Burma to Yünnan, and
west all along the line of Himala-
yan languages.
