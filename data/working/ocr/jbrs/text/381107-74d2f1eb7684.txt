Introduction
All over the world there are many regions where the widespread
depletion of forests has led to soil erosion, encroachment of deserts and
the resultant distress of the people living in the area. Burma is now
taking active steps to combat such conditions in the dry zone area of
Central Burma and the Agricultural and Rural Development Corpora-
hensive programme to stop the cutting down of the forests and to
encourage the reforestation of previously wooded countryside. This
will prevent soil erosion, conserve rainfall and thus put an end to the
gradual conversion of good crop growing lands into deserts. The
Forest Department is strongly supporting this reforestation and the
Union of Burma Applied Research Institude is also helping in this
important programme.
To promote this reforestation scheme we must introduce new
sources of heat energy for cooking (domestic and industrial); other-
wise the present sparse timber growth and large trees will be quickly
cut down for fuel.
The dry zone area villages have been using mainly wood and its
product charcoal for fuel to produce heat energy for domestic cooking
and semi-industrial use such as palm jaggery making, since time
immemorial. Unhindered use of wood naturally cuts down the forests.
Lately the use of petroleum oil and its products kerosene, diesel
oil, and petrol has come about to some extent but still to only a small
degree for lighting and almost nil for cooking.
The reason is that labour has in the past been cheap and the habit
of the villager is to go to the forest, cut down a few trees for firewood
which he considers free, rather than buy a kerosene stove and use
kerosene fuel for cooking his food or for commercial uses.

This habit has become so ingrained in the villager that even if he
had a kerosene stove he probably would not buy kerosene but would
try to get fire-wood in spite of the extra labour in cutting and splitting
the wood, the smoke nuisance, and the inconvenience in removing the
soot from the pots. So in order to prohibit the cutting of firewood,
the alternative means of cooking must be free, that is, the running
expenses must be nil. Solar Energy does satisfy this condition provided
the converter used is of suitable design.
The Properties of Solar Energy
All the time that the sun shines brightly, the land surface of
Burma receives solar energy per second equivalent to two hundred and
forty thousands million kilowatts from the sun's rays. This is a
tremendous amount of power, more than all the combined power
available from the hydroelectric and coal reserves in the country.
Of course we cannot hope to utilise all of this power as a lot of it
falls on inaccessible areas. But to take just one area, say the Central
Burma Dry Zone, we estimate that every second of a typical sunny day,
over one thousand million kilowatts of solar energy falls on the thousand
square miles around Mount Popa (that is an area about 30 miles long
by 30 miles wide).
What becomes of this huge amount of energy? A portion is
utilised by the plants, trees and grass to convert carbon dioxide of the
air into vegetable nutrition through chlorophyll, a portion evaporates
water from the rivers, streams and ponds in the area and the rest goes
to heat up the soil and the air. So here is a vast source of energy up
to now wasted by the Burmese except for a very minute fraction which
is used to dry saltfish and so on.
It means that the people living in that area can have up to a
million kilowatts of power doing work for them if they only
utilise one thousandth of the sun's rays pouring down on them. In
other words, they don't need to harness the sun's rays over the whole
thousand square miles of their territory, they just need to efficiently
harness the sun's rays over just one square mile to get a million kilo-
watts of solar energy coming in to do some work.

Now when we remember that surveyors have to spend months in
remote jungles trying to find the waterfall to yield just a few thousand
kilowatts of water power, it seems unbelievable that right where we live,
any square mile can give us a million kilowatts of solar power. Why
has'nt more use been made of the sun's rays?
The reason for the non-utilisation of solar energy is that the
normal heating power of the sun's rays is insufficient to boil water.
From the general point of view this is a very fortunate fact, otherwise
we all would be dead. But for the purpose of cooking food or provid-
ing power for cottage industries or for running engines, the boiling of
water is a requisite.
People have not used solar energy for so long that they have even
reached the state of mind that it is useless. Some scientists who are
unaware of the most modern developments also share this view. How-
ever, this is not correct, because we now know that solar energy can be
very useful if we go about it the right way.
Let us consider first a few figures. The intensity of solar
radiation half way between the earth and the sun is 1.94 gram calories
per square centimeter per minute which is equivalent to 1.35 kilowatts
per square meter.
According to Ramdas and Yegnanarayanan the average value for
India is 18,900,000,000 gram calories per day per acre, which works out
to about half kilowatt per square meter. This average value agrees
with the maximum intensity reached with the sun at the Zenith on a
cloudless day of about 1 kilowatt per square meter, or 1.4 gram calories
per square centimeter per minute. These values have also been
measured in Burma.
Now it is found that in order to boil water in an ordinary domestic
pot placed without any protection in the wind, a heat intensity of about
8.5 gram calories per square centimeter per minute equal to 6 kilowatts
per square meter is required. Hence it is seen that some sort of
concentrating device will be needed to enable the sun's rays to boil
water.

They have produced many practical devices to either absorb the
solar energy for heating of domestic water supplies and air conditioning
systems or to reflect and concentrate the sun's rays to boil water and
produce heat for other purposes. The boiling of water can be utilised
for making beverages, cooking food or for generating steam.
A review of these inventions reveals that they are not cheap
enough for widespread use by rural populations. So the writer has
designed and developed at the State Industrial Research Institute (Now
Union of Burma Applied Research Institute) a solar energy converter
which had as its objectives maximum simplicity and low cost. It has
been made by a local carpenter and can be duplicated by any village
carpenter within a few hours. The cost of a unit with two square feet
of surface area is less than four kyats. To boil a pot with about 9 tea
cups of water (2000 ccs) about 20 square feet of surface area is needed.
Thus a practical cooker would cost about thirty five kyats and together
with various accessories to improve the efficiency, it would cost about
forty kyats. With a useful life around 1500 hours, the cost of boiling
a pot of water would work out to 2.5 pyas. This may be compared
with the cost of 3 pyas using cheap wood fuel and 3.6 pyas using a
kerosene stove to boil the same amount of water.
Design
The basic idea of the solar cooker is of course to focus the sun's
rays to one spot and increase its intensity from ½ KW per square meter
to over 6 KW per square meter.
From our coordinate geometry course in mathematics we learn that
there is a curve called the parabola, whose formula is usually given as
y² = 4 ax
which has the property that any parallel beam of rays falling on it is
reflected back to one single spot. This property of the parabola, has
been utilised in the reverse direction in searchlights and motor car
headlamps to produce a parallel beam from a point source of lights.
The reflector of these searchlights is termed to be a paraboloid, which is
a surface obtained by rotating a parabola around its axis.

Now in designing our solar cooker, we set out to get a paraboloid
to focus the sun's rays but the fabrication of a true paraboloid is an
expensive matter and so the practical solution was to use a number of
vertical parabolas set along a horizontal parabola on the ground. The
method now used is to make wooden frames about 4 feet by 1 foot
wide, which are cut to the shape of parabolic curves along their length.
Over each of these frames a bright tinned plate (4 feet by 1 foct) is
nailed down and we get a metallic parabolic reflector. A number of
these are placed along a parabola on the ground and we can then get
the sun's rays concentrated on to a pot placed at the focus. The
distance of the focus from the reflector can be chosen by means of the
constant "a" in the above formula for the parabola.
Thus a cheap and simple device for utilising solar energy for
cooking (both domestic and industrial) is now available. It is so simple
that any village carpenter can make it if shown a model and the cost is
the lowest of any solar energy converter in the world. So Burma is at
present leading in the research on popular solar energy utilisation by
the masses.
Conclusion
Our objective in carrying out this new development on the solar
converter has been to produce a practical device which would fill the
need for a cheap and effective alternative to the use of wood fuel which
was leading to deforestation.
It is felt that for the problem at hand this simple, unpretensious
device is a more satisfying solution than some weird, complicated
apparatus which would not only cost a lot more but also have more
chance of going wrong due to excessive mechanical elaborations.
