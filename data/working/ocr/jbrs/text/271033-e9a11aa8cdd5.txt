THE COMMERCIAL TREATY OF 1862.*
On the 15th April 1862 Lieutenant-Colonel Phayre, Chief
Commissioner of British Burma, addressed the Government of
India on the subject of relations with the Burmese Court. There
were several questions on which an understanding with that Court
was desirable; and of these the most immediate was the question of
customs duties on the land-frontier between British Burma and
Upper Burma. The conclusion of the Second Burmese War had
left trade between Upper and Lower Burma completely at a
standstill, a blockade having been established on the Irrawaddy by
the British authorities. This blockade was lifted in June 1853 and
trade began to revive. In consequence the Government of India,
by Act XXX of 1854, had imposed an import duty of ten per cent
ad valorem on all imports from Upper Burma except such articles
as raw cotton and livestock which were needed in Lower Burma;
and an export duty had also been instituted on a few articles such
as salt and paddy which were in demand in Upper Burma. In the
year 1861-62 these frontier import-duties amounted to Rs. 3,00,344,
and the export duties to Rs. 2,71,410, making a total of Rs. 5,71,754.
These duties had been imposed partly for revenue purposes but
also partly for the sake of having a customs house at which any
export of arms and materials of war to Upper Burma could be
checked. On the score of revenue, Phayre was of opinion that the
duties were no longer justified; the revenues of Pegu were increas-
ing, a total of Rs. 46,51,938 in 1859-60 having increased to
Rs. 54,50,000 in 1861-62; and thus the frontier duties could be
dispensed with without embarrassment. Moreover, taxation in Pegu
was heavy; "I have hitherto made Pegu pay every farthing she
can", declared Phayre, "and I do desire to see some relaxation.
Pegu pays more than any other province in India in proportion to
her population, and with such a strain upon her, the abolition of
the frontier customs has become absolutely necessary." If it
proved impossible to reduce taxation in this way, some of the
direct taxes ought to be reduced. Trade, moreover, would benefit
by the abolition of the customs; and the removal would be
advantageous to the trade of the Burmese Kingdom also. In view
of this last consideration it might be possible to arrange a mutual
reduction of customs duties and to effect at the same time other
measures for the encouragement of commerce. Another point
which Phayre raised was the question whether trade could be
opened up overland between Rangoon and Yunnan; and there was

The Government of India replied that the proposal to visit
Mandalay was approved ; the object of the visit, which should be
effected in an unostentatious way as Phayre himself had suggested,
should be to conclude a commercial treaty relating to the following
points the abolition of the frontier import and export duties on

explore the caravan route to Yunnan via Bhamo, the hope of an

continually haunted the minds of English merchants in the middle
nineteenth century; free intercourse for British merchants by that
route, with permission to place agents at Bhamo; free passage for
Chinese labourers through Burma ; and free passage for opium
from British territory into Yunnan ; in return for these concessions
the Government of India would abolish its frontier duties entirely,
and would allow goods to enter Rangoon at only one-eighth of the
normal customs duties if intended for re-export to Upper Burma.
Further, if Phayre thought fit, he might agree to the withdrawal of
the existing prohibition of the export of arms to Upper Burma,
since the Burmese would always manage to get arms somehow if
they really wanted them, and it was better that they should be
obtained via British Burma where the imports conld be observed,
than by any other route.

received a friendly welcome from the principal Wungyis ; but his

Magwe Mingyi, with whom he had had official correspondence in the
past, was temporarily out of favour, and that the Pagan Mingyi
had the King's ear. However, Father Abbona, an Italian mis-
sionary who had been many years in Burma and had acted as a go-

who had some influence with the King, was well disposed towards
the idea of a commercial treaty ; and Bishop Bigandet, who had

for a personal interview with the King, and an audience was
granted on the 16th. There was no discussion of business; the King
gave Phayre a courteous reception and spoke at length on the benefits
of friendship; he said that he was having some of his sons taught
French and English, and brought forward one of his children, a boy
of eight years, who was learning Hindustani. On the 22nd Phayre
had a private audience, only Bishop Bigandet, Father Abbona,
Edwards, and two ministers being present. It seemed that consi-
derable influence had been brought to bear on the King, mainly by
the Einshemin, to prejudice him against a treaty of any sort, and
no decision was arrived at on that day Negotiations continued,
the King vacillating from day to day; for example, he appointed a
day for a further interview with Phayre, and then at the last

stipulated that the proposed clause preventing the Burmese Govern-

would give his people the impression that a concession had been
forced from him and so would humiliate him in their eyes; he
also objected on religious grounds to any mention of opium; and he
further demanded time in which to abolish the customs dues.
Phayre agreed to accept these objections provided that the King

The main terms of the treaty were as follows: The first clause
merely provided formally for the continuance of the existing
friendship between the contracting parties. The second and third
articles provided for freedom of passage and for mutual protection
of traders and other subjects. The fourth article provided that
imported goods passing through British Burma en route for Upper
Burma should be liable to a tax of one per cent only in Rangoon,
and if intended for further export through Upper Burma into
other territories should be liable to no tax at all by the Burmese
Government at the Burmese British frontier. Similarly Article V
provided that goods brought into Upper Burma overland for
export to Pegu should be liable to a Burmese customs duty of one
per cent only and if intended for further export by sea should be
liable to no duty on the part of the British at the frontier; though
they would be liable to any export duty or fees leviable in Rangoon.
Articles VI and VII provided that there should be freedom of
passage along the Irrawaddy River for traders subject to either
government, and that traders might settle and erect houses in either
territory. Article VIII provided that if the Government of India
should within one year abolish the frontier customs, the King

Some of these articles had required a good deal of negotiating.
The first three presented little difficulty. The fourth had called for
a slight concession; Phayre's instructions had been that the duty at
Rangoon on goods for re-export to Upper Burma should be one-
eighth the normal duty; but as the Burmese Government proposed
a one per cent duty only on goods entering its territory for re-
export to Pegu, Phayre was willing to agree to the same percentage
at Rangoon. In the case of the fifth article, the Burmese Govern-
ment had proposed that goods in transit through Pegu on their
way from Upper Burma to overseas countries should be free from
all duty in British territory, but Phayre pointed out that the
Government of India was willing to forego only its frontier
customs, not its export dues, in such cases. Discussions on this
point lasted three days and it looked as if the whole negotiation
would break down. The Burmese did not propose to charge export
duty on goods in transit through Upper Burma from Pegu to
Yunnan; why, then, should the Government of India make an
export charge on goods going in the reverse direction? Phayre
argued that the Government of India must be compensated for the
cost of maintaining the ports, lighthouses, etc., items of expenditure
which the Burmese Government did not incur. Ultimately this
argument was accepted. Later on, however, after the King had
approved the draft treaty, the Wungyi wanted to re-open the
question and suggested that the Burmese Government ought to be
allowed to impose an export duty to meet the expense of protecting
the Chinese frontier; but Phayre refused to discuss the matter
further.
In regard to Articles VI and VII, Phayre had originally pro-
posed that British traders should be explicitly permitted to travel

alarm his people; so the clause was worded that British merchants
should "be allowed to travel in such manner as they please
without hindrance by the Burmese ruler, and to purchase
whatever they may require " and to "settle in any part of the
Burmese territory". The effect of these provisions would in fact

Article VIII relating to the frontier customs was also difficult.
The King could not for the time being afford to forego these duties,
but he was willing to remove them as soon as possible. Phayre
therefore felt himself unable to agree to a definitive abandonment
of the British duties, but being confident that the Burmese would
in fact remove their duties within a year or two he agreed that the
British might abolish their customs within a year, leaving it open
to the King to follow suit if he could afford to. The King was not
prepared to bind himself, for this also, he said, would have a bad
effect on his people. The ninth article, providing for free passage
from one country to the other, presented no difficulty.
Two notable points were not mentioned in the treaty, viz.,
opium and arms. The King positively refused to have opium ex-
pressly mentioned; though he stated that he would not object to
its being carried through his country for re-export under Article
IV. Consumption in his own territories he did not permit, nor
was it suggested. Phayre did not regard this matter as of much
importance, since Yunnan produced its own opium and there would
be no profit in bringing it from Bengal to that country. As re-
gards the import of arms, the Burmese did not raise the question;
Phayre found that there was a factory established at Mandalay
producing small-arms under the direction of a Frenchman, and that
the Burmese were not at the moment seriously concerned about the
matter.
The Government of India had desired permission for an explo-
ratory party to traverse the route from Bhamo to Yunnan; but
Bishop Bigandet, who was to have accompanied the mission, was
not well, so Phayre arranged that Dr. Williams, agent of the
Irrawaddy Flotilla Company at Mandalay, should go to Bhamo to
make enquiries into the present condition of the overland trade;
but it was not necessary to mention this in the treaty.
On the whole, Phayre was well pleased with the treaty. The
difficulties impeding trade between Rangoon and Yunnan and
between Pegu and Upper Burma had been largely removed, he
thought, by the reductions of duties and by the provision of
freedom of intercourse ; at the same time relief had been given to
the taxpayer of Pegu by the removal of the land frontier customs
on the British side; and means had been provided for the tea-
planters of Assam to obtain Chinese labour.

of precious metals from Upper Burma, but the old prejudice on
that subject was too strong to be overcome. He hoped, however,
that "after some experience of the benefits which are likely to be
derived from the present treaty, they will perceive that their res-

The Government of India expressed its approval of the treaty
and agreed at Phayre's suggestion, to recognise Father Abbona's
assistance by granting him a free passage, at a cost to Government
of Rs. 1,300, to his own country. Government also suggested that
in view of the increased trade which was to be expected, it would
be well to have an official Agent to the Chief Commissioner at
Mandalay to deal with any difficulties which might arise. Phayre
was therefore directed to approach the Burmese authorities with a
view to establishing an agent there “quietly and unostentatiously";
and as a result Dr. Clement Williams, the Irrawaddy Flotilla
Company's representative, assumed the charge of the agency until,
in 1864, Captain Edward Sladen was appointed.
The treaty which Phayre had thus concluded did not, however,
work very satisfactorily, and in 1866 Phayre proposed to pay a
further visit to Mandalay in September to negotiate a supplemen-
tary treaty. His visit was delayed by the rebellion of the
Myingun Min, and he did not actually leave for Upper Burma till
October. He had originally intended to go in a semi-private man-
ner with only one assistant, Mr. Edwards; but the disturbed state
of the country led him to take an escort of sixty men.

Before leaving Mandalay Phayre had warned the Ministers
that the Government of India would consider the reimposition of
its own frontier duties, and on his return to Rangoon he addressed
Government on this subject. He did not desire to reimpose import
duties, since the people of Lower Burma, who were engaged
mainly in the production of paddy, drew other agricultural produce
from Upper Burma; but he recommended the reimposition of the
export duties on rice, ngapi, and salt; and to this proposal the
Government of India assented.

The first article provided that, except earth-oil, timber, and
precious stones including jade, which were reserved as royal
monopolies, goods should be subject for the next ten years to an
import and export duty of 5 per cent only, and no other payments
in respect of any goods should be exacted. After ten years the
duty might be reduced to 3 per cent or raised to 10 per cent
(Article II); the Government of India agreed not to levy any
frontier duties so long as the Burmese rate remained at 5 per cent
(Article III). The British Government was empowered to station
a Resident or Political Agent in Upper Burma with full and final
jurisdiction over civil suits between British subjects, while civil
suits between Burmese and British subjects were to be determined
by a mixed court consisting of the Agent and a Burmese officer of
similar rank; no appeal to the Hluttaw being allowed from either
the Agent's or the mixed court. The Burmese Government was
authorised to post a Resident in British territory if it so desired,
but the powers of its Resident were not defined (Article V). Per-
mission was also given to station British officials as observers at all
customs houses in Upper Burma, and similarly the Burmese
Government might establish observers at British customs houses.
(Articles VI and VII). Freedom of trade in precious metals and in
arms was provided (Article VIII). Extradition of nationals in
cases of murder, robbery, dacoity, and theft was arranged; and
also it was provided that Burmese subjects who had committed
such crimes in British territory and were arrested in Burmese
territory should be tried by Burmese law and custom but that a
British observer should attend the trial; and similarly British
subjects arrested in British territory after committing such crimes
in Upper Burma should be tried in the presence of Burmese
observers (Articles IX-XII).
Fytche felt that this treaty was eminently satisfactory. The
limitation of customs duties and the restriction of monopolies to
three commodities, would be greatly beneficial to trade; the great
object of these provisions was that they established, it was hoped,
some degree of stability in the conditions of commerce which
formerly had been so uncertain and fluctuating owing to the King's
practice of arbitrarily imposing restrictions of one sort or another

THE TREATY OF 1862.
On the 10th day of November 1862, answering to the 5th day
of the waning moon Tatshoungmon 1224, Lieutenant Colonel
A. P. Phayre, Chief Commissioner of British Burma, having been
duly empowered by his Excellency the Right Honourable the Earl
of Elgin and Kincardine, K.T., and G.C.B., Viceroy and Governor-
General of India, and Woongyee Thado Mengyee Maha Menghla-
thee-ha-thoo, having been duly empowered by His Majesty the
King of Burmah, concluded the following treaty:
Article I.-The Burmese and British rulers have for a long
time remained at peace and in friendship%; peace shall now be
extended to future generations, both parties being careful to
observe the conditions of a firm and lasting friendship.
Article II.-In accordance with the great friendship existing
between the two countries, traders and other subjects of the
Burmese Government who may travel and trade in the British
territory, shall, in conformity with the custom of great countries,
be treated and protected in the same manner as if they were
subjects of the British Government.
Article III.-Traders and other subjects of the British
Government, who may travel and trade in the Burmese territory,
shall, in accordance with the custom of great countries, be treated
and protected in the same manner as if they were subjects of the
Burmese Government.
Article IV. When goods are imported into Rangoon from
any British or foreign territory, and declared to be for export by
the Irrawaddy River to the Burmese territory, the English ruler
shall, provided bulk is not broken, and he believes the manifest to
be true, charge 1 per cent. on their value; and if he so desires, shall
allow them to be conveyed under the charge of an officer until arrival
at Maloon and Menhla. The tariff value of goods shall be forward-
ed yearly to the Burmese ruler. If such goods are declared for
export to other territories, and not for sale in the Burmese territories,
the Burmese ruler shall, if he believes the manifest to be true, not
cause bulk to be broken, and such goods shall be free of duty.
Article V.-When goods are imported into Burmah by persons
residing in the Burmese or any foreign territory, and declared to be
