Phra Phraison Salarak: Intercourse hetween Siam and Burma, in two instalments,
Journal of the Burma Research Society, Vol. XXV, part 2, pp. 49-108 ;
and Vol. XXVIII, part 2, pp. 109-176.
Some 31 years ago the author contributed to the Journal of the
Siam Society, Vol. V, part 1, an article called The Burmese Invasions
of Siam, based upon the famous Burmese official history known as
the Hmannan Yazawin. The article was later continued in four
instalments (JSS. Vol. VIII, pt. 2 ; Vol. XI, pt. 3; Vol. XII, pt. 2 ;
Vol. XIII, pt. 1). He now presents the other side of the story,
basing his work upon Siamese records known as the Royal Autograph
edition of History.
Though the author modestly calls his work a translation from
Siamese records and though it is true that most of the material here
is a translation, the article contains nevertheless a good deal more
than translations. Interspersed here and there are the author's
notes and observations. It has of course been said that histories
can never be altogether impartial for otherwise they would not de-
serve to be read. On that ground, therefore, Phra Phraison should
be excused for his numerous observations in what he has himself
described as a translation. It is obvious from these interpolated
notes that the author has been carried away in some places by a
natural feeling of admiration for the hero of the Siamese nation-
Phra Naresuan. In many other places his interpolations are ex-
tremely informative and interesting (see for example the explanation
of the Yodayabauk on page 166, which is to be found in no other
work). The material before us, therefore, is of the nature of a pre-
sentation of facts rather than a pure translation, and therefore is of
more interest.
The account begins somewhat prior to the actual commencement
of Burmese intercourse with Siam, in order, as the author says,
to get a good perspective of events. The Siamese records upon
which he has based his account can hardly claim the same authority
in this country as the Hmannan Yazawin does in Burma, for there
is a difference of opinion as to whether this or the parallel edition of
the Patriarch, Prince Paramanujit, was a more reliable one. It need
hardly be pointed out, besides, that recent researches have rendered
considerable parts of both histories, especially their chronology, out
of date.
The author says in his preface that in transliterating Siamese
names, titles and places he has followed the method adopted by
Mr. W. A. R. Wood who chose the phonetic rather than the scientific
system. The latter gave as his reason for the choice the fact that

his book would be read by many persons who knew no Siamese
and by more who knew no Sanskrit or Pali, which form the basis of
the scientific system. Without pretending to resume, in the limited
space of a review, an already too prolonged debate on the method of
transcribing Siamese, I feel that it would not be doing justice to
the work under review to pass over the subject altogether. Siamese
grammar is a tricky matter and as the language is still in the process
of being standardized, there are so many natural rules, not all of
which have been noted down in current books on Siamese grammar,
with the result that not only foreigners but also Siamese have been
led astray. Pure Thai words do not present any difficulty as a rule,
but when we come to loan words which abound in our language
confusion arises. One of the loan words in commonest use is the
Pali Rāja, meaning ruler. It is pronounced in Siamese in two ways.
When standing by itself, that is as a simple word, it is written Ŕāj
and pronounced Rad, because the final consonant J being unaccented
is practically mute. Thus, Rājapradit, and not Rātpradit; Rājaburi,
and not Ratburi; Phra Rajat'an, not Phra Râttan. Under the so-
called phonetic system a foreigner is liable to transcribe words from
the written Siamese in ignorance of this rule-often with ridiculous
results. It is true that many illiterate Siamese make the same mistake
and the foreign phoneticist often jumps to the conclusion that the
other way must be only a purist form used by incorrigible etymo-
logists. The effect of such an attitude on the part of the foreign
phoneticist is tantamount to what an Englishman would feel when
he reads of the conclusion of a Siamese that the English words horse,
my and lady are really 'orse, moy, and lidy in scientifically phonetic
English, only purists in the aristocracy retaining the correct forms.
I am not finding faults in this case with the author himself but with
the system which he has adopted and which purports to be based
upon the science of phonetics. In the article under review are to
be found many such "phonetic
phonetic " transcriptions-no doubt taken
from Wood's history, thus: At'itwong (p. 56) which if transcribed
phonetically should have been At'ityawong or scientifically Aditya
vamsa; Wat Rajpradit'an (p. 59) which should have been phonetically
Rajapradisat'an or scientifically Rajapratiṣṭathâna, and others.
Another point to be noted in this connection is the fact that
whercas in the alphabets of the Indian classical languages each written
consonant has its own value, thus B is Ba and not just a basic Bas
in the Siamese or Roman alphabets, and similarly D is Da, G is Ga,
H is Ha and so on. In Siamese, however, as in the Roman alphabet,
no such system exists and a consonant must be written with a vowel
in order to be pronounceable. When transcribing loan words in
Siamese nevertheless we often dispense with the short A vowel and
write down like the Sanskrit or Pali simply a consonant with the
result that those who do not know the Indian classical languages

mistake the consonant for a mute and give no vowel value to it. To
make matters worse we have no compound consonants in our ortho-
graphy and they are represented in Siamese orthography by two
consecutive consonants which for those again who know no Sanskrit
or Pali might be either two consecutive consonants with vowel values
just as much as a compound consonant. By way of example let us
take the Sanskrit word Ksatriya. Written in any Indian alphabet
it would be something like Kstriya in which I have indicated the
compound consonants by a line underneath the compounded letters.
We thus have the compound consonant Ks with the unwritten short
A vowel, then the compound consonant Tr with the (written) short
vowel I, and then the consonant Y with the unwritten short A vowel.
In Siamese, however, the problem is by no means so simple. Com-
pound letters can not be indicated, nor is the presence of the unwritten
short A vowel. It is written Ksatriy and often Ksatr and pronounced
Kasat. It is no wonder, therefore, that so many pitfalls await the
unwary phoneticist. In the articles under review mention should
be made of several such mistakes as well as some others which arise
through the non-recognition of Siamese phonetic rules. For instance :
Silapa (p. 59 note 3 ; and p. 172, n. 100) which should have been
Silpa; Hantara for Hantra (p. 74); Dibamittri for Dibamantri
(p. 88, n. 1), Bejrapura for Bejrapurna (p. 88, n. 2); Krasetradhipati
for Ksetradhipati (p. 107, n. 3); Mandhara for Mandira (p. 107,
n. 4); Deva Orajuna for Deva Arjuna (p. 110, n. 3 and p. 141, n. 56);
Kambeng for Kamhaeng (p. 147); Muradhabhisek for Murdha-
bhiseka (p. 151, n. 77); Parana for Prana (p. 152, n. 79); Sankbara
for Sikhara (p. 152, n. 80) and others.
Many other mistakes are to be found especially in the writing
of names, but as they are probably printer's errors, they do not fall
within the sphere of a review.
In presenting such a translation as this one, the author should
have given more prominence to the fact that the Royal Autograph
version of Siamese history was written at a time when it was still
understood in this country that Hanthawaddy being in the Mon
country its King and his army were Mon, and that the Burmese
were regarded as only a section of the enemy hosts and always differ-
entiated as the army of the King of Ava. It was not until com-
paratively recently that Prince Damrong pointed out to the reading
public of Siam that Hanthawaddy was in that period of its history
under the Burmese and the enemy was mainly of that nationality.
As regards the subject matter, I have had by my side all through
the time I was reading it a copy of the Royal Autograph version of
Siamese history and have carefully compared the Siamese text with
the author's translation all along. I feel no hesitation in saying

that the translated passages are accurate, and, taking into account
that a considerable portion of a volume of 265 octavo pages of Siamese
script has been treated, the work has been by no means small. But
few passages may be said to have been actual departures from or
misunderstandings of the original text. This fact testifies to the
translator's complete command of the language. Passages which
need correction are as follows:
On page 53, where the translator says the Cambodians had
thrown off their allegiance, the rendering of this archaic passage would
preferably be "The Cambodians have changed their attitude."
On page 55, under the reign of Phra Int'arajat'iraj, an important
mistake should be corrected. The Siamese text records a distur-
bance in the north and the King's expedition to Muang Phrabang
where the chiefs came out and acknowledged their submission.
Muang Phrabang here must not be confused with the town of Luang
Phrabang for it referred to the town of Nakon Sawan in central
Siam, now often known as Paknampo.
On page 67 about the middle, the name of Somdet Phra Rajat’irat
should be Somdech Phra Jaya Rajadhiraj.
On page 69, Paplu, meaning the betel-grove, should, under
the system he is using be written Pap'lu.
On page 81, the term Royal Patriarch is misleading. The monk
who becomes Patriarch of the Kingdom need not possess royal
birth, though when a Patriarch he is given a rank which is equal to
that of the highest royal princes.
On page 111 last paragraph, in mentioning the Siamese defenders
of P'echaburi, it is said that they were governors of the town and
two other officers who were also governors but of other towns. The
Siamese original mentions the titles of these two others and it is
evident from these titles that the two officials referred to were of the
category known as Ken Muang Rang, that is probationary governors
whose duty was in the nature of inspectors of the rank of governors
who often filled any gubernatorial vacancy that occurred from time
to time.
While on page 115 the Siamese transcription of the name of
the Mon official sent by the King of Hanthawaddy is correctly written
as Phya Kien, the same name is written Phya Kiet on pages 116 and
120, though correctly written again on page 117 as Phya Kien. The
fact is that the Sanskrit word Kirti becomes Kierti in Siamese ortho-
graphy and the last unaccented syllable is mute. A final R has the
effect of an N, hence Kien not Kiet.

On page 122 line 14, Ramesuan should of course be Naresuan.
It is probably only a printer's error but it is misleading and is there-
fore mentioned by way of a caution.
On page 139, in describing the King's dream on the eve of the
great single combat on elephant, the sentence he went about in a boat
should be altered to he waded in, for such was the traditional version
in the Siamese original.
On pages 144 and 166, the author's translation of the office of
Phra Rajaguru as Royal Preceptor would be etymologically correct.
The name implies that the office used to be in Brahmin hands. But
that must have been a long time ago. By the time of the episode
recorded, the Brahmin preceptor only retained ceremonial duties,
and the role of the King's jurisconsult had already passed, as in
modern times, into the hands of the professional jurist. Though the
latter need not have had any Brahmin origin they had bestowed upon
them the old traditional Brahmin titles of Phra Rajaguru, etc. As
recently as in the reign of King Rama VI, the King's legal adviser was
given the title of Chao Phya Mahidhara-a modification from the
old title of Phraguru Mahidhara, also a Brahmin office for a lawyer.
In presenting this work to the public, Phra Phraison Salarak
deserves the thanks of all students of Siamese history, and it should
be the hope of all that the author will continue it. It should, however,
be kept clearly in mind that what is being translated is an old docu-
ment which has become out of date through recent researches with
regard to some of its facts and more especially its chronology. Any
commentary must be distinctly separated from the translation.
DHANI,
