BURMESE MUSIC.
(A Preliminary Enquiry).
"Just at the moment, when western civilization is beginning to realise that oriental
music is something better than the barbaric noise it has long thought it to be, the oriental
nations are in grave danger of losing interest in it, and as it has been conveyed from
generation to generation not by the arts of writing and printing. but orally, so soon as that
interest is gone the art itself, unless special measures are taken, will have entirely evaporated
that is, will be forever unrecoverable. One would wish East and West to study one another's
music, whilst pursuing independent courses in their own, but that seems to be impossible."
PERCY A. SCHOLES,
Oxford Companion to Music, P 672.

Burmese music is a virgin field for research. Nobody can bewail this virginity more
than myself. To help me there does not seem to exist a single western work on Burmese
music though there are such classics as Fox Strangways' on Indian and J. H. Levis' on
Chinese music. Many writers on Burma have passed remarks, kind or humorous, on Burmese
music. But few have attempted a technical study of it. Nothing Burmese escaped the notice of
Sir James George Scott of course. But I know of only two who left some technical remarks
on Burmese music; Paul Edmonds, the author of Peacocks and Pagodas; and P.A Mariano, in
an appendix on Burmese music to that most lovingly produced book Burma by Max and
Bertha Ferrars. I shall discuss the observations of both by and by¹. I draw attention to this
paucity of reference material to prove that I cannot be well informed and therefore cannot
speak with authority on the subject.
It is fitting to begin with a translation of what has already been written on Burmese
music in the Burmese language. If it is unscientific it is at least what the Burmese have to say
about it. And I shall be lacking in veneration for the Burmese classics if I neglect the
following extracts. They are published in U San Win's admirable effort to preserve Burmese
classical tunes in his specially invented system of notation, entitled Burmese Music Notes

MURCHANA (gs) means primarily the 'swelling of sound' and seems to have been
applied originally to high and low pitch and to have meant the rise and fall of voice in song.
Then it came to have the technical sense of (1) a rise and fall from and to a particular note of
a specific scale, and in this sense is accurately translated by 'mode'. But since the note on
which they started was the most important and was generally ornamented with a grace note
murchhana (Hindustani) has come to mean (2) grace note as applied to a particular Rag. In
the old sense of the word the 'mode' consisted of seven notes, i.e. two tetrachords, so that
murchhana may (3) be held also to translate the Greek harmony (heptachord).
RAGA (po) means 'colour'; hence colour of mid, i.e. emotion. Its European analogue
will therefore be whatever gives colour to a piece of music; and since this may be, according
to circumstances, melody, harmony, counterpoint, or instrumentation, but most of all
harmony, we have no real equivalent to a word which applies technically to melody... Its
usual translation is 'melody-type or 'melody- mould', or even 'tune'. If it must be translated,
perhaps 'Mood' would convey as much as is compressible in to one word.
SAPTAKA (m) a 'set of seven takes the place of our word 'octave'; the saptaka
contains seven svaras, i.e. not notes but intervals between them; and all Indian notations
assume three saptakas, a higher (tara …), middle (maahya g) and lower (mandra or manda

VADI, SAMVADI, ANUVADI and VIVADI (Translated 'sonant' 'consonant'
'assonant' 'dissonant') ¾¾¾¾¾¾¾ are survivals of a theory of consonance
(Samvaditva ) now forgotten. Samvadi definitely means the consonance of fourth and
Fifth, as we should say 'Perfect consonance' Vadi is the note to which it is so related. Vivadi is
applied by Bharata to the semitone. He speaks (verse 24) of notes being vivadi to one another
when they are at the distance fo twenty çrutis. This is the same thing as two crutis (22-20)
which reading actually appears in another manuscript. Anuvadi is the name for 'all other
relations which are not vadi, samvadi or vivadi. These three relations belong to the tetrachord,
and presumably anuvadi does too. Current tradition makes anuvadi the major Third. But
Bharata says 'all other' (çesha), suppose that in the term anuvadi he included the major and
minor tones (which together make up the major Third) all the determining elements of
Bharata's scale would accounted for.
Fox Strangways has a chapter on legend and history too. I shall quote the legends in
which the same well-known names recur.
"Once upon a time the great anchorite Narada thought within himself he had mastered the
whole art and science of music. To curb his pride the all-knowing Vishnu took him to visit the abode
of the gods. They entered a spacious building the in mates of which were numerous men and women,
who were all weeping over their broken limbs. Vishnu stopped short and enquired of them the reason
for their lamentation. They answered that they were the Rags, and Raginis of music, created by
Mahadeva, but as one anchorite of the name of Narada, ignorant of the true knowledge of music and
unskillful in performance, had sung them recklessly, their features wee distorted their limbs broken,
and that unless Mahadeva or some other discreet and skillful person would sing them properly, there
was slender hope of their ever being restored to their former state of body. Narada, ashamed, kneeled
down before Vishnu and asked to be forgiven."
"With Narada, the R'shi who first 'heard' the laws of music are associated Tumburu, the first
singer, and Bharata, the first to draw up rules for the drama, of which music formed a large part. Of
Tumburu it is said that he increased the scale in which the Samaveda was chanted from five notes to
six or seven. The treatise which purports to record the doctrine of Narada, the Naradaçiksha
is probably of late date (1000 A D.?) and gives no clue whatever to the achievement of the Narada of
legend. The treatise which bears the name of Bharata, the Natyaçastra, is similarly an eponym. One or
other of these three names appears probably in every book on music that has been written, and the
existence of the authors is held, though in complete absence of any specific doctrine attributed to them
as a pious belief."

So far the classics: Yet it appears that in the music itself there is as much evidence (if
not more) of Chinese and Siamese influence as there is of ancient Indian influence in
literature. I hope these influences will receive the attention of researchers in Burmese history.
As far as I am aware the Chinese influence seems to be the oldest. An unspoilt
Burmese ear will catch Chinese cadences in any extemporization on the black keys of the
piano in the truly old Burmese style. But that is no evidence. I quote J. Kunst.

No doubt that would not be impossible in itself. But what is hardly believable is that not only
should the structure of these scales be founded everywhere on the cycle of the blown fifths, but that all
should start from the same fundamental note, that of the houang tchoung of 732 vibrations; which
brings us back to the same conclusion that, not only the relative structure of the scale, but also the
absolute pitch of the notes is the same in all cases considered. This circumstance eliminates the
hypothesis of a series of coincidences: One is certainly dealing here with intercourse between people
and people. For all we know, this intercourse may apply to the musical xylophones, of the pan-pipes of
the Incas, to those of certain Melanesian, Polynesian and present-day Brazilian tribes. And it is
intercourse of the same kind which one must concede, by virtue of the same arguments, to explain the
Javano-Balinese scales, as we believe we have demonstrated by means of a number of measurements
of sounds De Toonkunst Van Bali II, Figures I-V and VII-XIX. It has been proved that the influence of
the Chinese meteorological basis, corresponding to the houang tchoung-230mm.-was used or is still

The true cause seems to lie in the incorrect intonation of modern Chinese
instruments, incorrect in the sense that the intonation has deviated from the theoretical of the scale

Let us take up the keyless flute and find out what sort of scale it produces. This flute is
virtually the standard pitch pipe² of the Chinese ensemble... By careful experiment it has been
found that the fourth and the seventh of the scale thereon are both "neutral", that is, the fourth is
neither a half tone above the third as in the European diatonic major scale, nor a full tone above it
as in the theoretical Chinese heptatonic scale, but is just about mid way between the third and the
fifth, and the seventh is similarly mid way between the sixth and the octave. It becomes at once
clear why Chinese instruments sound impersonal or neutral or out of tune...
The true reason for the incorrect intonation the existing Chinese instruments cannot be
stated with absolute certainty. The most probable reason is this: During the Yuan dynasty both the
neutral and sharp F were used ..... But the production of both these chromatic tones could not be
done on the flute except with the assistance of special key devices unknown to the Chinese
instrument makers or with boring the second and fifth holes counting from the lower end, larger
than the rest. It was possible at that time when both f and f sharp were in use to bore the holes on
the Chinese flute so as to enable the player to produce the notes when desired by a definite system
of fingering such as now used by western fife players. But with the coming of the Ming dynasty,
this whole system transgressing the pentatonic scale was officially abolished.
Musicians then and therefore did not have to bother at all about producing f and f sharp,
and following the law of supply and demand, the flute makers undertook to make flutes that could
not produce either f or f- sharp accurately, but had more uniformity and symmetry in their
appearance by boring all the finger holes of the same size and with a minimum variation of
distances from each other³
If a player who had bought the flute cared to produce all the

agreeing neither with the European diatonic (with the perfect), nor the Chinese heptatonic scale
(with the augmented fourth). The same thing was then applied to the playing of stringed
instruments......to be in tune with the wind instruments.

Long after the foregoing paragraph was written, at the instance of Mr. Furnivall, who
pointed out the deficiencies of the bamboo pattalar, and who supplied the frequencies of the
notes of his own pattalar, further efforts were made to obtain the measurements of the
modern Burmese scale both on a pattalar and on the most characteristic Burmese wind
instrument, the hnè, i. e. an oboe, The pattalar (of brass) was carefully tuned by a
professional Burmese musician and then measured together with the hnès in Dr. West's
laboratory in the University of Rangoon . Mr. Furnivall's pattalar was measured in the
Cavendish Laboratory, Cambridge. This is an older instrument than the brass pattalar, on
which the scale is the scale of the present day. The Burmese scale of Hornbostel is still older,
the source from which its measurement was obtained being "Zeitschrift for Ethnologie" jaarg.
1911, blz. 601 vv. The oldest of all available measurements is the Siamese scale of Stumpf
from "Beitrage zur Akustik und Musikwissenschaft" Bd. III, 1901.
Thus, calculating from the given frequencies, the intervals in cents of all the scales
have been plotted on graph paper putting them in chronological order with the hypothetical
equi-distant seven tone scale on top, and the just scale and the western tempered scale at the
bottom for comparison. The result is a chart showing the probable evolution of the Burmese
scale after its advance from the pentatonic basis. See folding chart.
Whereas the present western scale moved away from the Just Intonation via Mean

The tuning of the gongs, as I rather anticipated, was similar to the tuning of the Pattalar. The
drum-circle, however, was tuned, I was excited to find, in the pentatonic scale; there was no F and
no B, with the exception of an F in the top octave of all. (See Example 1, 2, 3). My discovery of
the pentatonic tuning of the drum-circle fairly convinced me that the pentatonic scale is, as had
already begun to suspect, the foundation of Burmese music. And when I came to re-examine the
two tunes which I had thought showed no signs of pentatonic origin, I found one significant point
which I had previously over looked all the cadences were pentatonic, and there was no cadence in
which the leading note B appeared as the penultimate note.
Examples 1, 2 and 3 will show that Paul Edmonds' tuning of the drum-circle does not
agree with U San Win's. But that will be because the modern drum-circle has had added to it
three more drums. And perhaps it has also become sophisticated, as oriental ears have
unconsciously become with the western diatonic major scale. In the beginning the drum-
circle was not even a circle. It was a semi-circle, called :: (la-chan-saing) half-moon-
band, the tuning of which could not be but pentatonic, to supply the ground notes of the
tunes. The most characteristic (the first violin), among Burmese musical instruments is the
hnè, Burmese oboe, whose limitless flexibility of tone has now been overwhelmed by the
blustering gusto of the completed drum-circle. (See Diagrams 2 and 3). Although only one
octave is shown in the diagrams, notes of the octave are easily obtained by lipping and
overblowing. Actual blowing of the hnè in diagram 3 side by side with the modern oboe (new
philharmonic pitch) shows that the lowest note of the hnè corresponds to the lowest A of the
oboe, and the hnè's highest practicable note corresponds to the oboe's highest E, i.e. third line
above the treble stave. The natural scale of the hnè is D pitched on octave higher.
The bawlè Paul Edmonds heard might not have had any cadence which was not
pentatonic. Nowadays it has too many things. Worse offenders than Hla Tin have with their
variations, fioritura and bravura twisted its characteristic cadence out of all recognition. But
there certainly are any amount of pentatonic cadences in Burmese music. In fact, an
improvisation strictly on the black keys of the piano sounds more Burmese than many a
modern Burmese song.

Notes on Time.
There remains the question of Burmese musical time. Both Mr. Marian o and Paul
Edmonds wrote with enthusiasm on the strictness with which Burmese musicians kept time.
Very few among Burmans do not know how to keep the tiny bell (si :) and clapper (wah :)
going correctly when music is playing. A foot or a hand of listeners and player alike may
often be seen beating time. That may be partly because we as a race find it impossible to hear
music with stolid immobility. Partly, however, it is because our language is not accentual but
tonal.
Rhythm in a language such as English is largely dependent upon differences of
emphasis or accent upon the syllables in a word, and the words in a sentence. Differences of
emphasis or accent do occur in Burmese, but not to the same extent; consequently emphasis
or accent is not used very much, if at all, in the composition of Burmese verse.
Burmese verse depends rather upon the schematic arrangement of words with certain
sounds recurring at fixed points. That is Burmese verse has rhyme, but it has little or no
accentual rhythm. On the other hand timing, especially such as depends upon the number of
syllables pronounced in a given period and caesuras that mark the period are the grater
importance in Burmese than in English. In Burmese singing the caesuras are even more
important than the sets of syllables or words in each measure. See, the rests on the first beat
of measures 1 to 8 (Example 11 Voice)'.
The singer keeps time with a pair of tiny bells (si 8:) and a small clapper (wah ♂:) in
his hand. The clap' coincides with each of he rests mentioned till the final note of he section
when it coincides with the tonic.
Simple duple or simple quadruple is the usual time. In the duple the bell (O) and the
clapper (X) go alternately. In the quadruple there is a rest (-) on one or the other of he middle
beats.

THE WRITING OF BURMESE MUSIC
The first question is should it be written at all? I am of opinion that for singing, for
the voice, it will require a certain amount of temerity to note Burmese music. Fox Strangways
says:
"It seems to us curious to draw a picture of a tune; but those who do so think not of a
tune but of a mode. They conceive the tune as going in a general, not a particular direction.
When a peasant sings a folk-song with six variations, he speaks of them as six ' ways' of
singing it. And if you then sing him a seventh variation he accepts it, provided it is in the
same mode (:). Notation, therefore, would only hamper the unlimited power to vary. It
would also be useless, because the songs are handed down and valued as being 'so-and-so's
'song, with, of course, all his idiosyncrasies and mannerisms, which could not possibly be
noted.
Grace, as a singer understands it, is the continuous flow of the voice round the graced
note; and this is quite indefinable by notation which is confined to tones and semitones.
Harmony, for which that notation is devised, abolishes mode; for, it in its prime, largely
depended on minute difference in the 'size' of third, sixth, and other intervals. Mode depended
also on certain points of rest, namely, on the pentatonic notes. Harmony can deal with these,
certainly, and in doing so it takes on a coloring which has often a fascination; but it also
defines what the singer left undefined; and there by removes a certain wistfulness and charm.
If there are any who doubt that charm, it is probably because they have not heard folksong in
its native hunts. Being itself a perfectly sincere thing, its charm does not survive publicity,
nor the treatment of the song as something quaint, still less as something to be exploited.
Folk-song is like a hedgerow flower, which when plucked, withers more quickly than one
from a garden. If we were to get at its real beauty we must do with it what the singer himself
has done - we must sing it all through and many times. Its merit comes from its having run
the gauntlet of the best kind of criticism, that of the singers themselves. It has always been
judged by results. If it did not move these singers, they either altered it or dropped it. It is, and
can only be, the work of time.
That passage refers to the Indian folk-song, but applies to some extent to Burmese
singing as well. In any performance of Burmese chamber music you will notice that the
singer wields the time-making clapper and bell (8:). That is not because the

singer is the only person who has both hands free. It is because the singer is the most

conductor. All the players must hearken to the 'clap' of the singer's clapper and go fast or
slow as the 'claps' command. Restrict those claps either by metronome or notation and

every bar; all other players hearken to those claps. Therefore I am of opinion that the singer's
part should not be noted. Let him or her still enjoy absolute freedom, his or her taste being the
only, master, and the criterion by which a musical performance shall be judged.

BURMESE MUSIC
429
NOTES ON PLAYING THE BURMESE CLASSICAL SONGS
FROM STAFF-NOTATION
The following songs represent only four of the principal classes of Burmese classical
song. Patpyo, Yodaya and Talaing remain, not to mention the minor classes. The ambition of
the present contributor is to leave at least one each of all the classes as well as folk-songs in
staff-notation before either he or the songs disappear. Time, means, and opportunities are at
present insufficient.
Before actually playing these specimens the following points will perhaps be useful:
(1) Mannerism has to be firmly suppressed lest it should lend a western air to these
oriental tunes. In order to avoid misleading to player attempt has been made to write
out in full most of the ornaments. Trill has been introduced with misgivings. It is so
rare in Burmese music. So is the pizzicato in the opening bars of the first song. The
turns on the other hand are ubiquitous. But there are several kinds of turns as will be
seen. Phrase-marks are given tentatively. It will be best to play as written without
accent or style.
(2) The piano is not playing an accompaniment to the violin. Both should be equally
prominent. The two together make one utterance of the same theme. Both will follow
the singer, if there is one. The vocal part has to be entirely omitted because only a
Burmese singer who has slaved at learning to sing by imitation and repetition will
know how to sing such songs. If the piano sounds empty it must be remembered that
not only do we have no chords but also U San Win has reduced the measures to the
bare bones of simplicity. Any Burmese pianist will introduce any amount of personal
fioritura, which is the reason why it is so difficult to decide which version of a
classical song is the authentic one.
(3) The use of the Sustaining Pedal when playing Burmese music is highly objectionable.
Oriental tunes are conceived in a single melodic line and the undue sustaining of
notes never meant to blend together can only lead to confusion. The piano can be
played loudly without the use of the 'Loud Pedal' which after all is meant for higher
purpose than were loudness.
(4) It is not necessary to procure a bell and clapper. Snapping of the fingers will do for
the bell and tapping for the clapper. But one person should be snapping and tapping
right through a song except where time is ad libitum. If there can be no such person, a
player must tap with one foot the 'claps' which

Performers who are not conversant with Burmese classical music are requested to
start the second song ε at bar 11, marked "Var" because tempo rubato or ad
libitum is customary at the beginning and end of most Burmese songs, and only
Burmese musicians will know just how much liberty is to be taken with musical time.
For the same reason the third song may be ended with the first note of the 56th bar,
immediately behind D.S. playing the postlude, if desired, as a coda.
Western instrument like the piano and the oboe being keyed instruments and the
Burmese scale being what it is, no, amount of notation and annotation can reproduce
the tonality peculiar to Burmese music. We will always find the result do wè dai
i.e. guilty of foreign accent. However, this point has been dealt with in the article.
