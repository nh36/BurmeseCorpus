We have shown evidence above that the
Suttaniddesa was written in Ceylon.

The Simālankāra-ṭikā also contains
evidence to show that it was originally
written in Ceylon. It is possible that the
work had some connection with the
establishment of a simā at Jayavaddhana-
pura. The Gandhavaṁsa itself states that
the Sankhepavaṇṇanā was written in Ceylon.
When it is said that Chapața introduced
the Sankhepavaṇṇanā from Ceylon and
transcribed it from the Sinhalese into
the Burmese-Talaing alphabet what is
meant may be that the author brought
his own book from Ceylon and re-tran
scribed it in his native alphabet.
It has also to be pointed out that
none of the names of works attributed
to Saddhammajothipāla in the Gandhavam-
sa occurs in the list of books enumerated
in the Pagan Inscription23 of A. D. 1442.
This evidence also goes some way to
S
show that these works appeared in Burma
after the middle of the fifteenth century
when Chapata II returned from Ceylon.
What has so far been shown is suffi-
cient literary evidence to prove that there
were two learned theras, by the name
of Chapada who were well known for
their scholarship. The first flourished in
the twelfth century A. D. and the second
Saddhammajotipāla, in the fourteenth.
Although the first has not left any literary
products, there is the Chapta-pagoda
(Nyaung-u) at Pagan, built in the Sinha-
lese pattern, to remind us of this
panion of Uttarajiva Thera. The date
of the second is clear from some of his
own books. The confusion with regard
to the date and identity of the two theras,
first made as far as traceable, was by
Subhuti Thera, and was followed by
Wickremasinghe, Mable Bode, Geiger,
com-
Malalasekera and others until Buddhadatta
Mahathera raised the problem.
We have attempted to solve the ques-
tion here, and concluded that Chapaţa,
the pilgrim to Ceylon after whom the
Nyaung-u in Pagan is named, lived in
the twelfth century, and that Chapata,
Saddhammajotipāla, who lived in the
fourteenth century also had been to
Ceylon. The second Chapata was the
author of the various books listed in
the Gandhavamsa. The confusion has
been due to the similarity in the
names of both Burmese and Sinhalese
kings under whom both our scholars
flourished, and the cryptic manner in
which the year is stated in Saddhamma-
jotipāla's works, where a date is given.
