THE LOGIC OF INFERENCE IN STATISTICAL THEORY'
by
R. M. Sundrum, Ph.D. (London) 2
Inference in discussions of logic is, or at least used to be,
divided into the two categories of "deductive" and "inductive".
Adopting for the time being this convenient classification, we note
that from very early times, the logical rules governing deductive
inferences have been laid down with great care regarding the basic
principles and detailed application. The purely logical applications of
deductive inference have grown up side by side with the great development
of mathematics, whose main preoccupation has been in the deduction
of results from given conditions according to well defined rules of
operation. A considerable part of statistical theory, in particular
those parts in which mathematical methods have to be applied to
derive results, depends on this apparatus of deductive inference which
has been built up through the ages.
My present concern, however, will mainly be on the position of
inductive inference, if there is any such thing, and its place in
statistical theory. I have chosen this particular topic for discussion
before this Society because I hope that the point of view of the modern
statistician towards his subject may be instructive to workers in other
fields, as this point of view appears to me to be the groundwork of all
scientific procedure and to find its clearest expression in statistical
theory. Now, the whole process of thought called 'inductive inference'
is just the ordinary habit of mind known as 'learning from experience.'
I put it this way to show that this type of learning is much the more
important one in the ordinary conduct of life. We must remember
that it was long after enduring civilizations had been built up by some
such "learning from experience" that the early Greeks and Egyptians
laid down the logical rules of deductive inference. It is therefore the
more surprising that it is only recently that the rules governing inductive
inference were formalised and even later that they were questioned.
It is, I understand, a lively subject of controversy in philosophy
whether there is at all a possibility of making valid inferences of this
type, and if so, in what form or forms it may be conducted. I do
not want to go into that problem at all in the present discussion. My
purpose is only to indicate how the problem arises in most statistical

In the elementary instruction of the subject of statistics, a student

a revelation to me when I realized that there is a certain philo-
sophy of statistics which informs the methods currently used by the

among alternative methods. If this were the only reason for the

domestic concern to my profession. I have presumed to take up the
subject with you because I feel that it is the same philosophy, at least
in broad outline, which provides the basis of all scientific research in its
search for the underlying regularities of natural phenomena.
Now the problem of induction arises in statistical work when the
statistician is required to infer some properties of a population from
the information provided only by a sample of observations selected
from that population. Except in so far as his data is usually expressed
in terms of numerical measures or of simple attributes, this is precisely
the problem which confronts every scientist who attempts to draw valid
inferences from observations of experience to an underlying hypothesis
which explains natural phenomena. One important difference, however,
is that the statistician can deploy the notion of probability into his
calculations. In ordinary sampling, that is done by the injunction that
he should draw a "random" sample from his population, i.e. that he
should give every member of the population a pre - assigned probability
of being included in the sample to be drawn. Thus, the statistician is
not committed to produce "laws of nature” which may be held with
certainty; on the other hand, his results only assign to various hypo-

Having said so much, I must go on to comment on various notions
of probability current among statisticians and the particular notion I
adopt as the basis of my present discussion. There are two approaches to
the concept of probability, one in which probability is used to measure
some kind of the degree of belief in certain propositions, and one in
which probability is related to the frequency with which certain
events tend to happen under certain conditions. Perhaps it helps to call
them the "subjective” and “objective” definitions respectively. While
the objective or frequentist approach is held by a considerable majority
of practising statisticians, I must in all fairness refer to some very great
authorities on the subject who continue to advocate the practical
usefulness of the subjective concept. Perhaps the greatest modern.
exponent of this view is Prof. Harold Jeffreys, particularly in his
standard work, "The Theory of Probability". The difficulty with this
particular notion is that it seems to allow different people to estimate the
probability of the same event or proposition in different ways. Voicing
again only my personal opinion, the great achievements of Prof. Jeffreys
is that he suggests a way out of this dilemma. He says that there is
a certain kind of mind which estimates probabilities which may be
called that of a "reasonable" or "rational" man and that when all
estimates of probability used are of this type, it removes this otherwise
fatal objection to the theory. I have no time here to comment at any
length on this idea and must content myself with referring all those
interested to Prof. Jeffrey' book above referred to, or an able exposition
of various such theories by I. J. Good. ³ I may mention that the work
on probability theory by Lord Keynes 4 is also an attempt to establish
this notion of probability.
I must now go on to consider the relevance of this particular
notion of probability to the problem of inductive inference. In this
connection, this particular view of probability appears to have the
advantage over most objective theories in that we can now speak of the
"probability of a hypothesis", a concept which is not justified within
the frequentist school of thought. This advantage may be illustrated
by an example. Suppose a scientist has accumulated a certain amount

But the statistician can by the calculus of probability find the
probability of committing Errors Type I and II by following any
particular procedure of determining the critical region on which the
whole test of these hypotheses depends. The statistician can go very
much beyond this position by laying down methods which reduce

But the important thing for our present purpose is that any conclusion
drawn by the statistician using this procedure is conditioned by the fact
that certain errors are involved in these conclusions and the only thing
we know about these errors is the probability with which they occur.
Let us illustrate the consequences of this procedure by a concrete
example. A statistician in the course of his professional work has to
decide a series of statistical problems, in each of which he sets up a
Null hypothesis and an alternative hypothesis. In each problem, he
fixes a critical region such that the probability of Type I error is, say,
5 per cent and the probability of Type II error is also, say, 5 per cent.
This means that if the Null hypothesis is true, he might mistakenly
reject it on an average of 5 per cent of occasions and that if the alter-
native hypothesis is true, he might again mistakenly accept the Null
hypothesis on an average of 5 per cent of occasions. He does not
claim to establish any of these hypotheses, but by keeping the probabi-
lities of the two kinds of errors sufficiently low, he is content because
over a series of such problems, he keeps down the frequency with
which he makes mistakes.
At this point, the statisistician, goes beyond the strict confines of his
discipline and at least by implication ventures into the philosophical
problems of induction. This is brought out by the sense in which a
statistician accepts a hypothesis. By accepting a hypothesis, the
statistician does not claim that that hypothesis is in fact true. He
merely recommends that we act "as if" that hypothesis were true. In
fact, this is emphasised so much that the latest development in statis-
tical theory is called "decision function theory" based on the considera-

tion that statistical procedures of this type do not reveal any hidden

At this stage, we can draw a number of conclusions about the
whole philosophy behind these procedures. First we must notice that
this is far removed from the old idea of 'inductive inference.' In fact
we do not infer anything by this method. We only act in certain ways
with the guidance of some evidence provided by available data and
interpreted by statistical methods. To emphasise this distinction
strongly, Professor Jerzy Neyman, one of the founders of this theory,
has proposed that we speak of 'inductive behaviour.' He goes on
to suggest that this is the essence of the process of 'learning from
experience' in the ordinary sense of the term and also in the sense in
which all scientific research is also a matter of learning from experi-
ence. This helps to emphasise for us the tentativeness of the theories
we hold and the way in which they depend and change with the
accumulation of experience. On this philosophy, we do not look upon
scientific laws as descriptions of eternal regularities in the universe at
large, but merely as the most convenient conceptualisation of the results
to help arrive at "decisions."
Thus the statistician is a positivist in his philosophy. Perhaps it is
instructive to consider him as an operationalist in the way he defines
concepts and hypotheses and to look upon this well-defined and
precise statistical procedure as the 'operation' by which he includes
them in his Weltanschauung or leaves them out.
Another conclusion we can draw from all this and which is of
some importance to the philosophy behind the statistician's work is in
the way he frames hypotheses. Fundamental to this is the fact that
the statistician does not accept or reject a particular hypothesis but that
he does this in relation to some sufficiently well-defined alternative
hypothesis. It is therefore not impossible that a particular hypothesis
may be accepted on the same evidence against one alternative but
rejected against another.

subject emphasises to some extent the primacy of the hypothesis over
the observations. Observations are useless unless they can be
interpreted in the light of some hypothesis. This doctrine, a central
one in certain variations of logical positivism, has aroused some
controversy. Thus the objection is sometimes raised that certain
observations force a theory upon the observer, as for instance, when
milk accidentally spilt over a piece of coal turns it into gold. It is
then alleged that that incident surprises an observer sufficiently to lead
him to a new hypothesis in chemistry. This, of course, is not quite
correct for this new experience only serves to reject a certain implicitly
held hypothesis that milk spilt on coal would not turn it into gold to
give place to a wider hypothesis which covers this particular
observation as well.
At this stage of the discussion, it is perhaps appropriate to digress
from the main stream of the present argument to emphasise a particular
point. Statistical theory is NOT a discipline of the same order as
Economics, Psychology, Physics or Biology. Rather it is a tool which
should find a place in all such disciplines, like any other tool of
research. Perhaps, some of the abilities required to fashion statistical
techniques are so uncommon that a certain amount of specialization is
necessary and thus accounts for the statistical expert. But once such
tools have been developed, these should be acquired as essential
equipment in the intellectual armoury of every scientific researcher.
,,
If at the end of this discussion, it is permitted to me to draw to the
larger world of scientific workers the moral suggested by statistical
philosophy, it would be this. "Inductive generalizations from
observations can only be arrived at by a hazardous procedure; they
must be held with the utmost tentativeness and always with readiness to
be given up in the light of newer experience. In statistics itself, this is
stressed for us by the fact that every test of a hypothesis is
accompanied by a statement of the probabilities of two kinds of errors.
Further, the hypotheses which have stood the tests put upon them are
not to be considered so much to reveal the nature of the external
world" as to guide conduct in the immediate future, i.e. to lay down
for us certain patterns of "inductive behaviour."

Another moral to be drawn from all this lies in the great
dependence of the use of statistical methods on the formulation of
hypotheses, even prior to the collection of evidence, in fact, to
suggest what sort of evidence is at all relevant. It is true that a mass
of facts may suggest a plausible hypothesis, but it is all too often
forgotten that it suggests hypotheses only to a sufficiently lively
imagination. We have travelled some distance from the days when the
great Newton could claim, "Hypotheses non fingo". (I do not make
hypotheses.) to realize that now it is of the essence of scientific
research to frame hypotheses of all kinds, to compare them in the light
of evidence, and choose one of them as a guide to action. Scientific
research progresses in the hands of gifted people, but it also depends
on the existence of a healthy mental climate, which permits the
individual imagination to roam over all possible worlds in search of
hypotheses.
