RECENT BOOKS ON BURMA.
During the last four years probably more books
have been published in England and America about
Burma than in the preceding fifty years.
Burma has, for
once, been "in the news", and there has been a demand
for information which many authors, qualified or unqua-
lified to write on the subject, have hastened to satisfy.
Of all the veritable spate of books on Burma that
has appeared, the outstanding work is clearly John Leroy
Christian's Burma and the Japanese Invader. It is not
without significance that the best of the war-time publi-
cations about Burma should be one which was written
originally not to satisfy any ephemeral war-time demand
but as a scholarly survey of our country, projected and
nearly completed before
before war impinged on
on us. The
book appeared first under the title of Modern Burma,
published by the Institute of Pacific Relations. The work
had just been finished when the Japanese war began
and was published at the beginning of 1942; subsequently
a second edition, with additional chapters bearing on
the war, was issued in India by Messrs Collins under
revised title.
Colonel Christian was for many years a member
of the Burma Research Society and was a frequent
contributor to the pages of this Journal. He was formerly
in charge of the Technical School at Meiktila and while
there he devoted his boundless enthusiasm to the study
of Burma subjects. On his return to the United States
he continued his studies, until he was invited to serve
the Allied cause as an Intelligence Officer in the United
States Army. His untimely death in action during the
landing off the Rangoon River in May 1945 has been
a grievous loss to Burma as well as to his many
Burma friends. As the interpreter of Burma to the
outside world he stood supreme in our generation, and
even one's sense of personal grief at his death is almost
outweighed by one's consciousness of the loss to Burma
of one who, now above all times, could have played an
inestimably valuable part in the advancement of the
country and in the explaining of Burma's case to the
world at large.

His Burma and the Japanese Invader is inval-
uable as a work of reference. It covers the whole field
of Burma's life - Government, Education, Commerce,
Industry, Social Life, Agriculture, Religion, Politics,
and, based as it is on a most careful and detailed survey
of the available evidence, it is indispensable for anyone
who wishes to study any aspect of modern Burma. The
bibliography alone is an excellent guide to Burma
subjects; and the historian in particular will find that it
gives nearly all he needs in the way of references to the
printed literature.
In one or two minor points of detail some readers
may not agree with the author's views; but such cases
are few and unimportant. It is, indeed, surprising that
in a work of such wide compass, compiled single-handed,
there should be so extraordinarily little that anyone could
disagree with.
Christian also published, with Messrs Collins, a
shorter book under the title Burma. This is for more
popular consumption, and is an excellent introduction to
the country and its peoples for the reader who has no
previous acquaintance with them; and equally those
of us who are familiar with Burma will find it a
stimulating, thought-provoking study. The author gives
a most sympathetic picture of the Burman and shows,
of course, a thorough grasp of the country's problems.
The book does not profess to be a solid work of reference
, as is Burma and the Japanese Invader; it is not intended
for the same purpose; it is more expressive of the author's
own kindly, sympathetic
sympathetic nature. We may at times
feel the touch of his gentle irony, but we can all realise
the soundness of his judgement and the depth of the
writer's attachment to our country.
It will be for all of us a lasting grief that John
Christian did not live to contribute yet further to the
literature of Burma; but to him, if consciousness survives
in the unknown life after death, it will be a joy and
consolation to know that he died in the service of the
country that he loved.

Frankly (and when a book-review begins with the
word "frankly" it is certain that something unkind will
follow) Tennyson Jesse's Story of Burma (Macmillan
1946) is a disappointing book.
The title itself is inappropriate to the content.
The first half of the book is a brief and very sketchy
summary of the history of Burma as seen by a writer
who, however great her eminence, is not an historian; and
the second part comprises discursive remarks on Burmese
life and Burma's problems. Obviously, while the title
The Story of Burma may be appropriate to the first
part, it is inapplicable to the second. The title suggests
a history, but only half the book can claim in any way to
deal with history.
As history, moreover, the first part is not of great
value. It is significant that out of eighty-five pages given
to the historical section of the book, no less than thirty-
seven are devoted to King Thibaw and the annexation of
Upper Burma; and it is not unreasonable to suggest that
this pre-occupation with the events of a mere seven years
or so is somewhat disproportionate in considering the
history of a country whose extant historical records go
back over a thousand years. What is still more remarkable
is that the account leaps straight from Anawrahta to
Alaungpaya, with a sublime indifference to the seven
hundred years - seven hundred years that included Burma's
greatest period that intervened. The result is that Pagan
is not even mentioned; yet Pagan is the great glory of
Burma, and the era of Pagan saw Burma's culture at its
apex. To omit Pagan in writing a history of Burma is
to display a peculiar indifference to what Burma's culture
formerly was and to its capabilities for the future.
There are curious omissions of another sort. One
omission is a bibliography; it would have been interesting
to know on what sources the author drew for her informa-
tion. May one be forgiven for suggesting that the sources
were somewhat limited? For example, although Mr.
Furnival is indeed referred to as the authority on the
political economy of Burma, it would not appoar that
the author has seriously studied his book on this subject;
there is no indication, in her remarks on the social and
administrative system of Burma in the days of the Kings,

sense" is to miss the point that there was a very strong
social sense indeed. Is it correct, again, to say that "under
Alompra the art of internal administration decayed"?
Surely the truth is that the system of administration had

Of course, the B. B. T. C. may be able to recognise

Again, why say that "many Europeans have
laughed at the Buddha's teaching, because, so they say
glibly, he died of a surfeit of pork"? In the first place,
the legend is nowadays discounted; in the second place
few Europeans have even heard of it; in the third place

other reason.
In general, the book lacks cohesion and sequence.
of thought. There seems to be no good reason for
appending an account of Sir Harcourt Butler's Manao
at Myitkyina to remarks on Burmese costume; and the
curious hotch-potch towards the end of the book, where
a long disquisition on Japanese aggression is mixed up
with obscure references to the oil-interests, is difficult to
make anything of. Again after careful consideration one can
make out what the author is driving at, but the point could
have been made with perfect clarity and in fewer words.
The following sentence, however, "Burma remained
loyal and both disaffected and unaffected", leaves one
defeated. It simply does not make sense.
The concluding sections of the book, dealing with
Burma's present-day problems, inevitably suffer from
having been written some time before the end of the war,
and it would not be reasonable to expect anything more.
But in general, it has to be admitted, the book is
disappointing. This sense of disappointment arises
from one's high expectations. One looked for something
striking and revealing from Tennyson Jesse. As it is,
one has only a somewhat rambling book, which gives the
impression of having been hastily and carelessly thrown
together.

the subject of Burma at war is numerous; and almost all
of them are valueless.

This is true in particular of those books which
relate to the campaign of 1942. Burma for a short time
swarmed with newspaper-correspondents. Scarcely any
of them had even passed through the country before, let
alone had any real acquaintance with it. But they all
became forthwith experts on the country, its peoples, its
government, and everything else. So far as can be seen,
the average correspondent collected most of his informa-
tion in clubs and pubs and made no serious effort to
verify the hair-raising-and sometimes libellous-stories
that he heard. He then departed in haste, by air and
wrote a book. Of course, if a hard-up journalist can earn
a few welcome shillings by writing a book on a subject of
which he is profoundly ignorant, well, after all he no
doubt needs the money. But there is another side to all
this, and that is that such books, if taken seriouly, may
do harm, and there is no doubt that some of the books
now under discussion did a great deal of harm. There
being little other information available and the official
communiqués about the disaster in Burma being, as
usual, meagre, the world in general had to acquire its
information about what happened in Burma from these
books, and so there was created a quite false idea of the
sort of country Burma is and the sort of people the Bur-
mans are. A great deal of effort had then to be expended
in trying to remove misconceptions which need never have
arisen.
On a long-term view perhaps this is unimportant;
the historian who knows his job is quite capable of
distinguishing good from bad evidence, and it is unlikely
that when the history of the war comes to be written it
will be affected by the highly-coloured effusions which
appeared in and about the year 1943. On a short-term
view, however, it is disconcerting; most of
most of us are
dependant for our knowledge of current world affairs on
the newspapers which we receive from abroad; but if the
columns which reporters write in the daily press are no
more accurate than the books they write, then we are
truly in a parlous state, for our so-called knowledge of
what is going on in the world must be a valueless and
most misleading basis for judgment.
