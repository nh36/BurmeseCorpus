Palm-leaf MSS.
The use of palm leaf for manuscripts is an ancient
institution in Burma. It goes back to the Pyus of
Sriksetra (Old Prome of the eighth century A. D.) The
leaves of both the toddypalm (Borassus flaballifer) and
corypha palm (Corypha umbraculifera) have been used
for the purpose.2 The former is known as co$: (than) in
Burmese and σ (ta) in Mon, derivatives of Pali
(tala). The latter is 8 (piy) in Old Burmese, so (pe)
in Modern Burmese and (ruit) in Mon. Both are

According to Burmese tradition the life span of
a palm-leaf manuscript is a thousand years. The
oldest known Burmese palm-leaf book was described
with illustrations by Dr. J. A. Stewart in one of the prewar
numbers of the Bulletin of the School of Oriental and
African Studies³, no copy of which can yet be traced in
war-torn Burma.

"Sir Emerson Tennent in his History of Ceylon,

A palm-leaf book is usually made up of the leaves,
two coverboards, a cloth-wrapper and a riband. The
cover-boards are called ms, the cloth-wrapper og or
စာပေ and the riband စာထုပ်ကြိုး Or စာစည်းကြိုး. Shway Yoe, in
his book "The Burman: His Life and Notions", p. 131,
describes a Burmese palmleaf book thus.
"Each chest contains a large number of different
books. Every separate collection of leaves being enclosed
between wooden boards, some plain, some carved in
high relief, with figures of dewas and demons. Round
this is wrapped a kabalwe, a square piece of cloth with
inwoven scenes from the sacred books, or portions of
the Law, and the formulae used at the pagoda. Most
of these are of silk, very often with narrow slips of
bamboo worked in so as to give stiffness to the whole.
Instead of this, or sometimes in addition, is used the
sa-sigyo, a riband, about two fingers' breadth, and upwards
of a yard in length. This is knitted or crocheted in a
peculiarly close fashion, which puzzles English ladies.
On it is worked the name of the owner, his titles and
distinctions, and whatever aspirations he chooses to add.
They are very neat, and quite easy to read, an advantage
which does not always characterise Berlin wool work.
The King's grants of forest land, monopolies and the
like, were usually executed in this way.

Parabaik (Burmese parapuik, purapuik
Mon 8,8 bapuit, khapuit) is a folding book, usually
of paper. Parabaiks made of gold, silver, copper, brass
and leather are often mentioned in Burmese literature.
For enshrinement in pagoda, metal parabaiks are used.
Paper parabaiks are the most common.

Paper (Burmese cakki, Mong cakkho) is
manufactured from wood pulp or bamboo pulp or rice-
straw. The wood pulp used in Burma proper is of the
bark of a tree called, and in the Shan States that
of a tree called maihsai-le in Shan or 08816S in Burmese,
which is a species of mulberry. An account of paper-
making in the Shan States is given in the Gazetteer of
Upper Burma and the Shan States, Part 1, Vol. 11 p. 411,
thus.
"The bark having been stripped from the tree, the
outer brown portion is carefully pared off, leaving only the
white inner bark. This is boiled in water for about a
day, by which time it has assumed the appearance of
dirty rags. It is then beaten with a wooden mallet till
it has so lost its stringy bark character that any quantity
can easily be detached by the hand.
The paper

paper which is to be made. A favourite size is about
two-and-a-half feet by two feet, but some are nearly six
