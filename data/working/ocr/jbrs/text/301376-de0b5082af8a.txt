There are also other lucky and unlucky directions which must be observed.
An auspicious time is then selected for the success of their expedition.
Having selected the lucky days, time and direction the dacoits or thieves
will proceed with their preparations. A dacoit gang consists of at least 5
members. Any odd number number above five is permissible.
They also believe in good and bad omens ( 8805 ). Should they en-
counter any auspicious omen, this is taken as an indication for success. If
however they pass a funeral house or a cemetery at the entrance to a village,
they turn back from their adventure. If any member of the gang meets
with an accident or is taken ill, they take it as a bad omen, The screeching
of an owl, encountering a snake, the howling of a dog are all bad omens.
Seeing a cat is regarded as a good omen.
There are different kinds of a charms which are generally carried by
the criminals for invulnerability and for warding off evils. In (ε:) is a
charm in which figures, letters, words or symbols are inscribed on slates,
broken bowls, paper or on copper, silver, or gold foil, the space being
divided into columns or squares.
Aing is a charm with figures or characters inscribed on copper foils
for rendering the possessor, gun proof and invisible.
Let-hpwè is an amulet used for invulnerability. It is made of a metallic
foil with in or aing inscribed thereon and is rolled, threaded and tied round
the arm.
Hka-lè is a charm similar to let-hpwè, but worn round the waist.
At are charmed needles made of gold, silver or other base metal. The
metal is usually prepared by means of alchemy, and is inserted under the
flesh in the arms or legs.
Set is a circular charm made of wild boar's solid tusks, deer's horn or
cocoanut shell, and is inserted under the skin in the neck or upper arm.
In preparing these ins, aings, let-hpwè, etc., a stanza in Pāli preceded by the
word om is recited many times over the charm, beneath a big tree, or some-
times at a cemetery where Nats or spirits dwell.
Amade or stone is carried by the criminals as a talisman, and is
obtained from the head of an animal such as a cat, toad, etc. The tip of
an elephant's tusk embedded in a tree trunk after a wild elephant has charged

Tattooing is common amongst criminals. Pigments specially prepared
are used for tattooing charms on the bodies for protection against evils
spirits and dangers. For this purpose auspicious days are chosen, and ins,
figures, numbers and symbols are tattooed on various parts of the body.
This charm is composed of four Burmese letters A Goor 0-6
(Sa-ma) and supposed to represent the 4 elements, water, fire,
earth, air, or the Buddhas. These letters therefore are believed to
possess supernatural powers and protect the person tattooed from all dangers
and evils.

For the purpose of invulnerability and of obtaining power to jump
great heights, most criminals tattoo themselves with the figure of a tiger
or cat or cannibal King "Bodithara."
They also carry with them, charmed handkerchiefs, carved figures of
Nats, or a dat-lon which is a metallic ball prepared by means of alchemy.
Charmed slaked lime is often smeared over the face and the body to make
themselves invulnerable.
During the rebellion a flag bearing on opposite sides the Burmese letters
co meaning Moon and es meaning Sun, was used by the gang, who believed
that they could become invulnerable to alt weapons, if they looked at the
sun by day and the moon by night, until the image of the sun or moon
was definitely fixed on their minds.
They believed that when neither the sun nor moon were visible in-
vulnerability could be achieved by keeping their eyes on the flag. In an
encounter with armed villagers these rebels including their leaders, were
shot dead.
Soporiferous candles are said to be used by burglars at night so that
they may penetrate into a house with no fear of awakening the inmates.
Sometimes they leave behind at the scene of crime some of their own be-
longings such as clothes, slippers, etc., believing that in this way, they will
escape suspicion or capture. Burglars will also commit a nuisance at the
scene of the crime for the same motive.
A murderer will leave his victim face downwards, after cutting out the
tongue, so that he will not be haunted by the ghost of the victim. It is
believed by some criminals that by licking the victim's blood, they will not
be discovered.
Dacoits believe in propitiating the Nats, before attacking any house in
the village. For this purpose they select a big banyan tree or natsin and
offer eatables to the Nats, such as cocoanuts, bananas, sweet rice, jaggaries,
betel leaves and cheroots. Liquor is often offered to the Nats especially
to U Min Kyaw, who is supposed to be a profound drunkard.
Firearms, dahs, spears, etc. are piled up against the tree or natsin whilst
propitiating, and should any weapon fall accidentally, this is considered a
bad omen and the project is abandoned.

SUPERSTITIONS OF BURMESE CRIMINALS.
Through superstition many crimes are committed. Amongst Europeans,
there is a superstition that a piece of stolen raw steak or herring tail buried
in the garden at the dead of night, will cause the disappearance of warts on
their bodies. In Burma it is a common belief that a plant stolen from a
widow's house will cure certain diseases.
Human fat is used for various love charms, hence bodies are exhumed
for the preparation of the charms. In one case a man killed his pregnant
wife in order to eat the unborn child believing that this would render him
visible and invulnerable.
People suffering from diseases are taken to the witch doctors who
often beat them to death whilst driving out the evil spirit.
Some tattooed persons believe that they are dah and gun proof and
many of them succumb to injuries after putting themselves to the test.
Many cases are reported of people bitten to death by snakes, through
their superstitious idea that after being tattooed they are immune from snake
poison. Most people believe in legendary tales and tabaung, which is a
song or a chance saying of a child, lunatic, a person possessed by a spirit,
or by an actor. They also believe in the existence of the philosopher's stone
and the elixir of life. Legendary tales of people who have attained the
supreme objects of alchemy such as turning base metals into gold and silver
and prolonging their lives indefinitely, are handed down from generation
to generation.
It is popularly believed that one Pataman U Aung had achieved this
and is still alive. Another legendary tale which is still believed by Burmese
people is that King Kyanzittha will one day return to his throne.
In the year 1926 a hermit called Bandaka preached to the effect that
the British Rule would come to an end during that year, and that he
(Bandaka) would become a King. Round the outside of his hermitage,
known as Hti-byu-yon (Hall of the White Umbrella) there were the figures
of three white elephants at one corner, and at another corner there were
four other figures representing a King, a King of Nats, Kein-naya, i. e. a
mythical bird with a human head, and a hermit. According to the belief
these figures correspond with an old prophecy which is as follows: -"At
the beginning of the founding of two Kingdoms, from a kyaung, in the
middle of Webula hill on the west, and at the turning of Shwegu Gyaung
(Golden Cave) the hermit will come out with the Ministers, Kein-naya, and
the Sakka, the King of the Nats."

Bandaka engaged a troupe of actors to stage a play entitled Maung-
Te-Za at Bishu. The hero Maung Te Za who was supposed to be a re-
incarnation of King Kyanzittha of the Pagan dynasty, built a Pagoda to
Kya-O in the Sagaing District, during the reign of King Thibaw. The
Headman reported to the King that the Pagoda was really meant to be used
as an arsenal and on hearing this Thibaw ordered the Headman to have
Maung Te Za executed. At the appointed time of execution however,
Patman U Aung suddenly appeared and put the Headman in Te Za's place
on the scaffold. He then told Te Za to come back in Tazaungmon (1288)
1926 and complete the Pagoda at Kya-O, by fixing the umbrella on it.
The actor who took the part of Patman U Aung addressed the audi-
ence as follows:

Bandaka in fact went to Kya-O with the avowed intention of putting
an umbrella on the Pagoda. The Teza play was staged, to make the audi-
ence believe that just as Te Za in the play was a reincarnation of Kyanzittha,
so Bandaka in turn was a reincarnation of Te Za, and that he was going
to Kya-O to fulfil the saying of Patman U Aung. Through this propa-
ganda many people were tattooed at the hermitage and were sold medicine
which was alleged to render them invulnerable, the result being the Shwebo
rising. This was due entirely to ignorance and the making use of super-
stitious ideas of the Burmese people by unscrupulous criminals.
In the recent rebellion of 1931, the rebel chief or Galon King Saya
San, took advantage of the superstitions of his followers. All the members
of the Galon army were tattooed with various kinds of charms, and given
armlets, charmed handkerchiefs and hka-lè to be worn during the attack.
How some criminals trade on the superstitious belief of their countrymen,
may be found in some of the following cases. An alchemist claimed that
he could transmute base metals into gold or increase the weight of gold
ornaments. He invited any body to bring gold ornaments with a promise
to increase the weight of gold by means of his medicine. The gold orna-
ments were then put in a pot with his medicine and kept overnight, and the
next morning, when the pot was opened the gold ornaments had certainly
increased in number. He thus gained the faith of the villagers, and the
next time, when more gold was brought to him the ornaments as well as
the Saya had disappeared by the following morning,

In another case, one alchemist professed that he could turn mercury
into solid silver. He put mercury into a crucible, and heated it in a furnace
before his audience. Before putting on the lid, he covered the mercury with
pieces of charcoal, and a piece of paper with an inscription. After the
operation the crucible was cooled, and on opening a white solid metal was
in evidence. The question was, how did he introduce the solid metal. The
mercury was bought from a local chemist, and was free from any impurities,
and since his charmed paper could not turn mercury into solid silver by
heating, the only conclusion was that he introduced a solid piece of silver
shaped in the form of charcoal. Of course nobody thought of testing the
supposed charcoal. He sold the solid metal recovered, for three or four
times its value to people who believed that by carrying this as a talisman,
they would be protected from evil.
