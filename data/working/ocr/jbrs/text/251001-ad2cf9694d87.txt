THE PREHISTORIC STONE IMPLEMENTS OF BURMA.
BY
T. O, MORRIS
I. INTRODUCTION
The widespread occurrence of prehistoric stone implements in Burma
has long been known. The ground and polished tools of late Neolithic age,
which are hoarded by present day villagers as products of the supernatural,
were noticed by visitors to the country soon after the annexation of Lower
Burma. Later, in 1894, Fritz Noetling, then on the staff of the Indian
Geological Survey, first recorded the occurrence of chipped implements of
much more primitive aspect.
It is impossible to be sure that some of the chipped implements des-
cribed hereunder as palæolithic may not actually be of later date, as they
have all been surface finds. For some types of implement, such as arrow
heads, the early technique of chipping was used long after the time when it
had ceased to be characteristic of the stone working industries as a whole.
A few suspected cases among Burma palæoliths will be mentioned in due
course. Similarly note will be made of the possibility that some of the
neoliths may have been manufactured long after the art of making copper
implements had been introduced into the region. With these qualifications
in mind, however, there can be no objection to the broad classification made
in this paper.
Various distinct types can be recognised among both groups of
specimens, but, up to the present, very little systematic collecting and no
excavation has been carried out with a view to establishing their precise
chronological succession and associations. In this respect geological and
archæological research in Burma has lagged far behind what has been
achieved in recent years in neighbouring countries. Most of the geological
work which is undertaken in Burma has little interest in material newer
than the Middle Pliocene; and from the other side, the 5th century A.D.
civilization of Hmawza remains the earliest stage well documented by
archæological labours.
Between these limits lies the whole of Upper Pliocene and Quaternary
time. During the past few years I have been able to accomplish a prelimin-
ary scaling of this gap by means of the successive terrace levels of the
Irrawaddy and Chindwin Rivers, and so to provide a broad physical back-
ground for further development. Five distinct cycles of erosion, graded to
successively lower sea levels, have taken part in the evolution of the
physiography of the region since the conclusion of the strong post-
Irrawaddian (post Middle Pliocene) crustal folding. A sixth cycle is at
present in progress. For convenience of classification and discussion I have

comparison with the vast field to be covered. We may hope, however, that
this will not remain the state of affairs much longer. In the extensive
deposits of the terraces of the great rivers, in the raised sea beaches, in the
caves of the hills, and in the various known proto-historic centres of civiliza-
tion, Burma possesses a potential wealth of prehistoric material as yet little
appreciated.
II. PALEOLITHIC IMPLEMENTS
(a) Previous research.
The form and materials of these implements differ completely from those
of the ground and polished neolithic tools; hence it is not surprising that they
fail to attract the attention of present day natives of the country. The credit
for their first discovery belongs to Noetling, who noticed some when mapping
and collecting fossils from the basal laterite bed, the "Red Bed", of the
Irrawaddian Series at Yenangyaung (14). Unfortunately Noetling misled
himself with regard to the stratigraphical horizon of his finds, and announced

(b) Distribution of the palæoliths

In the course of my tours in Burma I have been able to note the occurr-
ence of these chipped implements far away from the neighbourhood of
Yenangyaung and its basal Irrawaddian Red Bed. Their distribution, as
known at present, extends over the higher terraces of the Irrawaddy, from
Pagan in the north to Thayetmyo in the south. My finds have mostly been
made from the surface of patches of gravel and the fields of red soil on the
Taungbyinmyint, Taungbyin, and Konbyinmyint. There can be no ques-
tion of derivation from any particular horizon of the Tertiary succession, for
the flakes are of the same types and of the same materials no matter whether
their neighbouring solid rocks are low down in the Peguan or thousands of
feet above the base of the Irrawaddian Series. Furthermore, the materials
of which the implements are made are identical with and very obviously
derived from pebbles of the terrace gravels.✓
I have found a few small chipped flakes as low, speaking topographically,
as the surface of the Konbyin; but not in positions or in sufficient numbers to
feel sure that they might not have been washed or otherwise carried down
from higher levels.
I have examined many good sections of the gravels, sands, and red silts
which are locally preserved over extensive areas of the Taungbyinmyint,
Taungbyin, Konbyinmyint, and Konbyin terraces, and which are the Burma
pluvial equivalents of the glacial and loessic deposits of more northerly
latitudes; but I have not yet discovered any implements actually within them.
This is not to suggest that implements do not occur in these deposits; only
that my opportunities for intensive searching have been inadequate. Similarly
the present limited geographical range of surface finds is, in all probability,
simply a consequence of the favourably sparse vegetation of the "Dry Zone"
of Burma. In the more humid country of Lower Burma and the Chindwin
the forest cover of the higher terrace levels has been but little cleared away
by cultivation, and areas for convenient search are much rarer and smaller
than in the "Dry Zone".
(c) Materials of the palæoliths
No flint is known to occur in Burma. The material to which this term
has been applied by previous authors is mainly silicified rhyolite and rhyolitic
tuff. Pebbles of these rocks were obtained from the terrace gravels, and were
by far the most favoured material of the chipped implement makers.
The samples of finest and most homogeneous crystalline texture possess
working properties little inferior to the Chalk flint of Northwest Europe.
Specimens can be collected showing points and bulbs of percussion, radial
fissures, and concentric waves on the flake scars, as well developed as those
produced on the best flint. The coarser and less homogeneous varieties,
which were available in greater abundance then the very best material,
possess a much less perfect conchoidal fracture, and the flaking of imple-
ments made from them is much inferior in quality.

Pebbles of the best rhyolitic material, as they occur in the terrace
gravels, have a smooth but minutely pitted exterior, generally weathered to a
deep shade of russet brown. On flakes which preserve portions of the
original pebble crust it is evident that the patination produced by weathering
subsequent to flaking is of much lighter shade. The colours developed range
from a light yellowish cream, almost white, to a deep brown. Microscopic
examination shows that these materials are very homogeneous silicified
micro-or crypto-crystalline rhyolites and rhyolitic tuffs.
Two other abundant varieties of rhyolitic material were used. One
developes a streaky white and grey patination, with minute dark speckles;
the other a pink, purple, and creamy patination, generally in contorted
streaks with small spots and blotches. Under the microscope the first type
is seen to be silicified rhyolite with pronounced flow-banding, sometimes
minutely brecciated and containing fragments of tuffaceous material. The
second type comprises silicified crystal and lithic tuffs, the small spots and
blotches on the weather face coinciding with " ghosts" of former phenocrysts
and lapilli.
As a comprehensive term for these related but rather diverse rock types
I have made use of the old field name {{ felsite". It is a very convenient
portmanteau word, it meets the difficulties of accurate diagnosis in the
absence of microscopical aid, and avoids erroneous application of the terms
"flint" and "chert" The four types described above cover all varieties that
are likely to be found and may be summarised briefly as :
(1) yellowish felsite, passing through intermediate shades to
(2) brownish felsite,
(3) streaky grey felsite, and
(4) pink porphyritic felsite, often streaky and brecciated.
Other siliceous materials extensively used for the manufacture of chipped
implements were vein quartz, fine grained quartzite, and monocotyledonous
fossil wood. The fracture of these rocks, however, is much less regular than
that of the felsite group, and their use was much less favoured.
(d) Classification of the palæoliths
Until further investigations have established a stratigraphical succession
of chipped implement types for Burma any attempt to arrange surface finds
in a time sequence on the evidence of their own form must depend on the
succession of cultures already worked out in Europe and around the
Mediterranean. Considered from this point of view it seems to me that
representatives of three cultural stages, widely separated in time, can be
recognised in collections made up to the present - Eolithic, Chellean, and
Upper Palæolithic.
On the Taungbyinmyint and Taungbyin surfaces large pebbles and
fragments of pebbles with a rude chipping, which seems to have been
designed to produce points and edges similar to those of the Kent plateau
eoliths (19 fig. 12), or the more primitive Choukoutien (21), or Java (4)
types, are not uncommon. I have not noticed anything quite like these
specimens on the lower terraces.

Scrapers.-Three distinct kinds of scrapers may be distinguished.
(1) Side Scrapers (Plate II)-Each consists of a cleanly detached

The larger specimens appear to have close analogues in the Madras

(2) End Scrapers (Plate III, figs. 1, 2, and 3)-These were made from

I have remarked above that the form and materials of the chipped
implements are quite distinct from those of the neoliths. To this distinction
may be added that of distribution - the palæoliths seem to be restricted to
the higher terraces, whereas neoliths occur on all levels down to and includ-
ing the Konbyin, and probably within the Lebyin alluvium.
Most of the common Burma neoliths resemble types characteristic of
Neolithic industries elsewhere throughout much of the Old World. The
evolution of manufacturing technique and of form seems to have been similar
to that in regions as far distant as Scandinavia and Northern China. But
most usefully, however, for the classification of scattered surface finds in
Burma, we may turn to the successive stages of neolithic industry worked out
and beautifully illustrated by the Geological Survey of Indo-China, and
recognise implements absolutely identical in form.
A period of transition is shown by the oldest implementiferous layers
revealed by cave excavations in Indo-China (11, pp. 41-45), and is supposed
to indicate contact between a race, probably Australoid, in the palæolithic
stage of culture, and a more advanced people, probably ancestors of present
day Melanesians, who had already learnt the arts of grinding and polishing
their implements and of pottery manufacture. In the cave deposits of the
Bac Son and Hoa Binh regions in Tonkin and the kitchen-middens of the
coastal belt the French investigators have been able to trace the gradual
development of neolithic artefacts into a stage when copper and bronze
implements also came into use.

(2) Middle Neolithic-grinding was often extended over the whole surface

(3) Upper Neolithic-characterised by perfection and finish of stone tools.

(b) Classification of the neoliths
Nearly all the Burma neoliths I have seen were obviously the cutting
tools of a wood-working people. They comprise axes, adzes, wedges, and
chisel blades, in design very similar to the iron tools of indigenous manu-
facture used by the present inhabitants of the country. Indeed, the few
copper and bronze axes known from Burma indicate that here as elsewhere
the earliest metal implements were essentially copies of their Neolithic
predecessors.
Apart from wood-working tools the only other neoliths that have been
recorded are four ring stones, which were probably grinding wheels used for
making the highly finished Upper Neolithic cutting tools; the large stone
ploughshare (?), mentioned above; and one pounder or hammerstone.

The small size and narrow blades of some adzes, such as Plate IX, fig. 2,
give some idea of the fine woodwork of which their Neolithic users must have
been capable. Such specimens as plate VIII, fig. 4, were probably socketed
in the end of straight handles and used precisely as modern wood chisels.
The Upper Neolithic rectangular shouldered celts were almost certainly
used for trimming timber, after the manner of adzes, rather than for rough
chopping. Wider stouter blades, like plate IX, fig. 1, were probably the
implements used for rougher work. This particular specimen must have
been about eight inches long before it was broken.
In the strong thick implements, with blunt butts and wide-angled
symmetrical cutting edges, such as plate VIII, figs. 1, 2, and 3, I think we
may recognise wedges which were used in conjunction with wooden mallets
*for splitting timber evenly along the grain.
Some of the axes collected by Mr N. G. Cholmeley in the Kachin Hills
and the Northern Shan States are quite different from any I have seen in the
lowland country. One specimen, plate X, fig. 1, is a flat blade with plane
ground sides which curve rapidly outwards towards the cutting edge. Five
others, like plate X, fig. 2, have long rounded tangs which expand rapidly
into flat blades with strongly arcuate cutting edges. In shape these imple-
ments so closely resemble advanced Bronze Age celts that it seems probable
they were copies of such implements, made by people whose supply of metals
was inadequate for their needs.
The axes in Mr A. Braybon's collection from the Liepok Chaung,
Mergui District, are more like Malayan and Javan types than specimens
from Burma proper; in particular the pointed adze or hoe, plate X, fig. 3.
The large implement collected by Mr W. A. Robertson from Toungoo, plate
XI, is similar but much heavier.
Ring Stones.-Ring stones, quoit discs, or "anneaux disques", of various
materials, sizes, and degrees of finish have been recorded from Neolithic
industries throughout the Far East - from the Yang Shao culture of North
China, from south China, from Indo-China, from Malaya, and from Burma,
It is difficult to believe, in view of the time and care which must have been
spent on their manufacture, that the Burma specimens figured herewith ever
received the rough treatment suggested by the term "quoit", either for
hunting, for games, or for ceremonial. They are much more delicate than
the rough sub-spherical ring stones which have been described from Southern
India (and which are still used to-day by primitive tribes) as mace-heads(9, p.
19; pl. 19, no. 3395; and pl. 52, no. 4085); on the other hand they are much
heavier and have a much smaller central hole than the circlets which almost
certainly served as arm or leg ornaments (for example, 1, pl. VI, figs. 3, 5,
6, and 7). It would be impossible to pass anything larger than a very small
child's hand or foot through the central hole of any of the four Burma ring
stones under discussion. It seems much more probable that these latter were
used as slitting, grinding, and polishing discs for the manufacture of the late
Neolithic shouldered adzes, etc. The ends of linear notches, such as would
be made by a sharp-edged ring stone mounted on a lathe, often remain at the
intersection of shoulders and tang of the shouldered adzes, and it requires but

broken into fragments by children a short time after it had been shown to -

The above three specimens are all ground to a sharp edge along their
external circumference and have central holes of uniform diameter. A
fragment of another ring stone, from Thakat village, Patolon Chaung,
Lower Chindwin District, has a central hole which diminishes from one side
of the disc to the other and has a spiral groove cut in it, apparently to make
it grip a wooden axle more effectively (Plate XIII, fig. 1). The external
edge is about 0.35 of an inch wide, and has a shallow groove cut in it.
Pounding Stones.-I know of only one Burma neolith of this type, which
I found at Myaing Sakan, about six miles southeast of Sinlamaung village,
Upper Chindwin (Plate XIII, fig. 2). The butt end and cross section are sub-
ellipsoidal, but one side is more convex than the other. In general shape
and size the implement resembles the bark-cloth beaters of Malaya, but
carries no signs of the incised cross-hatching characteristic of those imple-
ments.
