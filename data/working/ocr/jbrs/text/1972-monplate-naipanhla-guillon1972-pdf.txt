A MON COPPER PLATE IN THE NATIONAL
LIBRARY, BANGKOK *
Nai Pan Hla & E. Guillon
It
This copper plate belongs to the
Manuscript and Inscription Division of
the National Library in Bangkok,
measures 53 cm long, 95 cm broad and
2 cm thick and has six lines of Mon
inscriptions written on each side. Apart
from the rarity and value of this kind
of copper plate, the Mon writings of the
plate give us much that is of great interest
and from several points of view. From
them, we are able to trace the history
of the spelling of the words and of
course, of the historical events and precise
date of the record.
This copper plate has been translated
into Thai by Nai Cham Thong-khamwan
(Dongamvarma) in 1961 and it was publi-
shed with Burmese translation by U Chit
Thein of Archaeological Survey of Burma
in 1965. Over half a century ago,
however, Robert Halliday, who had
contributed much to our knowledge of
the Mon language, mentioned this inscrip-
tion in his article entitled “Immigration
of the Mons into Siam" which appeared
in J.S.S. Vol X, part 3, 1913. He wrote
"It may be mentioned that there is here
in the National Library in Bangkok a
copper plate recording in Mon the found-
ing of a pagoda by King Bañā Rām".
We wonder why Halliday referred it
to Bañā Rām. It was engraved not by
King Bañā Rām but by his Chief Com-
mander Siri Manorājā who erected the
said pagoda.
The oldest copper plate which came
to light in Thailand at the northern end
of U T'ong was inscribed in Sanskrit
with pre-Angkorian script. It apparently
shows Dvāravatī Mon influence and can
bè dated earlier than the 7th century A.
D. This Sanskrit old copper plate also
contains six lines but only on
one side.¹
What were the purposes of these plates
and where were they kept? Probably under
the stupas or some other religious monu-
ments. And what were the origins of our
copper plate? The inscriptions were written
in 1503 in the Haṁsāvatī Kingdom but how
did the plate find its way to Thailand?
We do not want to trace in detail the
historical background of Southeast Asia
at that time but we can certainly say that
the present Mons in Thailand are not
descendants of the Dvārávati or Hari-
puñjaya Kingdoms but they had come
over there from Burma as far back as
the 16th century and probably even more
recently. One of the reasons for the
migrations was that they were granted
several times by the Thai monarchs, plots
of free land for cultivation. During these
emigrations (perhaps six times), the Mons
took with them palm-leaf manuscripts
and other religious objects, (the tradition
still exists amongst the Mons of Burma)

A Short Note on Mon Lexicography
"
44
17
In editing the Mon Copper Plate we consulted many dictionaries. Out af all, A Hand
Book of Peguan Language” by H. M. Bixby seems to be the earliest. It was written in 1860 but
not published. The manuscript is now preserved in the main Library of Rangoon Arts and
Science University. "The Grammatical Notes and Vocabulary of the Peguan Language by J. M.
Haswell was published in 1874 in Rangoon. "A Vocabulary of English and Peguan" by E. O.
Stevens was published in Rangoon in 1896 and his “Mon-English-Burmese Dictionary came out
in 1901.
"A Mon-English Dictionary" by Robert Halliday was printed in Bangkok in 1922 and has
been reprinted in 1955 by the Mon Cultural Section of the Ministry of Culture, Rangoon,
A Dictionary of Modern Spoken Mon by H. L. Shorto came out in 1962. His latest work on
"A
Dictionary of the Mon Inscriptions from the Sixth to the Sixteenth Centuries* which was published by
the University of London at the end of 1971 is so far the best hand book for every student of
Mon studies. The author is now professor of Mon- hmer Studies in the School of Oriental and
African Studies, University of London. Despite a few entries, Shorto's work is admirable. Besides
his own collection, he mentioned that he made use of some 5,000 index cards left by the late Dr.
C. O. Blagden, master of Mon inscriptions who died in 1948. We observe that Professor Shorto
mostly follows Dr. Blagden's and Dr. Luce's readings and renderings of Old Mon inscriptions.
Consequently, he enters the word pumiñ for the meaning "full" and the word pupiñ for "to fill
Obviously pumiñ is a misreading by Dr. Blagden for pupiñ which means full, to be full
Old Mon word for the meaning "to fill" is spupiñ. The prefix s indicates future tense.
"
Another important instance is the word kun ko' which he accepts Dr. Luce's reading and
rendering. This gloss appears both in Myinkaba Kubyaukgyi and Lokahteikpan temples at Pagan
dating back to early 12th century. Dr. Luce was right in the former temple but doubtful in the
latter. The correct reading in the latter is knań ko' and not kun ko'; knań ko' means collar-bone
and not princely garments of Bodhisatva as plausibly rendered by Luce. However, Shorto is right
for gloss 195 at Myinkaba Kubyaukgyi temple knaň ko' meaning collar-bone, Except for such a
few entries Shorto's dictionary of Mon inscriptions is extremely helpful and highly reliable.
