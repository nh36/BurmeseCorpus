THE JÁTAKA AT pagán
* The main texts of the Tipitaka appear to have reached Pagán from Ceylon
only in the closing years of Aniruddha's reign, c. 1075 A. D. The one exception is
the Jātaka Commentary, with its 550 stories of the Buddha's previous lives. Aniruddha
first used some of these, on a series of unglazed terracotta plaques, to line the bottom
plinth of his Shwéhsandaw pagoda (c. 1060 A.D.). These have no writing to identify
them. But the two complete sets of the 550 Jātakas, on unglazed plaques, which
adorn the corridors he built (perhaps c. 1070 A. D.) around the East and West
Hpetleik pagodas at Lokananda, are each named and numbered. His source here was
clearly not the Singhalese, whose recension has only 547 stories, but a similar one (possi-
Bly from South India, say Kañcipura, capital of the Pallavas), which included three
additional Jātakas; 497 Velāma, 498 Mahagovinda, and 499 Sumedhapandita: thus
adding 3 to the subsequent numbers, ending at 550 Vessantara. The confusion caused
by this and other differences in the numbering (including the Singhalese subdivision
of the overall number into 22 Nipāta and subsidiary Vagga) is painfully apparent in
the plaques of the Shwezigon (v. OBEP I, pp. 269-274), the first of Kyanzittha's
pagodas (c. 1086 A. D.). It was doubtless this confusion which led to that king's
next care: to collect and purify the Tipitaka, which had become obscured and
corrupt” (it id., p. 61). By the time he built his final masterpiece, the Nanda temple
(a 1105 AaD. 2), the three extra Jātakas were omitted, and the Pagán numbering
generally agrees with the Singhalese, though a good many variant titles persist even as
late as the recent Burma Sixth Council edition (v. Artibus Asiae, Vol! XXXIII, 3 (1971),
pp. 172-175). The names and numbers of the last ten Jātakas (Mahānipāta), which
Aniruddh doubtless obtained after his capture of Thaton (c. 1057 A.D.), show marked
differences:-
