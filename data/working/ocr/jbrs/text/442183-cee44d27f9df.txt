The concept of deficit used in this paper relates to the overall or budget dificit
embracing the Central Government, State Governments and Boards and Corporations.
It takes into account their cash transactions both with the private sector and their
intra payments and receipts as well. An example of the latter assumes the form of custom
duties paid by Boards and Corporations to the Central Government and the receipts by
the Boards for the sale of their products such as cement, steel and medicinal products to
the Central Government. Of these intra-payments loans and advances made by the
Central Government to the State Boards is shown separately to bring out a more realistic
position of the latter. Likewise current and capital contributions as well as income tax
payments by SAMB is also shown separately to give a clearer picture of the budgetary
position of the Central Government.
The aim of this paper is to show three aspects of the budgetary position. The first
is to compare the tax efforts of the Government to meet current expenditure with that of
the pressure on aggregate expenditure of the government complex especially that of the
Central Government and the second to examine how the Government had financed the
budgetary gap, and lastly the impact of such financing on the internal stability of the
economy.
It is a common knowledge that insurrection reached its height during 1948-9,
when Burma could export only one million ton of rice. Trade was at a standstill.
Revenues from customs, the mainstay of the Government tax income, dropped by more
than half from K 172 million in 1947-8 to K 97 million in 1948-9. Revenue from land
fell drastically from K 35 million to K 5 million. Total revenue from taxes declined by
37 percent and stood at K 174 million.
Paradoxically expenditures on both current and capital account declined during this
worst period of disturbance. The explanation is to be found in the stoppage of rehabilita-
tion works in the field of transportation, communication, forestry and mines and in the
provision of social welfare services. The rise in the defence expenditure was too weak a
factor to offset the above. As the budget estimates did not distinguish between current and
capital, it is not possible to indicate whehter current receipts were adequate to meet
current expenditure. Since the scope of the Central Government capital undertakings was

It seems that the Government thinking on orthodox lines of fiscal management was
disturbed by this current deficit. It took immediate measures to curtail current expendi-
tures. Hence a cut in wages and salaries was made with effect from 1 January 1949.
also instituted twenty-five percent retrenchment in services other than defence and police.
Furthermore expenditures that could be postponed were stayed.
Despite the low level of rice exports, the price of rice was so favourable as to
produce a surplus in SAMB budget. The financial position of other Boards comprising
of Railways, IWTB, STB and Civil Supplies, etc. also showed a surplus as derived from
the changes in their cash balances with the Union Bank and treasuries. With SAMB and
other Boards, surpluses exceeding the overall deficit on Central Government account, the
drastic measures that were taken seemed to be uncalled for.
During the year 1949-50 the policy aim was for a balanced budget. The Govern-
ment seemed to be unaware of the fact that the overall financial position was strong during
the year before. The call for a balanced budget was motivated by the fear of deficit on
Central Government account only. No attempt was then made to work out a comprehen-
sive budget. To realise a balanced budget the Government put forth a plea for austerity.
At the same time tax rates on betting were raised by 59 percent, tariff rates on liquor was
raised by another 50 percent, and rates on super-taxes and entertainment taxes were
enhanced. Curiously enough, the Finance Minister was obsessed with the dangers of
inflation and gave an assurance that no matter how grave the circumstances were, the
Government would not permit currency in circulation to rise above the 1948-9 level. It
seemed that the Government was seeking a wrong remedy in fiscal policy when the real
cause of the rise in prices was the structural dislocations in the economy and the partial
breakdown in the distributive system consequent to internal strife. True to its promise, the
Government succeeded in cutting down its deficit to nearly half of what it was a year
before.
The budgetary surplus of SAMB showed a larger surplus owing to a better terms
of trade and a rise in volume exported. The surpluses of other Boards were reduced.
However the surpluses of all the State enterprises showed a much bigger surplus, which
exceeded the deficit on the Central Government account, thereby producing an overall
surplus of K 186 million. Fiscal policy could therefore be dissociated with the rise in
cost living to its peak level which had its origin in the political disturbances within the
country and outbreak of Korean War in the Far East.
The year 1950-51 saw a feverish preparation to meet scarcities that might arise
should the Korean War spread. The Government therefore adopted the policy of
stockpiling of essential commodities. Import control on textile and other essential
commodities were liberalized, as a result there occurred a heavy influx of cotton
piecegoods. Consequently receipts from custom duties and commercial taxes improved

By 1950-51 the tide of internal struggle had turned in favour of the Government.
Partly to win the hearts of the people and partly out of sheer humanitarian sentiments,
the Government committed itself to a welfare scheme. The Budget introduced for the
year was called a 'Welfare Budget'. Under the Welfare Budget of 1950-51 wage cuts
were restored. Expenditures on defence were raised. Of all, expenditures on education,
health and social welfare were increased most to conform with the Welfare Budget.
Facilities for child health were extended and a Health Assistant School was opened. As a
relief to peasants and farmers who suffered the most from insurrection, outstanding
land revenue and loans to co-operative societies relating to all post-war years up to and
including 1948-9 were written off.
With respect to capital expenditures, major outlays were made for aviation in
connection with the purchase of stores, plants and contractual work for the construction
of the Mingaladon Air Port. Railway lines were also rehablitated. The Government
spoke of a well-balanced capital budget meaning thereby a fifty-fifty division of capital
expenditure on revenue earning projects and non-revenue earning social welfare schemes.
Thanks to the liberalization of imports, cost of living in Rangoon spiralled
down by 40 percent during the year. As revenues from customs and commercial
taxes rose more than the increased expenditures on social welfare and
capital projects coupled with a substantial rise in repayments of loans taken by
public utilities and trading enterprises, the overall deficit of the Central Government came
down to K 96 million. With the improvement in security conditions and the high price
for rice exports, SAMB was able to reap a cash surplus of K. 333 million. Among other
state enterprises the surpluses of Civil Supplies, IWTB and STB were drained off on
account of a heavy deficit of Burma Railways. A net deficit was thereby recorded for the
first time and continued throughout the rest of the decade.
The surplus of SAMB being greater than combined deficit of the Central Govern-
ment and other Boards, the overall position turned to a sizable surplus.
The year 1951-2 witnessed a real improvement in internal security. Expenditures
on defense account registered a further increase as policing and patrolling of large areas
freed from insurgent occupation necessitate an expansion in armed forces. Attempt at
achieving more security went hand-in-hand with the promotion of economic and social
well being. Mass education movement was expanded from twenty one centres to more
than one hundred centres. Free tuition in all State Schools and in the University made a
large subsidy from the Central Government a necessity. Old schools were reopened.
Stipends and scholarships in increasing numbers were granted. The object of increasing
outlays on education was to prevent the ignorant and the illiterate populace from falling
an easy prey to insurgents' wild propaganda.

The year 1951-2 is significant in that it marked the adoption by the Government
of an economic policy aimed at a large scale development and provision of maximum
employment, along with a heavy emphasis on social welfare. Capital expenditures, there-
fore, were stepped up. Capital expenditures were continued to be made in the
construction of Mingaladon Air Port. Capital expenditures of the Boards also went up.
The construction of Spinning and Weaving Factory was completed during the year.
Resumption of Inland Waterways Services and Railway Services through area previously
infested and occupied by insurgents involved additional capital expenditures.
All told,
the execution of capital projects on both Central Government and State Boards'
account excluding SAMB had raised the deficit from K 186 million in 1950-51 to K 212
million. Among other factors, the favourable price of rice enabled SAMB despite the drop
in shipments, to come out with a record level of cash surplus of K 478 million. The
difference went to augment the foreign exchange reserves.
Although the policy for economic development was laid down in 1951-2, it was
only in 1952-3 that development activities started in earnest. The Pyidawtha Conference
was convened in August 1953 with the purpose to instal enthusiasm and win the support
of the people in implementing the programme. A fiscal innovation was introduced
whereby discretionary grants were made to be spent on local projects like roads, bridges,
schools, wells, medical dispensaries and libraries. The people, who were to be benefited
were to contribute roughly an equal amount in money, in kind and in free labour. The
Government went ahead with its social programme, embracing education, health and
social services, started in 1951-2. The Government had expressed in most unequivocal
terms its desire to expand social welfare schemes along with the investment programme.
An endeavour was also made during the year towards the religious revival. Increased
contribution was therefore made to the Buddha Sassana Council for the holding of the
Sixth Buddhist Synod and an additional sum for the rehabilitation of the Shwemawdaw
Pagoda. True to its words, a nominal expenditure of K 44 million on welfare activities
was stepped up to K 100 million in 1952-3. Total current expenditure rose sharply from
its previous level.
On the investment side, to execute the development projects, IDC, MRDC and
ARDC were formed during the year. Not much capital expenditure was made by the
former two because the kind of work to be undertaken was completely new and were
inhibited in several respects by the lack of law and order. Expenditures that were made
were confined to the organisational expense. The State Agricultural Bank was formed
during the year to extend much needed credit facilities in villages.
At the very outset of launching the development programme, the Government
was aware that the job of repairing the physical damage due to war and insurrection and
the task of lifting the economy from its low income trap would cost immensely in men,
material and money. The government realised that it could not do without a deficit which
would cause money in circulation to increase. In defence of the Central Government
deficit that was incurred during 1951-2, the government argued that in the prevailing
situation of a glut in the textile market, the net injection of money by the Government

Government's position with respect to a deficit budget became more clear. The Finance
Minister explained at length what an 'unbalanced budget' meant, and that an unbalanced
budget if deliberately unbalanced for the purpose of capital formation cannot be deemed
undesirable. The year 1952-3 can therefore be said to have marked a departure from its
earlier orthodox fiscal policy of keeping within its means no matter how grave the situation
may be.
The year 1953-4 saw the end of the rice boom. There were other developments
also. The old preferential tariff schedule was replaced by a new tariff schedule with its
distinguishing feature of high rates on non-essential items and low rates on capital goods.
The tariff was designed for both protective and revenue purposes. Coupled with the
rise in rates on consumer goods, the value of which itself had risen, custom duties rose
from 200 million to K 254 million or by 25%. Other tax revenue increased also, but
they were not appreciable. Despite the raising of exemption and earned income limits,
income taxes had increased due to a high level of consumer imports leading to a brisk
distributive trade as well as to a stricter assessment and collection.
Among current expenditure social welfare activities which continued to enjoy the
blessings of the Government, rose by 23 percent. The most striking increase of K 47
million in current expenditure took place under defence head. This expansion in the
armed forces had its background in the KMT aggression, the consolidation of liberated
areas and the need to liquidate the remaining insurgents. In this second year of active
implementation, capital expenditures of both the Central Government and State enterprises
gained momentum; that of the central government rose by K. 36 million to reach a mark
of K 265 million which figure excludes debt repayments and capital outlays on SCB and
and SAB, while that of the state enterprises, including SAMB, rose by as much as
K 236 million to hit a level of K 322 million.
Capital Expenditures incurred were for defence, irrigation, steel mill, jute mill,
sugar mills, pharmaceutical as well as for the housing programme. It appears that the
government was under the illusion that the success that would attend the industrial
projects would enable it to finance its social welfare schemes.
To make matters worse, the shift from a seller's market to a buyer's market in the
rice market had brought down the export price of rice. As a consequence SAMB overall
surplus shrunk to a low level of K. 176 from the previous high level of K 454 million.
Despite the fall in SAMB surplus it managed to pay the government more in taxes and
contribution by drawing down its cash balances.
Because of the heavy current and capital programme, overall deficit appalling shot
up from the previous level of K. 57 million to K 548 million (where not otherwise stated
the deficit refers to the financing figures) the highest to be recorded since independence.
As envisaged the gap was covered largely by drawing down accumulated cash balances and
by borrowing from the banks by as much as 95 percent.

Consequent to the fall in the export price of rice round about the close of 1953,
expenditures on current account of the central government was reduced. However it was
found not easy to reduce and a saving in current expenditure of only K 27 million would
be effected. Cut in current expenditure was chiefly made under defence which more than
offset the rise in education and health.
In the drive to safeguard foreign exchange reserves certain investment undertakings
were curtailed, deferred or rephased over longer time period. As a result total capital
expenditures declined from a level of K 587 million to K. 531 million. A cut of K 56
million was in the Central Government capital expenditure for civil works and defence,
while capital expenditures under all Boards and Corporation remained relatively stable.
There was however a shift in emphasis with construction expenditure at K 129 million
compared to K 68 million a year before.
It constituted 24 percent of total capital expenditure. This had its origin in the
large scale housing programme under which 1051 buildings were under construction as of
March 1955. Expenditure on agriculture, irrigation, forestry showed a corresponding
decline.
The year closed with a reduction in the overall deficit to K 485 million, the absolute
magnitude of which is by no means small. Three factors may be traced for the decline in
overall deficit. The first and the most important is the SAMB surplus which doubled
during the year. The second to the further gains in two receipts and finally to the fall in
capital expenditures of the cental government. Speaking in defence of the deficit for the
second time, the government pointed out that neither a surplus nor balance one was to be
derived for its own sake. What was of paramount importance was the speedy rehabi-
litation and progressive development so as to keep up with the other developing countries,
which cannot be achieved by limiting expenditures to the meagre revenues received each
year. The overall deficit was financed by borrowing from the banks on an unprecedented
scale, and constituted 91 percent of total financing.
During 1955-6 fresh efforts to raise revenues were made by raising the rates on
excise duties on such commodities as petrol, sugar and cigarettes, and stricter collection of
income taxes from private sector, bringing in an extra K 16 million and K 29 million
respectively. These gains were more than offset by losses from custom duties and
commercial taxes owing to the restriction of imports in March 1955. Together with
other losses, tax revenues were brought down by K 37 million.

With regard to current expenditure, it resumed upwards by K 15 million to a evel
of K 745 million. The rise in current expenditure was attributable to the continued
emphasis on law and order and social welfare. Expenditure for defence alone was
responsible for K 11 million rise. Social services expenditures which had persistantly
grown from a mere K 44 million in 1948-9 rose by K 21 million to K 170 million.
Capital expenditure during 1955-6 came down by K. 36 million to K 495 million
which figure excludes debt payments to India and capital outlay on SCB. The reduction
particularty reflects the attempt to avoid further depletion of foreign exchange reserves. It
may be recalled that the sharp drop in foreign exchange during the previous year had
caused much apprehension among the general public. The decline in capital expenditures
was entirely on the Central Government account. The decline in total capital expenditure
was not altogether deliberate. By the end of 1955 a large number of capital payments
under Boards and Corporations had reached the operating stage as evidenced in the sharp
rise in their operating receipts.
The overall deficit of K 240 million as calculated from the financing end was
much smaller compared to K 485 million and K. 548 million of the earlier two years.
The reason for a smaller deficit is to be found in lower deficit on Central Government as
well as other Board's accounts and to the significant improvement in SAMB cash surplus.
To close the deficit foreign reserves could no longer be tapped Hence a net imflationary
borrowing of K 100 million from the banking system, defined as net borrowing from the
banks less the increasease in cash balances was resorted to. In addition loans and grants
to the amount of K. 87 million was obtained. The first accounted for 42% and the second
36% of total finacial resources.
Revenue reccipts during 1956-7 improved remarkably after a slight setback a year
earlier. It stood at K764 milion compared to the average level of K 563 million during
the preceding three years. Factors responsible for the increase were the record level of
imports that brought in substantial increases in custom duties and commercial taxes.
Other revenue heads also indicate good performance.
Part of the increased revenues was lost in mecting the expansion in the
provision of current services. Other expansions were also also observed under many expen-
diture heads. The heaviest demand came from defence which increased by K 12 million
and from social welfare. Despite difficulties in finding additional resources for capital
formation, the Government was still keeping social welfare close at heart.
The drop in capital expenditures during the year was made possible by a substantial
decline on Board's account exceeding that of a slight rise on central government account.
Dispite this lower capital expenditure and notwithstanding the sharp reduction in current
deficit of the Central Government which had always been a dead weight on the resources
for investment, overall deficit of the government complex had increased. This was because
the surplus of all Boards had more than halved especially that of SAMB.

By this time the Government could no longer afford to draw down foreign
exchange. On the contrary it had become one of the policy objectives to have it raised to
dispel fears among the general public. The Government therefore relied heavily on
foreign loans and grants which accounted for 92 percent of total financial resources.
Although the government received PL 480 imports, they cannot be regarded as a
resource to cover the deficit because these imports have to be paid for in local currency
which the Government must either be charged against current receipts or borrow from the
banks and public. However PL 480 imports are not without significance. Their signi-
ficance lies in making whatever inflationary borrowing that might develop to be less
inflationary.
In 1957-8 further tax efforts were made. Efforts at raising tax revenues were
usually made in preparation for increase in current expenditures. Most frequently what
came in as additional revenues were more than drained off by the increments in current
expenditures. This happened again in 1957-8 when additional revenues were raised from
excise duties on cigarettes, from the enhancement of tariff rates on luxury and semi-luxury
imports and from the rise in stamp duties and court fees. Income tax laws were amended
to bring excessive capital gains under taxation. These efforts to gain more tax revenues
were nullified by restrictions on imports and the subsequent decline in business activities.
However, owing to the rise in non-tax revenues from domestic sources total revenues
showed a moderate gain of K 38 million. As has often happened gains in revenues were
accompanied by excessive rise in current expenditure rendering the current account of
the Central Government to dip more deeply in the red. In 1957-8 expenditure heads under
which major increases took place were in defence, police, contribution to stage govern-
ments, pension, education and health. The rise in defence alone by K 46 million was
more than sufficient to enlarge the current deficit of the central government to K 135
million.
Despite the drastic fall in the volume of rice exported SAMB showed a current
surplus at about the same level as in the previous year when a record level of rice exports
took place. The position of other Boards was more or less breakeven. State governments
for the first time produced a small surplus. Setting against these current source of finance,
the current deficit of the Central Governments, would leave a sum equal to approximately
28 percent of investment expenditures of K 473 million. Taking into consideraiion
capital receipts other than borrowings, the capital programme during 1957-8 produced
an overall deficit of K 308 million which was financed to the extent of 80% by loans and
grants.
Notwithstanding the change in administration in 1958-9 the finances of the
government continued to be characterised by a current deficit on account of the Central
Government and of a magnitude about the same as in previous year. In one respect it has
worsened, for the current surplus of SAMB has ebbed to a low figure of K 130 million.
In another, it has improved for the rest of the Boards have come to show a sizeable
surplus since their inception. Setting against the current surplus of the Boards and
Corporations against the current deficit of the Central Government there remained only

In the way of generalisation it may be stated that the first four years of indepen-
dence was marked by large overall surpluses. It was primarily due to two factors, one
affecting receipts and the other expenditure. Through the system of pricing and control
of the export of trade in rice, the government was able to draw a third to a half of its
annual resources from the sale of rice abroad in the form of rehabilitation contribution.
On the other hand, despite the crippled stage of the economy during these four years total
expenditures of the government complex stood lower than the pre-insurrection period.
because the decline in extraction, construction, transport and communication together with
expenditure on social services more than offset the rise in defence and administrative
expenditures. Execution of the development plan had not yet begun.
The overall surpluses thus created was unplanned in contrast to the planned
overall deficit of later years. These surpluses did not exert a deflationary impact on the
economy, for they represent current saving of the government from incomes that accrued
from abroad. Judging from the surpluses of SAMB and the changes in the cash balances
alone, one might contend that the government was spending more than what it received
from the public.
The year 1952-3 ushered in a period of persistantly large and uninterrupted
deficits. In 1952-3 the excess of current receipts over current expenditures had contribut-
ed greatly to investment expenditures. Since then i.e. begining 1953-4 this current
excess dropped by more than half and remained relatively stable until the end of 1955-6.
During these three years, the expenditure gap of 1953-4 and 1954-5 was almost entirely
closed by way of inflationary borrowing from the banks and by draw down of each
balances. In just one year, that is in 1953-54, the government had used up its entire net
accumulation of the post, defined as the excess of current receipts from all sources over its
capital expenditures, as reflected in the drawn and government cash balances. It is to be
noted that the increases or the decreases in foreign exchange reserves of the country do
not signify that the government is either accumulating or decumulating. The reason is
when foreign exchange rise the monetized kyats may be spent within the country without
affecting the reserves.

The impact of the manner in which the overall deficit is financed is revealed in
Table II wherein the excess of government domestic payments over its domestic receipts is
derived backwards from the increase in private money supply. The reason for computing
the net domestic cash deficit in this way is because the figure of net private foreign
payments is believed to be larger than other variables such as the overall deficit and the
net government receipts from abroad. In addition the calculation is only one step away
from the actual change in the private money supply. However when crosschecked from
many angles one would lose confidence in the validity of these figures.
With these shortenings in mind the relationship between the domestic cash deficit
and the increase private money supply will be examined.
In the years 1953-4 and 1954-5 when the overall deficit was financed by borrowing
from the banks and draw down of cash balances, cash deficit were at high levels. When-
ever these deficits were sufficiently offset by liberal imports the expansions in money supply
were kept down at moderate levels. It cannot be argued that the sharp rise in the money
supply is brought about by excessive cash deficit. This fact is brought out by comparing
the events of 1954-5 and 1955-6. In spite of sharp drop in cash deficit, money supply
rose substantially because of a small import leakage. One important conclusion may be
drawn from the table.
If a large domestic cash deficit is incurred the Government is faced with two
choices, either maintain internal stability or strengthen its reserves. The present practice
of framing the budget is condusive to keeping overall expenditure within the bonds of
available resources both domestic and foreign. A resource table is draw up to determine
what the capital programme should be. The capital programme so determined is
embodied in the foreign exchange budget. Wherein the data pertaining to private imports
is taken as a variable to be adjusted as required. The foreign exchange budget will thus
reveal the reserve position at the end of the fiscal year. Finally from informations
available from the Resources Estimates and the foreign exchange budget, a cost consoli-
dated budget is constructed so as to bring out the impact on private money supply. If
the change in private money supply is deemed excessive adjustments are made to alter the
cash deficit of the government with the private sector. For such adjustments current and
capital expenditures are reexamined and curtailments are made accordingly.
Two serious difficulties are confronted in practice. The first is the absence of
priority assignments in deciding which expenditure in the current budget is to be cut and
secondly the lack of proper criteria in setting the pattern of investment.
These two
drawbacks have prevented the use of resources in the most productive manner.
Permit me to conclude with some fiscal lessons which are obvious. Since the
Burmese economy is highly dependent on the export of rice for the greater part of its
exchange, external prices of this commodity play a decisive role in the maintenance of
financial stability. High prices with low expenditures have produced surpluses. Later
superimposition of extensive investment programme on ths budget have brought about

large deficits relying first on cash balances accumulated in the past, later on borrowings

Fiscal policy in the past had effected mostly the volume of income and employment,
the distribution of incomes between classes and sectors, composition of investment,
allocation of productive resources into various economic activities, stability of general
price level of some segment of the price system and equilibrium in the balance of
payments. The sharp reversal in the terms of trade in 1953-4 has brought home the
fact that the maintenance of high income and employment, internal stability and balance
of payment equilibrium are objectives which could not be achieved simultaneously. In an
economy wide open to foreign trade as Burma is, attempts at rapid growth often conflicts
with external balance and consequently domestic price stability. A compromise solution
is therefore imperative. A budgetary restraint is a must whenever export proceeds are not
good or foreign assistance meagre. Two areas of restraint comes to my mind. They are
social services and defence expenditure. The provision of social services in an increasing
extent must await the time when the economy grows at a faster rate. Finally the
socialistic philosophy of the Government to play a large and positive role in the direction
of economic affairs and in the ownership and operation of the productive activities should
be reoriented in a pragmatic fashion.
