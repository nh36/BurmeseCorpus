INTRODUCTION
Dermogenvs pusillus Kuhl and van Hasselt which forms the
subject of the present paper attracted my attention in the year 1949
when I was co-opted to identify the fish content of the collections made
by Dr. J. L. Brooks, visiting professor in Zoology, University of
Rangoon, in connection with his study of the fresh-water biology of the
Inya Lake. The collections and observations were made since October
1949 but systematic study started only from June 1950.
Although Dr. Erna Mohr (1936) made a survey of the family
Hemirhamphidae, she described just a few of the embryonic stages of
D. Pusillus. Smith (1945) pointed about its viviparous nature.
As no
complete study of the development of this fish was available, I under-
took the problem. Incidentally, I hope that the present work will
throw more light on the comparative embryology of the fresh-water
oviparous and viviparous fishes since practically very little work has
been done in this field.
MATERIAL AND METHOD
The fish were collected from October 1949 to February 1952. Fish
from many lakes around Rangoon were collected and observed however
the main and regular collections were made from Inya Lake. For
catching the adults a drag net 4' by 10' with a sein of about 0.2" and
for the fry a dip net with a fine mesh were used. At every catch,
which was made once or twice a week at different times of the day
and at different parts of lake, some fish were observed and fixed on the
spot with Bouin's fluid and 4% formalin while the rest were kept in the

HABIT AND HABITAT
Dermogenys pusillus, a small fish belonging to the family
Hemirhamphidae is found in Rangoon in the fresh water lakes
which include Inya, Kandawgyi and those of the Cantonment Garden.
It has also been observed in the pools and puddles which get connected
with perennial sources of water supply during the rains and which
become dry in the summer. It can be easily observed near the edges of
the lakes where the water is sufficiently clear and grasses and weeds
grow sparingly and it's especially abundant in shaded areas which are
overhung by trees. Being gregarious in habit it is found in groups of
varying number and size, usually swimming about actively, and some-
times along with small Cyprinodonts. When disturbed it may jump
further out into the open or dive low, only to come up again as soon as
the danger is past. It is very active and can conveniently be collected
only by a drag net of 4' by 10' with a sein which when stretched is
about 0.2.

Young ones after hatching, unlike the adults, live lower down and
close to grasses and weeds or to the edges. This may be due to the
shelter they get from the plants and the food they obtain in the form
of small algae that grow on the submerged parts of these plants.
The adults are highly carnivorous, living only on terrestrial and
aquatic insects and their larvae. Examination of stomach contents show
that the insects belong to the orders Orthoptera, Hemiptera, Diptera
and Hymenoptera, and sometimes the larvae of Odonata are
found
Observation has shown that D. Pusillus is a surface feeder. A
small insect, which falls from an overhanging tree or a nearby weed
is immediately noticed. The nearest fish approaches slowly butas,
soon as the insect is within reach it suddenly darts at it. It swallows
the insect slightly below the water surface and after placing the
insect in line with its jaws. In captivity they are fed with adults
and larvae of termites, ants and mosquitoes. It has also been noticed
that the larvae especially those of the mosquito are preferred to the
adults. The food which is at the bottom of the aquarium or that which
sinks below the fish is rarely noticed. They seldom dive down to catch
food and even if they do it is in vain. This is due to the position of
the mouth and eyes in relation to the jaws. In the aquarium the young.
ones are sometimes eaten by the mother and other adults.
The young fish after hatching feed on small algae. This fact is
known by examining the stomach contents. At this stage the alimentary
canal is found to be dilated in the posterior region. In slightly larger
ones, which have their lower jaws produced to a length about twice that
of the upper jaws both algae and small insects are found in the
alimentary canals. When they are fully developed the stomach
contents show only the remains of insects, and the posterior region of
the gut is no longer dilated.
D. Pusillus breeds thoughout the year. This has been proved by
the finding of the fry and females bearing embryos of different stages
at all seasons. The development is internal but copulation has not yet
been observed either in the natural habitat or in the aquarium. The

D. Pusillus is not used as human food probably on account of its
very small size. On the other hand it can be fed to the laboratory
animals. Actually it has been so used in the University of Rangoon,
But it is more useful in the sense that it destroys the mosquito larvae.
There is no record of mosquito destroying propensities of these fish
except that by Mukerji (1935) who mentions that D. Burmanicus feeds
on larvae and pupae of insects. But the present study has shown that
D. Pusillus is highly partial to the larvae and pupae of mosquito.
With their ability to breed throughout the year and thus to increase in
large number within a short period and with their voracious feeding
habit, it will be quite advantageous to rear them in places where
mosquitoes abound and malaria prevails.
The males of D. Pusillus are used in the national sports of
Thialand and with regard to their combative nature an interesting
account has been given by Smith (1945). According to him these fish
are used in sports and games where two males are made to combat
against each other. They are especially cultivated for this purpose, as a
fish in captivity is more pugnacious.
DEVELOPMENT
The mature egg of D. Pusillus measures from 1.05 mm. to
1.31 mm. in diameter. It is round and slightly yellowish. Like the
eggs of most Teleost fishes it has a large proportion of yolk and
segmentation is discoidal. Inspite of my best efforts no earlier
segmenting stage than that of many cells has yet been obtained from
the dissected females which are collected from the lakes or reared in
the aquarium. (Fig. 1).
Stage 1. It is the earliest segmenting stage collected. The
blastodisc appears under the microscope as a whitish, opaque convex
and slightly raised disc. The blastodisc measures about 0.4 mm. in
diameter. The segmentation furrows form a network which divides the
surface layer into 32 cells which are small and approximately equal.

The egg mass at this stage is distinguishable into three layers, namely,
the blastoderm at the animal pole, the subgerminal layer of yolk
globules and the yolk proper which indicates the vegetal pole. (Fig. 2).
Stage 2. The blastodisc enlarges and grows over the yolk mass till
its marginal ring passes slightly beyond the equator of the egg. The
encroachment of blastoderm proceeds no further and so epiboly is
incomplete. The large oil globules like those of the subgerminal layer
are visible at this stage in the yolk mass of the vegetal pole. (Fig. 3).
Stage 3. On one side of the blastodisc appears an embryonic
shield. This indicates the process of gastrulation and marks off the
future dorsal side of the embryo. The embryonic shield has its base
turned towards the germ-ring with which it is coincident. The
embryonic shield enlarges and a lineal streak is formed in its middle
beginning from the side of the germ-ring. (Fig. 4). This primitive
streak becomes elongated and along with it the embryonic shield also
becomes elongated but narrower. The streak at this stage measures
about 0.5 mm. in length. (Fig. 5). The anterior end of the embryo
swells slightly while its posterior end tapers behind. Thus the embryo
assumes a spathulate shape, and the primitive brain and the spinal cord
differentiate. The notochord and the segmental markings of the
mesoderm are not visible as yet.
Stage 4. The embryo becomes longer and curved over the egg
mass. Its length equals approximately half that of the circumference
of the egg. The head region rises above the yolk and thus the
egg-membrane around it becomes clearly visible. The brain is already
differentiated into the three regions: the fore-brain, mid-brain and
hind-brain. (Fig. 6). The fore-brain is bent slightly downward and
on either side of it is seen the optic cup with a rounded lens in the
process of formation. The mid-brain is well developed, expanding
laterally forming two large and rounded sacs. It is marked off from
the hind-brain prominently by deep lateral constrictions. On either
side of the hind-brain appears one slight swelling which is the rudiment
of the auditory organ or the otocyst. The spinal cord and its centra
canal are clearly seen except at the posterior end where the spinal cord
is flat and merges with peripheral yolk. The yolk around the tail end
shows a large number of yolk globules. The notochord becomes
clearly visible at this stage for the first time as a cylindrical rod of

DISCUSSION.
Dermogenys pusillus Kuhl and van Hasselt of the family Hemir-
hamphidae is found in ponds, lakes and slow flowing fresh water
streams.

DERMOGENYS PUSILLUS
17
The maximum length of the fish according to Smith (1945) is
70 mm., and Mohr (1936) says that the maximum length of the male
and female is 40 mm. and 72 mm. respectively. I have examined about
a thousand specimens or more and found that the maximum length for
the male is 47 mm. and that for the female 79 mm.
Sexual dimorphism in this fish is quite pronounced.
The male has
a tubular and soft intromittant organ lying close to the anal fin. On
several occasions I have examined the fluid that oozes out from it and
found sperms, with round heads and long tails. Mohr has mentioned
that the second anal ray is the longest whereas I have found the third
ray to be the longest. Since the opening of the intromittant organ lies
just before the anal fin it is widely separated from the anus.
This may
be taken as a point to distinguish the male and female fry because in
the adult female the urinogenital pore is immediately behind the anus
and separated from the latter by a septum. The brick red pigments
found in the anal or dorsal or both fins in the male are not mentioned
by Mohr or Smith. The significance of which is not fully known.
The adults and the fry with the unequal jaws are carnivorous
but the young fry with approximately equal jaws are herbivorous.
Since the adults have special liking for mosquito arvae this fish may be
reared with advantage for antimalarial purposes and can be added to
the list of larvicidal fishes of Burma.
The small fry from one to five days old are eaten by their mother
and other adults kept in the aquarium. Whether it happens in the
natural habitat is not confirmed.
As the young fry and female with embryos of various stages of
development can be collected at any time of the year it indicates that
there is no special spawning season. This fish is very suitable for the
study of embryology as it can be conveniently collected in any quantity
and reared in the aquarium.
Copulation has not been observed in the aquaria or in nature.
On one occasion I have observed for about thirty minutes an unsuccess-
ful attempt at it. A male after driving off other males, was found to
follow a female from behind, diving below her and keeping in position
with it jaws moving vigorously in sexual excitement but the female
jumped and avoided him every time he tried. However, the pronounced

sexual dimorphism, the presence of an intromittant organ and internal
development clearly indicate that copulation does take place.
Mention has been made by Mohr that there are fewer number of
males than females in the materials she has examined but I have found
in every catch of several hundred fish collected from different localities
that the population ratio of males and females is 2: 1,
Smith states that a female under certain conditions can produce a
limited number of young without having been associated with a male.
This fact is not confirmed by me though a few females have been left
in an aquaria for nearly three months. On the other hand I am inclined
to think that parthenogenesis is impossible in nature due to the over-
whelming population of the males.
It is stated by the same author that the males are pugnacious.. I
have seen the males chasing one another but never serious enough to
come to a stace where they wrestle or the opponent is made disabled.
Females are more hardy in captivity than the males.
The testes are yellow, cylindrical, unusually large and lie dorsally
on either side of the gut, extending almost the entire length of the
coelom. All the larger males dissected and examined are found to
contain ripe sperms.
The ovaries are large, long, tubular and lie in the same position as
the testes. It is hard to name these structures because the embryos are
developed in the same place where the eggs are formed. This is a very
peculiar phenomenon, unlike the other vertebrates, where the embryos
do not develop inside the ovary but in the uterus.
Eggs are developed in the follicles (egg-case) attached in a row to
the lateral sides of the ovisac as there is no clear line of demarcation
between the ovary and the oviduct. The persistence of the follicles or
the egg-cases till birth is another pecularity of the fish. The follicle
keeps pace with the growing embryo and is richly supplied with blood
vessels. It is not known whether there is any nutritive connection
between the embryo and the mother. However, no yolk placenta is
found and the large quantity of yolk present in the egg is not completely
absorbed even after the birth of the fry.

A very interesting abnormality is noticed, where the embryos are
found surrounded on both sides by the yolk, in the posterior region of
the ovisaç. No further development was traced.
While embryos are developing a good number of small eggs are
observed in between the embryos attached to the wall of ovisac.
Mohr has mentioned that the way the embryos are coiled may
bear some relation to the side of the ovisac. No such regularity is
found. The embryo in the same ovisac bends clock-wise or anticlock-
wise.
It is observed that the pelvic fins appear very late in the embryo
and differentiate into the form of the adult when the fry attains a
length of 11-14 mm.
The lower jaw of the fry at birth is slightly longer than the upper
jaw but gradually lengthens and is nearly three times that of the upper
jaw on reaching maturity.
Practically all the fish are parasitised by a fluke. Sometimes a
large number of the parasites are found but the host fish look
apparently healthy. It will be very interesting and probably quite easy
to study the life history of this parasite since the material is available
any desired quantity at all seasons.
