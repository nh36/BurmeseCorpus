JOURNAL OF BURMA RESEARCH SOCIETY*
The first number of the Journal of the Burma Research Society has just been
issued from the American Baptist Mission Press, where copies can be obtained at Rs. 2-8
each. From time to time various letters from correspondents have appeared in our
columns, somewhat angrily demanding to know what the Society was doing and when
their journal was to appear. Seeing that the inaugural meeting was held on March 29,
1910, the appearance of the journal now is certainly somewhat belated. There are always
delays, of course, in the starting of any new work; but fifteen months is a very long delay,
and it is all the more inexplicable as the accounts show that the most common impedi-
ment in new enterprises, namely, the financial one, has not operated in this case. By the
end of May 1910 nearly two thousand rupees had come in from subscribers, and by
January 1911, the last month for which the accounts are given, the balance in hand was
over three thousand rupees. It is a happy augury for the future success of the Society
that money should be subscribed so freely, and we trust in future it will be possible to
publish the journal more promptly and regularly. There seems to be no difficulty in
obtaining suitable matter as we understand that the second number is ready for the press
now, and a fair amount of matter is already in hand for the third number.
publication of a journal of this kind should not take more than a month or six weeks,
and even then it should be possible to produce it without the number of misprints which
are a blemish on this first number of the journal. Such mistakes are to some extent
inevitable and excusable when the printing has to be rushed through in a few hours; but
when weeks are available, they should be very few indeed. Allowance must be made,
however, for the first number, and we now turn to the more congenial task of commenting
on the very interesting matter contained in the journal.
The mere
After an account of the inaugural meeting and of the first general meeting, comes
a suggestive article by Mr. J. S. Furnivall on Matriarchal Vestiges in Burma. Interesting
as the article is we do not think Mr. Furnivall quite makes out his case. He says that
the object of his paper is to show that Burman society was organised on a matriarchal
basis in comparatively recent times. He does not profess, however, to do anything
beyond preliminary spadework in this enquiry. His first argument is from the Khasis of
Assam who retain the matriarchal system to this day. On this point we entirely agree
with Dr. Ross, who contributes a critical note on Mr. Furnivall's article, and who says:-
"Now mere remote ethnological affinities between the Burman and the Khasis will
not enable us to infer even the possibility of the social institutions of this hill-tribe today
being similar to a by-gone polity of the former people".
We would add that hill-tribes are apt to progress backwards in civilisation and that
it is rather unfair to their more civilised relations to argue that in comparatively recent

times they must have done all that their savage relations do now. The argument Mr.
Furnivall adduces from the custom of the Burmese kings marrying their half-sisters is
even less convincing as a trace of matriarchal society. The mere fact of the son who had
not this double claim to royal lineage having an equal chance of securing the throne with
those sons who had it, shows that this custom has nothing to do with the idea of
inheritance through the mother only. In practice, there was generally a scramble for the
throne among the brothers as well as the sons of the king who had just died, and the only
claim that really counted in the contest was that of royal blood through the father. The
rank of the mother counted for very little indeed. It is quite easy to see how the custom
might have arisen without any connection with ideas going back to primitive matriarchal
institutions It was not uncommon for the kings of Burma to have families of a hundred
children or thereabouts. There could have been very little of the genuine brother and
sister feeling among the children in that case. No Church had been fulminating against
the marriage of near kin, and the scientific objections to it were wholly unknown.
Marriage was purely a civil contract, and when so many sons and daughters had to be
provided for out of the public revenues, it would evidently ease the financial strain when
two of them married each other instead of bringing in additional mouths in the shape of
husbands and wives from outside.
The other articles are one on The Early Use of the Buddhist Era in Burma by
Mr. Taw Sein Ko; one on Human Sacrifices near the Upper Chindwin by Mr. G. E. R.
Grant-Brown; Elementary Studies in Lahoo, Akha (Kaw), and Wa Language by the
Rev. C. B. Antisdel, who also contributes an interesting account of The Lahoo Narrative of
the Creation; Some Anthropometric Data of the Talaings by Mr. B. Houghton; Karen
Folk-lore by the Rev. D. Gilmore; The Kings of Burma by Mr. Grant-Brown; and one on
Missionary Burmese by Maung Tin. There are also various shorter contributions and
finally a number of reviews of recent works connected with Burma which are by no means
the least interesting part of the journal. These are followed by sympathetic obituary
notices of the late Mr. Todd-Naylor and of General de Beylie. It is impossible to do
more than enumerate all these, but every one will find something to interest him in the
journal' and if subsequent numbers are equally good they should do much to stimulate
interest in the work of the Society. We should be glad to know whether anything is
being done in the way of collecting books and manuscripts which, according to the
published rules, is one of the things the Society undertakes to do. Even in English many
works have been published about Burma, some of which it is now almost impossible
to get.
The Society might well collect a complete library of these. But what is much more
important, we think, is that they should collect any vernacular manuscripts which may
throw any light on the past history of Burma. The Talaing ones more especially are in
danger of being lost altogether as few in the present day can read Talaing. No doubt, in
the libraries of the monasteries all over Burma there are some manuscripts which would
throw light on the past, though the bulk of them may be mere religious disquisitions of
no historical value whatever. To try to rescue the valuable ones from oblivion, probably
from early destruction, would be one of the most useful works the Society could under-
take, though, we admit, it is one of great difficulty.
