The present translation is the second installment of the translation of such portions as
have reference to the relations, mostly warlike between Siam and Burma as recorded in P'ra Raj
P’ônsawadan Chabab, P'ra Raj Hat alok'a or the "Royal Autograph Edition of the Siamese
Chronicles". The First installment was published in Vol. X, Part II of the Journal of the Burma
Research Society in August 1935. This Installment brings the translation to the end of the first
volume of the Siamese Chronicles. There are two more volumes to complete it The second volume
ends with the fall of Ayut'ia before the Burmese attacks in the reign of Sinbyushin of the
Alaungpaya dynasty.
Though I cannot make a definite promise that I will complete the translations, it is my
intention to continue it as time permits. It is not an easy work for me, as both English and Siamese
are, to me, foreign languages. The difficulties in English can be overcome by reference to the
dictionaries and works of reference on the English language; but the difficulties in Siamese are
very great, especially as the language used is somewhat archaic, and the expressions used with the
reference to anything appertaining to the King and royalty and royal ceremonies are quite different
from those having reference to common people. In Burma I have no one to whom I can appeal for
explanation; and any difficulty in the language which I find necessary to solve has to be referred to
my friend Phya Sombathi Parihar, a retired official of His Siamese Majesty's Privy Purse
Department in Bangkok; and this takes about three or four weeks Hence the delay in the work, I
am under great obligations to him and I tender him my sincere thanks.
I am indebted to Mr G.H Luce, Reader in Far Eastern History to Rangoon University, for
the revision of my English to make it worthy of publication in a learned journal, and I am very
thankful to him for his kindness in thus helping me.

The Siamese were thoroughly worn out and crippled by the capture of Ayut'ia by
the King of Burma after a protracted siege, and also by the taking away of a large number
of the population as prisoners of war to Hanthawaddy. But they had a wonderful power of
recuperation and revival; and after the lapse of about eight years they were able to think
of throwing off the heavy yoke of Burmese suzerainty. They were able the more easily to
re-assert their independence by the death of one of the ablest and greatest King's of
Burma, Hanthawaddy Sinbyushin better known as Bayin Naung Kyawdin Nawrat'a and
by the succession to the throne of a son who compared with his father, was a mere
weakling.
After the departure of His Majesty of Burma, the new King of Siam made the
usual promotions in rank and title of those who had rendered good service and were
worthy of such recognition; and important official posts were offered to those in whose

Siam's eastern neighbour, Camboja, though greatly inferior to Siam in all
respects, in the ability of her rulers and the fighting capacity of her people, invariably
took the opportunity whenever Siam, under stress either of invasion from Burma or of
internal strife which occasionally happened when incompetent and inefficient Kings
succeeded able and powerful ones, was temporarily weak and incapacitated.
During the temporary crippled condition of Siam and while she was recuperating,
Camboja made several incursions into Siam, never coming to a pitched battle but always
capturing and taking away a large number of the inhabitants from towns and villages
lying between the Cambojan frontier and the capital of Siam. It is not the purpose of this
work to translate the accounts of these predatory expeditions of Camboja, and therefore
only passing mention will be made where it is thought advisable to do so.
Only a year after the new King ascended the throne, the King of Camboja
invaded Siam with a force of 30,000 men. When report of the invasion sent by the
Governor of Nak'on Nayok' was received at the capital, the ministers and nobles thought
it advisable to ask their King to leave Ayut'ia and to go and stay in P'itsanulok², giving
as their reason that they had not enough men to defend the city, that the King of
Hanthawaddy had taken away the greater portion of the big and small guns, leaving
behind only a small number insufficient for the defense of the city, and that they had not
yet manufactured sufficient gun-powder even for the small number of guns. The King
agreed to go and orders were issued to get the royal barges ready for the journey. When
everything was ready the King asked K’un T’ep Orajun³, the official in charge of the
royal barges, what he personally thought of the intended journey to P’itsanulok. K'un
T'ep Orajun told the King that the army of invasion was not very great and he wished His
Majesty to remain in the capital and watch the energy and fighting power of the enemy.
Should the King be too hasty in getting away from the enemy, the King of Hanthawaddy
might blame his precipitate departure from the capital. The King saw there was much
sense in what this officer said and he deferred his departure, and in the end there was
no necessity to go away, because although the Cambojans attempted to storm the city

In the year 926 (A.D 1565) the King of Hanthawaddy, i.e. King Bayin Naung
Kyawdin Nawrata, became very ill and died on Saturday the 9th waxing moon of the
second Siamese month (January). He was then sixty-five years of age and had reigned for
thirty-five years. His son by the name of Mang Erng" then ascended the throne. He is the
Nanda Bayin of the Hmannan History; but as the Siamese chronicler continues to call
him the King of Hanthawaddy History; the same appellation will be adhered to in the
translation. The new King appointed his son Mang Sam Kliet' as Maha Uparaja.
Not long after the accession of the new King, Muang Room Muang K'anf¹º tried
to be independent by disobeying orders from the King. The two Muangs (towns) are
mentioned without the connective "and" throughout the subsequent context. Taking this
fact in conjunction with what is narrated about the attack on them, it is very probable that
they are names of one town only. News of this state of affairs reached Phra Naresuan at
P'itsanulok, and so he went down to Ayut'ia and asked his father to be allowed to go and
help Hanthawaddy to subdue the rebellious town, and at the same time to study the state
of affairs in the Burmese capital. His father readily gave him permission to go. Phra
Naresuan went back to P’itsanulok, got together a force of 100,000 men, 800 elephants
and 1500 horses, and started for Hanthawaddy on an auspicious day. He halted about
three days' journey from the capital and sent information of his coming. The King of
Hanthawaddy was very glad of the arrival of the auxiliary force from Ayut'ia and he sent
a messenger with presents of delicious food for the prince and invited him to come in. At
the audience the King told Phra Naresuan about the rebellious conduct of Muang Room
Muang K'ang. The prince told the King that he had heard the news while he was still in
his town, and so had came to help his suzerian. The King ordered Phra Naresuan, the
Maha Uparaja and Prince Sank'at'at¹ to go up and subdue the town. The three princes
started on their errand, and on arrival at their destination they encamped most likely in a
circle, because they built a pavilion in the centre for mutual consultation.

The King then planned measures to do away with Phra Naresuan. He sent a letter
to the Siamese Prince requesting him to come and help him in subduing the King of Ava
who was trying to break away from Hanthawaddy and become independent. Phra
Naresuan thought that the letter meant what it purported to be, and had no suspicion of
any ulterior design underlying it. He went down to the capital to ask his father's
permission and to take leave of him. His father gave his permission readily. He returned
to P'ítsanulok, mobilized a force of 100,000 men, 1500 horses and 800 elephants, and left
for Hanthawaddy on Sunday the 6th waning moon of the 3rd Siamese month (February)
927 (A.D. 1566). He reached Muang Kamp’engp'et in two days; and staying there for
three days resumed his journey to Hanthawaddy by way of Jieng T'ong Kum Tamaw.
At that time the Shan families resident in the jurisdiction of Muang
Kamp'engp'et, probably owing to the exaction and oppression of the Burmese officers
stationed there, left their hearths and homes to go and stay elsewhere, headed by a few
influential leaders. The Burmese and Mon soldiers followed them and tried to bring them
back, but in the fight that ensued the pursuers were worsted, and the Shans continued
their journey in the direction of P'ítsanulok. Phra Naresuan came to know of this, and so
he sent a mounted messenger post-haste to Luang Kosa who was left in charge of
P'ítsanulok, to send orders to all frontier outposts to prevent the Shans from leaving the
jurisdiction of the province of P’ítsanulok. Luang Kosa carried out the prince's orders,
and all the Shans entered P'ítsanulok and were properly looked after by the authorities.
Nandasu and Rajsank'ran sent a letter to P'ítsanulok demanding the surrender of the
Shans. A strong letter refusing to surrender them was sent back.

glad, thinking that things were turning out just as he had planned. He sent a force of
10,000 men to lie in ambush on the line of march of the Prince, and distant about a day's
journey from the capital. Then he ordered Phya Kien and Phya P'raram to go and meet
the Prince, with instructions that when the Prince had come past the ambush, they should
bring the forces out from the ambush and attack him in the rear, while he himself would
come out and attack him in front. They were to try and capture Phra Naresuan and put
him to death. By the death of the Prince, Hanthawaddy would gain the supreme position
in all the surrounding countries and towns. The two officers left Hanthawaddy, and on
reaching Muang K'reng went and saw the Prince and told him simply that they were sent
by His Majesty of Hanthawaddy to come and meet him. They then went and saw Maha
Thera K'an Chong who had been their spiritual teacher, and divulged the whole of His
Burman Majesty's plot to cause the death of Phra Naresuan. Maha Thera K'an Chong
pitied the Prince who had done no wrong, and so at nightfall he took the two officers to
the Prince and told him that his two disciples had told him that His Majesty of
Hanthawaddy was plotting to kill him.
The Prince questioned the two officers and they divulged the whole plot to him.
He was very sorry that the King of Hanthawaddy should prove to himself so faithless,
and he made up his mind to avenge this attempt on his life. He thanked the priest most
sincerely for his kindness in saving his life and offered to take him and the two officers to
Ayut'ia together with all their disciples and relations, and see that they were well
provided for in Siam, because His Majesty of Hanthawaddy was sure, he said, to wreak
his vengeance on them for frustrating his plans. They all accepted the offer. He then told
his nobles and officers that he had done no wrong and yet the King of Hanthawaddy had
plotted to take his life, and by this act of the King the tie of friendship between Ayut'ia
and Hanthawaddy had been severed, and suffering and misery would be fall in
consequence the population, both laymen and monks.

REIGN OF SOMDET PHRA SANP'ET II (Phra Naresuan).
On the death of his father, Phra Naresuan ascended the throne, and appointed his
brother as Maha Uparat. He was then thirty-five years of age.
Siam's eastern neighbour Camboja had always been a source of annoyance to
Siam, because the successive kings of Camboja never appeared to be able to keep faith
and observe treaty obligations, however solemnly undertaken. Whenever Siam found
herself in difficulties due to invasion from Burma, and the Siamese armies were busily
engaged in repelling the Burmese, Camboja invariably took advantage of the situation to
make a predatory incursion by land into the eastern provinces of Siam and carry off large
number of the population as captives. Sometimes when she was more adventurous she
ventured to go out by sea and attack the southern provinces of Siam. She had never been
able to fight Siam successfully, and she was repeatedly brought into subjection, but
always threw it off on the first opportunity available, when Siamese armies could not be
spared to attack and subdue her. The new King recounted the several instances of the
breach of faith of the kings of Camboja during the reigns of his father and grandfather,
and said that now he had ascended the throne and the kingdom was his, he would teach

REIGN OF SOMDET PHRA BORMOMRAJA" I or Song T'am'
96
Phra P'imum T'am, the usurper, ascended the throne of Ayut'ia and assumed the
title of King Song T'am. He appointed Chamun Sri Sararak as his Uparat, but the new
Uparat enjoyed his exalted position only seven days, as he sickened and died seven days
after his appointment.
About this time many Japanese trading ships came to Ayut'ia to trade. The
Japanese traders were very angry on account of the unjust treatment received from the
responsible ministers. They conspired together, and with the assistance of a priest called
P’ra P’imun intended to kill the King. They got together about five hundred men and
entered the grounds of the palace, and waited to seize the King when he came out to a
royal pavilion to listen to the priests giving religious lessons. Eight priests from Wat
Pradu came and led the King into the presence of the assembled Japanese. There was an
uproar among the conspirators, each inciting the other to take the initiative but none
daring to execute the intended plan, and they were quarrelling among themselves.

REIGN OF SOMDET PHRA AT ITWONG 104
All the ministers, nobles and counselors brought the paraphernalia of royalty and
other appendages and ornaments of a sovereign and offered them to Chow Phya Kalahom
Suriyawong, but he did not accept them, saying that what he had done was not with the
object of seizing the throne but on account of the necessity to save his life; there was Phra
At'itwong, the son of the King, still alive, and only when they raised him to the throne to
succeed his father would they be doing what was right and proper. After due
consideration the ministers raised Phra At'itwong to the throne. He was then a mere boy
of nine years, and entirely ignorant of his responsibilities and duties as a King. He was
always catching goats and sheep and playing with them and his attendants had to take his
clothes and food wherever he was playing. This went on about six months, and then the
ministers and nobles, after deliberation, came to the conclusion that the country would
suffer if they were to continue keeping the young boy as King; they therefore decided to
depose him and offer the crown to Chow Phya Kalahom Suriyawong. Accordingly, they
took the paraphernalia of royalty and other utensils of a sovereign to him, and begged
him to ascend the throne of Ayut'ia and rule over the people of the country, so that they
might have peace, prosperity and happiness.
