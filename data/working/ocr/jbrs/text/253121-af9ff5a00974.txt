THE KINWUN MINGYI AT HOME.
1. HIS PARENTS.
This article only deals with the Mingyi's life in the Pagyi and Alon
Wunships, now comprised in the Lower Chindwin district. His public services
at the capital are too well known to need recapitulation. My facts are
derived from his relatives and other residents at Mindaingbin and Alon. I
have also consulted the copy of his lecture to the Dedaye Social Club on 12th
January 1933, which was kindly supplied to me by Mr W. L. Barretto, then
Deputy Commissioner, Pyapon. He obtained some of his information from
the Hteingyun-kyaung Sayadaw at Kyaiklat, and from the late U Po Lu of
Dedaye, who had lived three months in the Mingyi's house.
The territory of Pagyi, which is now approximately the Palè and
Salingyi townships, supplied the bulk of the soldiers in the six regiments of
the Apyin Chauksu, the Outer Brigade. Maung Hmo of Mindaingbin, west
of Pale, served in the Natshinywe regiment and rose to be a sergeant (thwe-
thauk-gyi). He retired to his native village and became a dat-saya, treating
patients by dieting. This is the local tradition, and it explains the difference
of opinion between Mr Barretto, who said that U Hmo was a soldier, and a
correspondent from Kyaiklat who wrote after the lecture that he was a doctor
and that his authority was the late Mr Taw Sein Ko, who had been a tabè
of the Mingyi. But the latter's article in "Burmese Sketches" describes him
as a soldier.
U Hmo's wife was Ma Hnin Zi, locally called Ma Min Zi, and they had
seven children. U Hmo died in Mandalay in King Mindon's reign on a visit
to his distinguished son, who thenceforth sent his mother an allowance of a
hundred rupees a month. He also gave her about three hundred rupees
towards erecting a pagoda and monastery. The latter no longer stands, but
the pagoda is the smaller of the two in the large monastery compound bet-
ween the village and the dak bungalow : it has no distinctive features. Ma
Hnin Zi's slender brick tomb has an inscription composed by the Kinwun
Mindaingbin is eight miles west of Palè and not twenty miles north-west of
Alon, as stated by Mr Barretto.

U Hmo's third child, Maung Chaung, became a sitké-gyi and commanded
all the troops in Pagyi. He was given a gold bayet by King Mindon and
died in the succeeding reign. The fourth was Ma Bu, who had two daughters,
both of whom married U Kywa, Pagyi Taik saye at Salingyi, whose tomb
can be seen at Mindaingbin. The elder daughter Ma Saw had a son, who
was born in 1865. This was U Bo Byu, who became a District surveyor, and
then Township Officer of Western Pagyi with headquarters at Mindaingbin.
He reverted to being an Inspector of Land Records and retired from Govern-
ment service. He did well as a recruiting officer during the great war, and
in 1920 received a certificate of honour and a gold watch for his services.
He became an advocate, and was member of the Legislative Council for
Lower Chindwin West, and died about ten years ago. He and his wife, Ma
Ma or Ma Myit, share a tomb in the family burial-ground. She, and their
sons, built a rock-temple in the famous Powundaung range, north-west of
Salingyi, in about 1920.
The next son of U Hmo was U Shwe, who died at Mandalay in his
youth while attached to the Kinwun. Yet another, U Hta, became Kindat
Sitkè-gyi and died at Kindat; his two sons are living at Alon. The last of U
Hmo's family, Ma Htu, has left the most desendants. She and her husband
have tombs at Mindaingbin. Their eldest son, U Po Tha, was Kyauk-gyaung
Sayegyi, that is Chief Clerk of the Household Brigade, and worked in Manda-
lay, where he married Ma Pwa Gyi. He remained there after the Annexation.
The second son, U Tha Mo, was born in 1853, and was an arbitrator Judge
(Kon) in Burmese times. On one or two occasions he visited his uncle in
Mandalay. He is still living in Mindaingbin, the Mingyi's last surviving
nephew, and I am indebted to him for much of my information.
Ma Htu's third son was U Po O (2), T. Ď. M. He was headman of
Mindaingbin and Yazawut Oke of twenty-four villages, and became Myook of
Western Pagyi with headquarters at Mindaingbin in August 1888. He served
with distinction in the operations against dacoits during the Annexation
troubles, his greatest exploit being that in which he hunted down and killed
the notorious Bo Saga early in 1889. His tomb and that of his wife, Ma
Thaung, are to be seen at Mindaingbin. He died in 1903. His son, U Ko Ko
Gyi, was born in about 1885, was a clerk in the District office in Monywa,
twice M. L. C. for Lower Chindwin East, and President of the Municipal
Committee. He was coopted to the District School Board in 1933. He is a
pleader of the second grade and lives in Monywa.
Another son of Ma Htu's was U Ye Gyan, who served in his uncle's
household in Mandalay, where he met and married Ma Kin Le. She died in
about 1918, and has a tomb next to her husband's. He, too, was headman of
Mindaingbin, but was appointed Township Officer of Maingkaing in the
Upper Chindwin, He reverted to the headmanship in 1888, and died in 1920.
His elder son, U Htin, was born in 1884. He was a petition-writer at Palè,
which succeded Mindaingbin as the Township headquarters, from 1905 to
1920, in which ar he possessed property valued at Rs 2,000. He was then
elected heaún.an in succession to U Ye Gyan, and still holds that appointment.
Another son was U Shwe Wun, who was born in 1887. He was Chairman

When his parents died, the Kinwun Mingyi renounced his share of the

however, no documents or other ancestral property relevant to the present
article, the last of such being lost in a fire at Mindaingbin in 1920. He gave
each brother and sister a copy of his "Thonzè-hna-saung Dwè."
3. HIS EARLY YEARS.
Maung Chin was born on Sunday, the twelfth waxing of Tabodwè 1183,
which is the third of February 1822. Taw Sein Ko says it was in 1821, and
wrongly calls the village Madaingbin. No portents occurred at his birth, and
he had no abnormalities except that the ring-finger of his right hand was not
quite straight, curving slightly inward. There is a belief that anyone born on
a Tanin-ganwe-ne is somehow crooked, and many Burmans believed that the
kingdom of Thibaw was betrayed by the Kinwun Mingyi to the British. He,
however, though admitting his crooked finger, would not admit that he was
in any other way a 'crook.'
Maung Chin lived with his parents, U Hmo and Ma Hnin Zi, in their
house in Mindaingbin, till he was ten, when he became a koyin. The Shinbyu
ceremony was held at a monastery in Ngapyawdaw South village close to
Sinshin, which was the chief village of the Myo wherein Mindaingbin lay.
The presiding monk had been U Khin Gyi San, but I could not ascertain his
monastic name. A monastery of later date stands on the site. The boy was a
keen and promising student. As a member of a daing in the athi nè of Pagyi,
he was liable for military service in his father's regiment, but this he avoided
by becoming an upazin, and he was ordained in the same monastery at the
age of twenty. His monastic named is stated in "Burmese Sketches" and by
Mr Barretto to have been U Alawka, though from his having been born on a
Sunday this has been disputed. He went to Amarapura in 1842, almost
immediately after his ordination, and a few years later became a layman.
His career in the wider world of the capital lies outside the scope of this
article.

4. BRIEF RETURN TO THE CHINDWIN.
He never re-visited his birth-place, and Alon on the east bank of the
Chindwin was the nearest he got to it. He was appointed Alon wun in 1862.
He had married the Singyan Thakimma or Minthami or Myosa, who is
variously described as a queen of the deposed Pagan Min and as a maid-of-
honour but she did not accompany him to Alon. Taw Sein Ko says she
was a queen. The Alon Nè was an important charge, in which Bandula
had been one of his predecessors. The six regiments of the Inner Brigade
(Atwin Chauk su) were recruited from it, and numerous famous soldiers
and civilians were natives thereof. It was bounded by Tabayin on the
north and east, by the river on the west, by Amyin on the south, and

by Kani on the north-west. He received a monthly salary of Rs 200 to
300. His decisions in civil suits could be upset only by the Hlutdaw, to
which lay also the only appeal against his death sentences. His house
was 45 cubits by 45, and was made of teak, and stood in the Myodwin
quarter of Alon. It was demolished in 1905. His court was close by, a
teak building, 30 by 30 by 20 in height, similar in construction to the
present Nandaw of the Bodawgyi Nat. It was the Township Court after the
Annexation, till 1908 when the headquarters were transferred to Monywa
and the court was taken down and auctioned. The site belongs to U Kyaw.
The only remnant is the small shed, where oaths were administered. Parts
of the walls that encircled Alon survive in the vicinity. His rule was
distinguished by an exceptional degree of peace and prosperity. He took
into Government service many young men of good character and education,
and he found time to continue the literary efforts which had brought him to
fame at the capital.
He married Ma Shwe Mè, or Ma Kin Mè, sister of the Myothugyi of
Alon but next year he was re-called to Mandalay. She and their adopted
sons remained in Alon, but the Singyan Thakinma died soon after, and they
Joined him at Mandalay.

U Kya U was headman of Alon till 1886, when as U Maung Gyi he

The other adopted son, U Bo, better known as U Maung Gale, was Township

When the Kinwun Mingyi died in 1908, he left all his property to his
second wife and two adopted sons.
A cousin of Ma Shwe Mè was U Sein, headman of Kunohn, who succeded
his brother U San Shun, who rendered valuable services against dacoits after
the Annexation and was killed in 1893 by the famous dacoit Bo Nga Chaing.
U Sein had been born in 1858 and died in 1913, leaving a son U San Pu,
headman till 1919. The latter is of interest as the husband of the daughter of
Maha Bandula's youngest son, U Hkyon, thus linking up two famous
Chindwin worthies.
5. BUILDINGS AT MINDAINGBIN AND ALON.
In the Kinwun's native village are a pagoda, a kyaung, a zayat, and a
row of tombs, that recall him and his family to memory. The pagoda was
built by his mother, while the adjoining monastery stands on the site of the
one built by her, and is itself the work of his nephew U Po O, as is the
neighbouring zayat. The latter has a beautifully carved wooden roof, with
figures of peacocks, galons, nagas, parrots, and nats, on it, the craftsman
being U Yan Gyaw of Zibyubin in Yinmabin Township about thirty years
ago.
On the other side of the village is a piece of land owned by the present
headman, where stand the nine family tombs, in excellent repair. They are,
from north-west to south-east, those of (1) U Kywa, (2) U Ye Gyan, (3) Ma
Kin Le, behind which is (4) Ma Hnin Zi. In the main row again are (5) U
Kan Daw, (6) Ma Tu, (7) U Po O, (8) Ma Thaung, and (9) U Bo Byu and
Ma Myit.
While he was Alon Wun, the Kinwun Mingyi built the Yelèġyun pagoda
and tazaung, on a bluff in the midst of paddy-fields that are liable to flooding
-whence its name. In the tazaung is a large Buddha of cemented brick, but
the buildings are in poor repair. There is a fine view in all directions. To
the west, beyond the fields and the railway are the dark green trees of Alon,
with pagoda spires rising over them, on the south is the steep hill of
Kyaukka, and on the east are the Thapan hills, which shade off into the
Hnaw forest on the horizon.
The notes in this article are not exhaustive, and some of the statements
could not be verified, but it is hoped they might prove useful as an indication
of sources to anyone who engages on that long-overdue work, a complete
biography of the Kinwun Mingyi.
