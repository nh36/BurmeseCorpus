1. HIS ANCESTORS
The present article will not deal with Bandula's achievements as a
general, which are sufficiently well known and which can be studied in U
Thein Maung's 'Life of Bandula' and elsewhere, but only with the years he
spent in the Lower Chindwin and with his family. As U Thein Maung
shows at page 125 of his book, there are several versions as to Bandula's
wives, sons and daughters. The accompanying tables try to combine these
versions. The dates are dates of death. Much of the information was
obtained in Ngapayin village.

Bandula's grandfather U Ya was born at Ngapayin, a village in the
Alon Wunship that had been noted for its service to the Crown since the
days of King Alompra. Like others from the village, U Ya served at the
capital as a page in the palace and eindawpa of Alompra's youngest son
U Waing, the Padu Prince and Alon Myosa, who in 1781 became King
Bodawpaya. His second wife is locally described as a daughter of the
King of Siam. She may have been a lady captured in the Siamese cam-
paign or possibly the daughter of a Shan Sawbwa: but I could not
verify the matter. U Ya's son by his first wife was U Pauk Taw, who was
appointed Tagani Hmu, which is an officer of the corps that guarded the

About fifty men, or one-third of the regiment, were recruited in the Inbaung
villages east of the Chindwin and about fifteen miles north-west of
Ngapayin, and another fifty odd came from around Tabayin, fifteen miles.
to the north-east. U Pauk Taw retired to his native village and farmed
his paddy and sessamum lands there and in Kyogon and Maungdaung
village-tracts. He had a small two-roomed house in the centre of Ngapayin,
near where Bandula's pagoda now stands, and it was there that his eldest son
was born on Wednesday, the first waxing of Tazaungmon 1144 (October
1782). The boy was called Maung Yit, because at the time of his birth

eight of those doors, and on each is a nat with a conical helmet and the

buds) on long curving stems, like umbrellas. Above them are carvings of
animals, on the north-east doors a to-naya (an animal like a lion, with a long
curving tail, fabled to dwell in the Himalayan forests) and a horse, on the
north-west the same, on the south-west two horses, and two naya-byans (a
flying version of the 'to', with a thin head and many crocodile teeth. Its
image was formerly to be found on standard weights) on the south-east.
Interspersed on the panels are leaves and flowers. The rails which form
the parapet are also well carved. In the main hall upstairs are the original
sturdy teak pillars, some of them retaining traces of their red lacquering,
and the top half of the west wall is elaborately panelled and carved with a
diamond motif and the lower in squares. Above the outer pillars is a
sculpted cornice. The whole building is a charming and all too rare
example of old Burmese art, and much of it is possibly unchanged since
Bandula learnt his alphabet there. To the south-east is a small modern
building. The monastery was used as a school till recent years. It has
now only one occupant, U Nemeinda, who welcomes visitors to his historic
home. Father Sangermano noticed that Burmese children lived very
hardily. They were allowed to run wild and wore no clothes till they were
twelve. Thus their limbs were strongly developed and they could endure
great hardships. Maung Yit's companions were other sons of soldiers, and
we may be sure that their talk and their games were of war. Bandula's
brother became a general, and so did at least three other lads from
neighbouring villages, and all fought under Bandula. The entrance to the
school compound is guarded by the wooden figures of two grinning taga-
saungs; they are unarmed but crouch alertly and appear to raise a leg as
though for a kick. These early examples of commissionaires or 'chuckers.

Maung Yit had two brothers and one sister. One brother, Maung
Aye was Amyin Nga-myo Wun and became a general, with the title of Min
Din Min Gaung. He commanded an army corps in Bandula's great attack
on the British positions at Rangoon in December 1824, his sector being
round Hsandawgyo Road (now Signal Pagoda Road), but like the others his
attack was foiled. He retreated with the main body under Bandula to
Donabyu, whence he fled on his brother's death. When he brought the
news to Ava, his head was instantly cut off, as related in Morrow's recent
novel "The Splendour of God," page 227. Bandula's sister Ma Doke was
a member of the household of the Kawlin Mintha, and the other brother,
Maung Myat Ne, was involved in Prince Tharrawaddy's seizure of the
throne in 1837, as was his nephew the second Bandula, these facts being
related at page 15 of U Thein Maung's biography. Their father died in
1795, and the thirteen-year old Maung Yit had to help his mother to
cultivate their fields and look after the younger children. He married at
about the age of twenty, but I have little information about his first two
wives. His third wife Ma Thi La, was born at Lèzin, a few miles east of
Monywa, and her uncle Bandu Sethu was myothugyi of Budalin. Her
grandfather on her mother's side, Nagathaman Gyi, was also myothugyi,
I think, and he built the Moke-kyaung-gyi pagoda in the Minwun quarter of
Budalin, which is still standing in moderately good condition. Bandu Sethu
built the Kyaunggyi-dwin-taik monastery, which is kept in repair by a
descendant named U Ya Baw of Thabyebindaw. His sister Ma Thu Za
married C Shwè of Budalin, and one or other must have had a house also
at Lèzin. Their son; U Yin Maung, was an arbitrator judge (kon) and a
Minister in the reign of Tharrawaddy Min, and Tabayin Myook in the time
of Pagan Min. Their eldest daughter Ma Ngwe U married the headman of
Thekkègyin, which is close to Lèzin, and their son U Swa was headman too;
he continued to hold the appointment after the arrival of the British, till his
death in 1900, and his son U Kan Yon then became headman. U Shwe's
fourth child, Ma Thi Hla, or Ma Thi La, was Bandula's second or third wife,
and they had about three children. She inherited the family property in
Budalin, near the Kanma tank, and Bandula lived there in his few leisure
moments and planted the fruit-trees in the compound and the quarter is
called Minwun after him. Her second husband was U Wa, myothugyi of
Alon, and their daughter married U Tun Yaung, colonel of the Taungdawè
regiment. Most of the lower ranks of this corps were recruited in the
Alon Wunship, and the Bo may have been a local man too. Ma Thi Hla's
peculiarity was her long thin ears, and this became a family trait; her
daughter Ma Toke Toke had them, and so has her great-nephew, U Kya,
a man of 73, whom I saw in 1934. U Kya owned the ancestral property in
Minwun quarter, but has had to sell it to U Kyet Kyi, a descendant of Ma
Toke Toke.

Bandula married Ma Min Bu in about 1802, and U Kyan Gyi, their only
child, was probably born about 1804. Possibly she died soon after, as
there were no other children, and Bandula had several subsequent wives.
U Kyan Gyi is stated to be her son on page 19 of U Thein Maung's
book, but at page 125 it is said that U Po Kya's 'Myamma Gon Ye' makes
him a son of Ma Thi La, as does the monk of the Sangyaung monastery,
who derived his information from Bandula's daughter-in-law Ma Ngwe Ya,
as shown on page 125 of the same work. The pongyi refers to him as U
Gyi, and this is the name by which he is still called locally, rather than U
Kyan Gyi. The pagoda bell at Ngapayin, however, what dates from
August 1825, states that he was the son of Ma Min Bu, and that is our
earliest authority. The bell's inscription was reproduced in the Sun
Magazine of January 1932, and U Gyi's career is discussed in part 2,
Number 35, of the same. A picture of him faces page 24 of U Thein
Maung's biography. The artist Colesworthy Grant made a portrait of him
in Rangoon in 1855 before going on the mission to Amarapura.
His father's fame secured him rapid promotion, though he inherited
none of his father's genius. He helped Prince Tharrawaddy to the throne
in 1837. He was Tabayin Myowun, as his father had been, at the begin-
ning of the reign of Pagan Min, and bad his headquarters in that town,
and was Thonze Myosa and Maha Bandula, his official title being Thado
Mingyi Minhla Mingaung Yaza. He was put in command of the Burmese
Army in the middle stages of the second Anglo-Burmese War, and his
feeble efforts are described at page 368 et seq of the British Burma
Gazetteer volume I. He was commanded to return to Amarapura dressed
in woman's clothes, but feared the fate of his uncle in the first war, and
surrendered to the British on the 15th October 1852, with his son and
daughter and younger brother and other relatives, riding into the British
lines near Rathemyo on a pony. He had had a quarrel with Prince
Mindon, who deposed Pagan Min in February 1853, and had to remain
in British Burma. He was at first kept on a British warship the

His lesser wife, Ma Thi La, has been described. The pongyi gives
Ma Shwe Su as the eldest child., and then Ma Toke Toke, of the long ears,
whose descendant U Kyet Kyi is living in Budalin. He gives U Gyi as the
next child, as stated above, and then U Hkyon, who married Ma Kyin Thi,
who was born in 1826. U Hkyon was Alon Wun and Tabayin Wun in the
reigns of Bagyidaw, Tharrawaddy Min, and Pagan Min. He is wrongly
called U Chin in the list of Tabayin Wuns at page 405 of the Upper Burma
Gazetteer, part 2, volume 3. His daughter Ma Nyein Me married U San
Pu, a cousin of Ma Kin Mè, second wife of the Kinwun Mingyi. U San Pu
was headman of Kunohn tract, north of Budalin, from 1913 till 1919. His
father, Mg Sein, who was headman before him, was Ma Kin Me's first
cousin. U San Pu was still living in 1934; his son is said to be in the
Shan States. U Hkyon's son U Po Tha was born at Ngapayin, and became
headman of Okpo on the district border in 1889, his predecessor having
been killed by dacoits during Annexation troubles. Information about this
branch of the family is to be found in petitions and other papers concerning
the elections of headman at Okpo, preserved in the Record Room at
Monywa. U Po Tha was a sergeant in the mounted police from 1887 to
1889, and was active in the operations aganist the famous dacoit bo, Hla U,
and others, and was present during their attack on Wadawma police-station.
He died in 1896, when the headmanship went into another family, despite
petitions by Ma Hkyin Thi and U Hkyon who was still living then in
Ngapayin, in considerable poverty. It is nowhere mentioned that he is the
son of the great Bandula, presumably because it was feared that his father's
fierce opposition to the British might prejudice them against his family.
U Hkyon's wife is said on page 125 of Thein Maung to be Ma Ngwe Ya,
but locally I was told Ma Ngwe Ma. She was born in 1871 and is still
living in Alon. Their eldest son, U Nyo, was only about eleven when his
father died. He lived for some time in a monastery at Yinmabin under the
name of U Nyeya. The second son, U So, is now in Alon: he was born

By some accounts, he had to flee from court in consequence of
striking an official in his early days, and took refuge near Alon in a flat
scrub-jungle country between Alon and Ngapayin, where his friends and
relations supplied him with food. He was presently restored to favour and
in June 1813 was appointed Tabayin Myowun. By these accounts, it was
while he was absconding that he promised to build a pagoda if all came
well, but by others it was before he began his invasion of Assam in 1816.
At any rate his Thissaman pagoda still remains, in the Taungbyin Shwetaga
quarter of Alon. It was built in August 1819 near the now vanished
Shwetaga gate, and is in fairly good repair: the present author had the
precincts cleared of vegetation in 1933. It is sixty-five feet high, and the
sides of the plinth are ninety-two feet long. It is of conventional pattern,
with a large stone figure of a lion at each of the four corners, and four
vases above the lions about half-way up the pagoda. The walls of the

He founded the Neyaban pagoda at Myinmu, and a pagoda, a tank,
and a garden, in Yeu, and a garden north of Tabayin, which are still called

property in Budalin. One of the three trees survives, a tree of most
luxuriant foliage, which still gives a fair crop of fruit. He also planted there
two thi-bins that have died, and an okshit-bin which remains.

In 1819 Bandula's master, the Sagaing Prince, ascended the throne as
King Bagyidaw, and at once rewarded his follower, who was appointed.
Alon Wun, given a present of a thousand rupees, and granted lands near
Ngapayin which brought his total holding up to two hundred acres.
recent years a book was found at Kudaw near Ngapayin, which is supposed
to have belonged to him. It contains prophecies for some years from 1810
onwards, and is described at page 44 et seq of B.R.S.J. 5. 2. Bandula's.
greatest memorial in this region is the pagoda called by his name, which
he began to build near his birth-place in the centre of Ngapayin village,
and which was finished in August 1825, after his death. It stands on raised
ground, and is visible from the railway-line near Ngapayin station. It is in
fair repair, and is protected by solid walls, in the midst of each side of
which is a handsome elaborately carved gateway with stylized Naga.
ornaments. The walls are in fair condition, and the compound is kept
moderately clean. Near the main pagoda is a small modern one, a bell,
and a tazaung. The bell is of copper, weighs 750 ticals, and has on top of
it two chinthes and two nagas and on its sides a long inscription describing
the foundation. The teak image-house contains a gift statue of Buddha
in a sitting position, and preserves its original gilt and lacquered pillars and
a charming gilded ceilng with carvings of lotuses and dragons in bold
relief. Original, too, are some other flower and foliage designs, partly gilt..
According to the inscription on the bell, this tazaung cost Rs. 36,715, and.
the pagoda Rs. 30,050. The latter seems poor value compared with
Thathanabaing U Nyeya's much more elaborate pagoda at Payagyi, not far
away, which cost only Rs. 26,000 in 1846: but Bandula's is not so spick.
and span as the other, and the top portion is said to have been gilded.
formerly. There are no trustees, and no funds are available for keeping it
in good repair. Round it are four chinthes. It height is 82 feet. Two
photographs of the pagoda and one each of the bell and Bandula's monastery
were published, with an account of the origin of the name Ngapayin, in
the 'Sun Illustrated Weekly' of July 1932.
