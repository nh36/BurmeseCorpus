A JOURNEY TO DALET IN 1827.*
26th Nocember 1827. Embarked on board the wheel Flat accom.
panied by Lieutt. Glen with a detachment of the Police Corps and
Mr. Duff in charge of the Akyab Division of Gun Boats and after a
pleasant passage reached Talak 1 on the night of the 30th. I was much
satisfied to find that several villages had sprung up on both banks of
the Talak river since my last trip and that the village of Talak was also
much increased and a considerable trade carried on with the people on
the opposite side of the Yoomadong² who bring over Burma silks,
terra Japonica, cotton and cotton thread, Japan boxes, and some bullion
which they exchange for Nyapie, British piece goods and beetle nut,
&c. 150 bullocks laden with merchandize of the above descrip-
tion had come from the banks of the Irrawaddy a few days previous
to our arrival and the merchants having got rid of their wares were
preparing to return. They told me that the proclomation relative to
the fair had not reached the Burmah dominions and requested that I
would furnish them with copies which I readily complied with notwith-
standing the proclamation had been widely circulated in our provinces.
I regret to say that the fair was attended by very few people either
from Ramree, Sandoway or the town of Arracan and by only five
Bengallee shopkeepers from Akyab. Every credit is due to the Zeemin.
dar of Talak and to the Durogah of that Thanah for the great pains
that had been taken by them in the construction of a commodious, neat
Bazar; its not being occupied must be attributed to the apathy of the
people in our own provinces who, altho' almost all pedlars and petty
merchants, are wanting in the true spirit of enterprize. I have no doubt,
however, that after the objects of the annual marts at Talak and Aings
shall have been properly understood by the people of both countries
the fairs will be well attended and the much to be wished for free and
friendly intercourse established on a footing likely to ensure the interests
of both States. The Burmah government at present, however, with its
usual blind policy has adopted measures more likely to cramp the ardour
of the mercantile community of its dominions than to promote their
prosperity. Permit chokees have been established on the opposite side
of the mountains to levy taxes upon all exports and imports at the

6th. Finding it rather difficult to pay the hill tribes a personal visit,
I resolved to depute Tongrabo with a party of hill men to request the
chiefs to come to a palaver desiring him to assure them that they should
be well received and entertained. He accordingly set out with about 20 hill
men in two small dingies, expecting to reach his destination by the
following night. This day Captain Thompson from Sandoway joined
our party.
Friday 7th. December. Started after breakfast in two small open
dingies; there being ten paddles to each we were propelled up the
stream with astonishing velocity. When brought up by a shallow the
men jumped out and forced the skiff over by main force. At 2 o'clock
P.M. reached the mouth of the left branch up which we steered and
after being dragged over many rapid shallows we were at length
brought to a standstill about 12 miles above where we had left our
accommodation boats and about 30 miles to the N. E. of Talak Than-
nah. Although we were not so successful as we could have wished
we had the gratification to know that we had penetrated further in
that direction than any Europeans before us. The scenery is wild and

Saturday 8th December. This morning at daybreak Mr. Duff

branch of the Talak river which is reported to take its rise in the last
range of hills leading into Ava.
Sunday 9th December. Mr. Duff not having returned and as yet
no account of the mission and the depth of the stream daily decreasing
we deemed it advisable to drop down and return to Talak, which place
we reached the same evening and were shortly afterwards rejoined by
Mr. Duff who had e plored up the right branch about 15 miles above
its junction with the main river. He reports the hills and scenery to be
similar to what we have already met with but the nearer to the source
the hills are not so thickly clothed with bamboo and there is more forest
timber. The Gurjan² tree in great aboundance and well grown. The
course from the junction for the first ten miles runs in a N.N.W.
direction and then branches again off into streams, one towards the N.
N.W.; the other to the N.E. Up the latter Mr. Duff proceeded about
five miles when the stream became so shallow, narrow and rocky that it
was difficult to proceed further, and, as all his provisions together with
what supplies the hill men had taken with them were expended, he was
obliged to return.

Monday 10th December 1827. This forenoon Tongrabo returned
from his mission which although not attended with success has opened
the door for further communication. He reports that the chiefs were
not averse to come down but were afraid of the displeasure of the Phyng
Meosogree to whom they pay tribute now. As their villages are on the
Arakan side of the Yoomadong it is necessary that the chiefs should
acknowledge our Government and this can only be effected by nomina-
ting one of the hill tribes to the Sardarship of the mountains and nullahs
to the eastward of the Talak river, known by the name of Talak Ashay,
a mountain tract extending to the frontier of the Ava dominions in
length about 60 miles by from 10 to 15 broad. The policy of such a
measure is evident for the Mughs who have obtained mountain tracts,
particularly those remigrated from Ramoo² are totally unfit to hold
the situation, there (sic) want of energy, and difference of caste are
serious obstacles, in addition to which they are afraid of the hill men
and consequently have never attempted, far less studied, the necessity
of cultivating a friendly intercourse with them, who in return feel annoyed
at being placed under the control of those who neither have claims from
their ancestors for the superiority nor take any interest in their welfare.

CUSTOMS OF THE KHYANGS,
MARRIAGE.
The Khyang sends presents to his bride of hogs, Buffaloes and cloth
according to his means and the Bride returns the compliments by pre-
senting her intended Husband with a spear, and Khong2 (a large jar
in which a Spirit is distilled from a preparation of boiled rice for the
use of the family) these preliminaries having been concluded, they enter
into an engagement before witnesses to live together as man and wife,
but have no other ceremony. The Khyangs are a hospitable race
amongst themselves, and for every time the Khong, above alluded to,
has been emptied, they keep a tally of small split pieces of Bamboo
which are stuck in the most conspicuous part of the inside of the roof to
shew the extent of the proprietor's hospitability. Formerly the chiefs,
similarly to the usage of the ancident feudal times of Britain, claimed a
right to all the pretty women of the tribe, which they too often asserted
to the great annoyance of their vassals, who, to protect the honour of
their wives, adopted the System of tattooing the faces of all their women,
and the Custom gradually became common amongst all ranks and
continues so this day; in fact should a female child die before the Cere.
mony of tattooing had been effected the friends would consider it nece.
ssary to blacken the face with charcoal before committing the body to
the flames.
LAWS OF DIVORCE.
Should the wife wish for a Separation she has only to return an
equivilent in Kind to the Husband for the presents She received from
him before the contract, when she is at liberty to form a new connexion,
but if the husband wishes for a Separation he must give up all the
property, moveable and unmoveable. If the parties separated by mutual

PENAL LAWS.
Should a Khyang kill one of his own tribe, Kisas is not demanded, but
the Culprit must give to the nearest relation of the deceased two Slaves
and, if not able to furnish them, he and his wife must go into Slavery
but, if a Khyang kill one of another tribe, the Suffering tribe demands
two lives in retribution and, if not given up, takes vengeance when
opportunity offers.—
RESOURCES AND EMPLOYMENT.
The Khyangs are an industrious race, and want is very rarely expe-
rienced amongst them excepting when at war with a neighbouring tribe
when their villages are burnt, and their cultivation laid waste by their
Mutual Marauding incursions, and all the males and Females carried
into slavery, where they remain, till either ransomed or exchanged.
They hardly know the use of money but their mountains produce Cotton
and Tobacco and Sufficient Rice for their consumption, the streams
abound with fish, the forests with game, and they find no scarcity of
excellent roots to supply the place of vegetables, in addition to which,
they have the plantain in abundance with varicus other fruits. The
Khyangs employ themselves in hunting when not engaged in agricultural
pursuits, and their women either in weeding their cotton and tobacco
plantations, or in the useful arts, Such as Spinning and weaving; the
thread and cloth with what bees wax and Elephants teeth they may find
in the forests enable them to pay to the ruling power a considerable
revenue which is always received in kind and as the thread and cloths
are much prized by the people in the plains no difficulty is experienced
in converting them together with the ivory and bees-Wax either into
bullion or Cash.
HABITATIONS.
The villages, some of which are large containing from 50 to 100
families, are each under the control of a petty chief; the Huts are
elevated above the ground from 10 to 15 feet; the entrance is by a
small ladder which is generally drawn up at night to prevent sudden
surprise; 5 or 6 families generally live under one roof; each has a

The Khyangs not being restricted by their religion, if any they
have, indulge their appetites in every thing that pleases the eye; dogs,
buffaloes, hogs, Elephants, tigers, Allegators and reptiles of every kind
are all equally palatable, but the far famed Ngapee is their greatest

DRESS.
The men generally wear nothing on their bodies, but a small piece of
cloth to cover the private parts, but are seldom seen without a turband
of blue cotton cloth; the women wear a loose covering, something
resembling a shift coming down nearly to the knee; in the higher classes
embroidered with thread of various colors of which the indigo and
other colouring plants to be found in their neighbourhood furnish a
tasteful Supply.
ARMS.
The use of the gun and matchlock is not unknown to the hill tribes,
but as they do not understand the art of manufacturing good powder,
their offensive and defensive weapons chiefly consist in the Spear, long
Dhow, Shield and cross bows. When they go on a marauding excursion,
they always carry with them a large supply of slips of bamboo sharply
pointed which they fix in the ground to retard the pursuit of their
followers, and, as hill men never use shoes, they dare not venture to
follow a marauding party at Night.

The language, although almost entirely unknown to the People of

RELIGION.
The Khyangs acknowledge a Supreme being and believe in
Supernatural Agents without Number; to the latter they pay great
adoration.
MUSIC.
The only instruments that I have seen amongst the hill tribes are the
Gong, Tomtom, Cymbols and a sort of Flute. The Mugs and Burmese,
however, have a taste for Music and regular bands of musicians are to
be found in every District. The head musician of the Province holds a
title from the King and none are permitted to practise Music or perform
plays without a license from him. The Chief musician pays Annually
to the Government a handsome sum for this privilege.
GENERAL REMARKS.
As the Khyangs are a most Superstitious race nothing is more
likely to ensure success in any communication with them than impres-
sing upon their minds the idea of being Superior to them ins general
knowledge and occult Arts. I could therefore wish when I proceed on
my purposed trip to the Source of the Kaladyne and Oosklyne River, on
our North Eastern frontier, to be furnished with means for a few chemi-
cal experiments, with an electrical machine, a few common musical
boxes, and Phantasmagoria with a numerous and selected set of
slides. The present Sir John Malcolm when on his last Mission to
Persia found that the last made a very desirable impression upon the
minds of the comparatively well informed, polished, and polite People
of that Country; Consequently it may be expected that the utmost
surprise and wonder will be excited, by a proper management, of what
will be considered by the Hill tribes, Supernatural Agency; at any rate,
it will create extreme curiosity, and many Chiefs will, from that motive,
be induced to pay me a visit, who would otherwise remain aloof in their
Mountain fortresses, and, as the motive of my visit will be to cultivate a
friendly intercourse with the numerous and powerful tribes that inhabit
those regions, no means likely to accomplish this desirable object ought
to be neglected.

In January last year Doctor Carr 1 and self made a trip to Talak
and Aing in an open ship's boat and were exposed to the effects of the
climate night and day for a fortnight yet all our party including servants
and Clashies 2 amounting to 18 souls returned in excellent health. How
very different, however, has been our fate this time. Four fifths of
those who accompanied me are in hospital; Lieutt Glen³ has already fallen
a victim to the malignant fever with two sepoys and one Chuprassi and
I am now after a most painful illness only recovering from its baneful
effects; nothing can be a clearer proof of its virulence, the Mugs who
accompanied us having suffered in as great a degree as the Hindoostanees.
From what I have already seen and heard of the interior, particularly
of the mountains, I am inclined to think that the months of February and
March are the best for making excursions, but then no trip should be
commenced before the sun has risen sufficiently to dissipate the fogs
which invariably hang over the mountains and forests till a late hour in the
afternoon. Care should also be taken to get under cover before dark
for the dews are so heavy and the changes from intense heat to extreme
cold so very sudden that unless every possible precaution to counteract
the bad effects be taken a malignant jungle fever will assuredly be the
consequence.
Sd. C. Paton
