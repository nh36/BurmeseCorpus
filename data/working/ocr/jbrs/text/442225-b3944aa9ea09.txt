THE EFFECT OF CONCENTRATION OF SALTS ON THEIR
RATE OF ABSORPTION BY PLANTS IN WATER CULTURE
AND THE ROLE OF AERATION
by
Than Tun*
INTRODUCTION
The significance of the concentration of salts in relation to the rate of absorption
has not received as much attention as other aspects of salt uptake.
Judging from the literature, the general belief seems to be that the rate of uptake of
ions is dependent on their concentration in the culture medium, the reduction in concentra-
tion leading to a reduced rate of uptake. In opposition to this view, Olsen came
to the conclusion that "the rate of absorption is independent of the concentration,
of course apart from the very lowest concentrations lying below 0.003 mg. equivalent
per litre."
Olsen criticises the previous workers on the following grounds.
Some investigators used solutions of different pH values. For example, the
weakest solution used by Gracenin had a pH of 4.6 and the strongest solution had a pH
(3.1, and the rate of phosphate uptake rises rapidly with decreasing pH.
Most investigators used ordinary distilled water which contains about 0.2 mg. of
copper per litre, and this amount has toxic effects on plants and decreases the rate of
uptake of other ions when the concentration of the solution is low, while copper does not
have this effect when the concentration of the solution is high, due to the antagonistic
effect of other ions.
Olsen seems to attach the greatest importance to "stirring the solution", and
attributes to this the effect of aeration of the medium. He reasons that "if the solution is
allowed to remain unstirred, the plants first absorb the part of the solution nearest to the
root. Hence a considerable difference in concentration is caused in the solution, which
can only be evened out by diffusion, but, as is well known, this is a slow process. The
greater the difference in concentration, the stronger, however, will be the stream of ions
diffusing in, and therefore plants in stationary solutions are able to absorb greater
amounts of ions from solutions with high concentrations than from solutions with lower".

MATERIALS and METHODS
1. Preliminary Culture of Seedlings
The undermentioned method was found to be the most satisfactory for getting
seedlings with undamaged root system, as required for the experiments.
For wheat seedlings, the seeds, after being soaked in water, were kept in a refrige-
rator over a night and then were sown on thoroughly wetted blotting paper in a tray
covered with a glass sheet. When they were about three days old, 12 seedlings were
transferred to a cork mat with 12 holes in such a way that the root system passed through
the holes with the seeds resting on the mat. The cork mat with the seedlings was floated
on the culture medium contained in a 600 ml beaker. Through a hole on the side of the
cork mat, a glass tube for aerating the culture medium passed to the bottom of the
beaker. Continuous aeration was maintained by an electric air pump. As the plants
were grown in the laboratory where the light intensity was low, an additional supply of
light was given every day from 8.00 am, to 11.00 pm., using 4 hundred-watt bulbs, kept
3 feet above the plants. The root system was also darkened by wrapping the beaker with
thick tarred paper. The plants were grown in this way for 14-20 days before being used
for the experiments.
For pea seedlings, the seeds were first soaked in water over a night and were sown
in thoroughly wetted sawdust. After 3 days' growth, 9 seedlings were transferred to a
perforated cork mat, and grown in the same way as the wheat.

III. Experimental Method for the Estimation of the Rate of Sali Absorption
The cork mat with the seedlings from the preliminary culture was taken out and the
cork together with the roots was thoroughly washed with distilled water, and then
transferred to a known volume of culture solution of the required composition contained
in a beaker, and aerated continuously. After the solution had been left aside about one
hour till equilibrium was attained, an initial sample of appropriate volume was taken out
for analysis of phosphate and nitrate. Then further samples were taken out and analysed
at intervals.
The amount of phosphate and nitrate absorbed was plotted against time. From
the slope of the graph, the rate can be calculated.

EXPERIMENT II
1)
This series of experiments was designed:-
to study the effect of the concentration of phosphate and nitrate on their rate of
uptake by seedlings:
2) to investigate the possible reason for not observing any uptake of nitrate by seedlings,
as found in Experiment I;
3) to study the role of aeration and to discover whether its effect lies in the physical
process of stirring or in maintaining high oxygen tension in the medium for root respira-
tion, in which case the effect will be regarded as physiological.
The possibility has been suggested that even when the seedlings were 18 days old,
they were relying on the seed nitrogen and that they used seed nitrogen in preference to
the externally supplied inorganic nitrogen source. To verify this hypothesis, seedlings
were grown in the preliminary culture as explained before, but with their seeds removed
after 5 days growth. Thus, after removal of the seeds, the seedlings would have to rely
on the externally supplied inorganic nitrogen source. Seedlings grown in this manner
were used in all the later experiments.
Since the seedlings were grown in the same way for the same period, it was assumed
that they had approximately the same amount of root system in the sets of the same type
of plant, and this was confirmed experimentally on a dry weight basis. Thus, if the rate
of absortion of salts by different sets of the same type of plant in Concentration “5” (the
same concentration as the preliminary culture solution) and in Concentration “1” (1/5 of
the concentration of the preliminary culture) are equal, we may take it as an indication
that the rate of salt absorption is independent of the concentration of it in the culture
solution, at least over the range from Concentration "5" to Concentration "I".
Also, if the main effect of aeration of the medium on the rate of salt uptake is due
to the physical process of stirring, then if nitrogen gas is bubbled through instead of air,
the rate of uptake of salts should remain unchanged. On the other hand, if it decreases
significantly, the inference will be that the effect of aeration is also due to maintaining a
high oxygen tension in the medium for root respiration, in which case it will play a
physiological role.

On the basis of the above theoretical considerations, the experiments were carried
out as follows:
Absorption of phosphate and nitrate was studied with one set of pea seedlings and
one set of wheat seedlings in Concetnration "5". A similar procedure was adopted with
Concentration "I". After about 5-6 hours, aeration was stopped in one set of pea
seedlings in Concentration "5" and in one set of wheat seedlings in Concentration "1",
and nitrogen gas was bubbled continuously through the medium. The study of absorp-
tion was continued.
Thus, taking the whole series of experiments together, nitrogen gas was bubbled
into one Concentration "5" and one Concentration "1"; another Concentration “1”
and another Concentration "5", which were still aerated, served as controls.
Seedlings used in this experimental srries were 18 days old.
The results are presented in graphical form (see Fig. IV-VII).
Observations and Conclusions
1) There was considerable uptake of both nitrate and phosphate by both pea and wheat
seedlings, unlike in experiment I. This seems to support the hypothesis that even up to
18 days after germination, the seeds were relying mainly on the seed nitrogen, and only
when this source of nitrogen was removed did they absorb the externally supplied nitrate,
and that the seed nitrogen was more readily assimilated than the nitrate supplied in the
medium.
2) The rate of phosphate uptake per set of seedlings is approximately the same for each
type of plant at a comparable period in both Concentration “5” and Concentration “1”,
indicating that it is independent of the Concentration over the range tested (i.e. from about
3.2 mg. to 16.3 mg. of phosphate per litre of cultural solution) (see Fig. IV & V).
3) Oddly enough, the rate of nitrate uptake, by contrast with the uptake of phosphate,
was markedly influenced by its concentration in the medium. When the concentration of
nitrate was increased from 35.0 mg. to 176.0 mg. per litre, the rate of its absorption
increased by about 2.2 times (see Fig. VI & VII).
4) After aeration was stopped, and even when nitrogen gas was bubbled through the
culture solution to stir it vigorously, the rate of uptake of both nitrate and phosphate
continued for about 1-2 hours at a much reduced rate and practically stopped after that.
This suggests the effect of aeration of the medium on the rate of salt uptake was not due
to the physical process of stirring, but to the oxygen supply having a physiological effect
(see Fig. IV-VII).

This series of experiments was designed so that a direct comparison of the rate of
uptake of phosphate and nitrate by the same set of plants in media of different concentra-
tions could be made.
The two culture solutions used in this experimental series were the same as in
Experiment II, namely, Concentration "1" and Concentration "5". The absorption of
phosphate and nitrate by one set of pea seedlings and by one set of wheat seedlings was
first studied in Concentration "1" for a period of three hours, after which the same sets
were transferred to Concentration "5" and the absorption of phosphate and nitrate was
further followed. Another set of a pea seedlings and another set of wheat seedlings were
used in similar experiments, but absorption was first studied in Concentration “5” and
later in Concentration "**
Seedlings used in this experimental series were 20 days old.
Results are presented in graphical form (Fig. VIII-XI).
Observations and Conclusions
1) The rates of uptake of phosphate by the same set of plants in Concentration "1" and
in Concentration "5" were approximately the same, suggesting that absorption of phos-
phate is independent of its concentration in the culture medium over the range investigat-
ed, i.e. from about 3.2 to 16.3 mg. per litre (see Fig. VIII, IX).
2) The rate of uptake of nitrate was markedly affected by its concentration in the
external medium. The increase of nitrate concentration from 35 to 176 mg. per litre was
followed by an increase in the rate of its absorption by about 3.2-3.7 times (see
Fig. X-XI).
SUMMARY AND DISCUSSION
1)
Seedlings grown with their seeds attached to them even up to about 18 days of age,
did not absorb detectable amounts of nitrate, though they absorbed considerable amounts
of phospate from the culture medium (Experiment I),
Seedlings with their seeds removed absorbed considerable quantities of nitrate as
well as phosphate (Experiments II and III). This suggests that both pea and wheat
seedlings, even 18 days after germination, relied solely on the seed nitrogen, and only
when this source was removed did they absorb the inorganic nitrate supplied in the
medium. It also suggests that seed nitrogen is more readily assimilated than the inorganic
nitrate supplied in the medium.
2) The rate of absorption of phosphate by seedlings was high during the "day-period"
and low during the "night-period" (Experiment I). Two main possible factors, acting
directly or indirectly, singly or together, are suggested:
(a) Light: As we are dealing with a complex system, i.e. a whole plant, it is
possible that light may have some effect on the rate of salt absorption through its effect on
the physiology of the shoot system. It is likely that the effect of light operates via its
effect on the rate of transpiration. The correlation between the rate of transpiration
and that extent of salt uptake has been reported by Hylmo (1953) and Brouwer (1954,
1956).

(b) Temperature: As with any other physiological process, this factor may have a
considerable effect on the rate of salt uptake. The temperature of the medium dropped
from an average day temperature of 20°C to an average of 18°C during the night. It is
also not unlikely that temperature affects salt uptake indirectly through its influence on
transpiration.
At the moment it is not possible to evaluate the relative importance of these factors,
which can only be done by carrying out experiments under varying light intensities and
temperatures.
3) After stopping aeration, and even when nitrogen gas was bubbled vigorously through
the medium, salt absorption continued at a much reduced rate for about 1-2 hours, and
ceased thereafter (Experiment II). This clearly shows that the effect of aeration of the
medium does not lie in its mechanical stirring, but that aeration is important as a means
of keeping high oxygen tension in the medium for root respiration. This is in agreement
with the finding that salt uptake is coupled with the level of oxygen (reported by Steward
(1933), Hoagland and Broyer (1936, 1942).
4) The rate of absorption of phosphate is independent of its concentration in the external
medium, in agreement with Olsen's finding (1950). The rate is the same in culture
solutions having 3.2 to 16.3 of phosphate per litre (Experiments II and III).
The rate of nitrate absorption, on the other hand, is greatly influenced by its
concentration in the external medium. This finding is contradictory to that of Olsen
(1950). Increase of nitrate concentration from 35 mg. to 176 mg. per litre was accom-
panied by an increase in the rate of uptake by 2.3-3.7 times (Experiments II,III).
It is not possible to explain the difference in nature between the rates of nitrate
absorption and phosphate absorption in relation to their concentration in the culture
solution. This difference may possibly lie in their different metabolic role, but further
work is needed to elucidate this point.
