"Some Account of the Wars between Burmah and China," is the
title of what is by far Burney's most important contribution to the
Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal. It runs to 94 pages of very
close print. 30 It traces the history of relations between the two countries
from the earliest times down to the exchange of embassies between
Peking and Ava in 1833-4. Burney's treatment of the subject from
the middle of the eighteenth century onwards is the most detailed ever
to be published in English and contains so many translations from
original material that it is a positive mine of information. His account
of the Chinese invasions of Burma during the years 1765-9 compares
favourably to put it mildy-- with the story told by T'eng Tch'ong
in his Ming 1 translated by Siguret in the second volume of

The translations of the journals kept by the Burmese envoys are
of unique interest. "I have procured copies of the routes and of most
of the reports submitted to the king by each", writes Burney of the
missions of 1823 and 1833. And of the 1823 mission: "Two or three
years ago, at my request, the ministers of Ava kindly made the
subordinate Burmese envoys draw up an abstract of the report they had
sent in, and I now give a translation of it. " 37 The envoys put down
not only the details of their journeys but much else besides. "When
you reach Pekin," run the instructions to the 1833 mission, "observe
and record everything carefully and unreservedly. You must note and
bring back with you, after making enquiries secretly and ascertaining,
what the Emperor of China worships in order to obtain Neibban; what
he practises and worships in order to obtain advantages in this world;
as well as an account of his queens, concubines, kinsmen, children,
nobles and officers, and of their equipage, dress and ceremonies, with a
map and description of China and Tartary." 38 And Burney's
translated extracts of their reports show that they carried out the task
faithfully.

Burney himself was particularly interested in Buddhist studies, and
the splendid collection of Burmese manuscripts, which he presented to
the East India Company's Library-now the India Office Library-contains
a large proportion of Buddhist writings. His papers in the Royal
Empire Society's collection contain extensive and scholarly notes on
Buddhism in his handwriting. The India Office collection also includes
the Burmese materials which he collected on the subject of the Kabaw
Valley question, with much annotation and translation in his
handwriting.
His papers provide ample testimony, if indeed it were needed, of
the high standard of scholarship at the Court of Ava during his time.
In the Royal Empire Society's collection there is a note from the
distinguished Sanscrit scholor, Horace Hayman Wilson, asking Burney
to procure for him a Pali grammar and vocabulary from the Court
Brahmans, if possible', he writes, transcribed into Bengali or Nagari
characters'. There is also one form the lexicographer-missionary,
Dr. Adoniram Judson, introducing to him a Pali dictionary,
which, he says, is "the only one that has ever obtained currency among
the learned of this country", and, he goes on, "it is precisely the
Sanscrit Amera Kosha, adapted to the Pali dialect and written in the
modern Burman character. This work could be of inestimable use to
any Sanscrit scholar, who should desire to investigate the Pali language
through the medium of Burman". Presumably an adaption of this
work is referred to in Burney's obituary notice in the Royal Asiatic
Society's Proceedings. The reference runs: "A Dictionary of Pali,
with explanations in Sanskrit and Bengali, was also compiled by his
desire, a copy of which is now [ie. 1846] deposited in the Bodleian
Library at Oxford”. 40
An English-Burmese dictionary was in course of preparation
at the Burmese capital, when Burney first arrived. Its compilers were
the Mekkhara Prince, a man of wide learning who was uncle to the
King, and Charles Lane, an English merchant resident there. Burney
drew the attention of the Asiatic Society of Bengal to the work and
secured its publication through the society.

And among Burney's unremitting efforts to stimulate others to produce
work one must not omit to mention that it was under his personal
supervision that his assistant, Dr. G. T. Bayfield, compiled the Histori-
cal Review of the Political Relations between the British Government in
India and the Empire of Ava 42 up to the year 1834, which until quite
recent times was the main source for the study of this period in Anglo-
Burmese relations. It is not too much to say that under Burney's
influence the British Residency at Ava became a centre of original
research, a veritable hive of industry. What might ultimately have been
produced, under more favourable circumstances, may be judged from
the immense mass of Burney's own unpublished material. But the Ava
revolution of 1837 and the breakdown in Burney's health brought these
fruitful activities to a sudden and final, stop; for the Residency had to
be withdrawn and Burney's health never recovered sufficiently for him
seriously to resume the work that was so rudely interrupted when the
Tharrawaddy Prince hoisted the standard of rebellion against the Court
of Ava and dethroned his brother, King Bagyidaw. It is a sad story,
for there can be hardly any servant of the East India Company, who
has contributed more to its invaluable records, in quantity or in quality.
