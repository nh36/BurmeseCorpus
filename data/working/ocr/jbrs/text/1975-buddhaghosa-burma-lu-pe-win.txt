Buddhaghosa and Burma
U LU PE WIN*
Among the countries, to which the
great Buddhist emperor of India, Asoka
(who styled himself in his rock and pillar
edicts as Devanampiyo Piyadassi) sent
missionaries, were Sri Lanka and Coastal
Burma, then known as Suvarnabhumi.
The thera Mahinda who was sent to Sri
Lanka dissemminated the dhamma of
Buddha not only in Pali but also in
the language of the country, namely,
Sinhalese. Similarly, the two theras, Sona
and Uttara, went to Suvannabhumi and
taught the Tipitaka in the language of the
Kingdom, namely: Môn.
From the time of Piyadassi Asoka in
the 3rd Century B.C. up to the time when
the Mahavihara school of studies in Ceylon
had become world famous, Burma and
Sri Lanka kept on maintaining constant
cultural contact. There was a regular in-
terchange of Pali scholars between these
two centres of Păți learning, in the course
of which Cula Buddhaghosa of Suvanṇa-
bhumi, coastal Burma, visited Sri Lanka
and came back with Maha Buddhaghosa,
the great commentator and author of
Visuddhimagga, who had previously
reached Sri Lanka from Budhgaya, in
Majjhimadesa, India.
According to the ancestral tradition
(acariyaparampara)2 of the Môn monks,
the two theras Buddhaghosa, senior and
junior, brought with them all the atṭha-
katha books (now numbering fiftytwo) for
the dissemmination of Buddha's dhamma
in the then greater Môn area.
Moreover, when Ceylon was threatened
with invasion and consequent colonisation
by the Colas, Burma became an asylum
in A.D. 9th century for many a learned
bhikkhu of Sihala dipa (Sri Lanka), who
brought with them their Pāļi canonical and
extra-canonical literature in the form of
palm-leaf manuscripts.³

It was thus, that the treatises of
atthakatha reached coastal Burma (suvaṇ-
nabhumi and suvappadipa) and spread
throughout the country. The sincerely
sustained and serious study of these aṭṭha-
kathas came to have a great influence in
the lives of the Môn and Burmese samgħa,
and even of the laity.'
Evidently, the impact of the atthaka-
tha literature, as distinct from the Pāļi
pitakas, has been greater in Burma than
in other Buddhist lands. The Burmese
bhikkhus have all along had not only the
help of the atthakathas in their sincerely
serious study of the pitakas, but also the
Burmese translations of the same, in the
form of nissayas and various grades
of sub-commentaries known as tīkās,
mulatikās, madhu fikas and anutikas to-
gether with their ganthi
Since the pre-Pagan period in Thatôn
and since the palmy days of Pagan, Budd-
haghosa's Jataka commentary had long
become the code of conduct for every
teenager, particularly male minors of
Burma. It is still so for almost every
family. The Jatakas provide noble examples
of selfless social service rendered by the
Bodhisat, whether he was reborn as the
son of a sovereign or a village headman,
a caravan leader, a buyer and seller, a
trader and hawker or a craftsman or what
not. All these birth-stories according as
contained in Buddhaghosa's atthakatha,
the teenagers of Burma learn as Sā-
spiritual' preceptors (upajjhaya) beginning
maneras, Buddhist novices, from their
from the very evening as and when they
have become initiated into the Order of
monks (samgha.) It is no wonder, there-
fore, that in an area of some sixteen
square miles, where many square hollow
shrines are still standing in hundreds, if
not in thousands, the Jātaka commentary
plays a prominent part in the stone and
terracotta sculptures and mural paintings
of Pagan which have all along adorned
all the temples since A.D 11th 12th

It is to be safely surmised that Buddha-
ghosa purposely pinpointed and tabooed
these two treatises to stop the rising tide
of Sanskrit studies, which had in his time
superseded and ousted the Pali-pursuits
in entire India except at Kañcipūra, near
the present city of Madras. Even in the
then Sīhaladīpa, the co-eval of and rival
to Mahavihara School, namely, the Abha-
yagiri Institute was showing signs of
promoting Sanskrit studies (from the fifth
century A. D. onwards) in preference to
Pali learning.
Over his deep concern lest pursuit of
Pāli studies should wane more and more,
Buddhaghosa had but to champion the
cause of his favourite language, totally
tabooing the study of its rival tongue.
Thus, the literature of Ramayaṇa, in par-
ticular, went to other lands beyond the
borders of Burma. It was only (after
the death of Alaungpaya and of his son
and heir) when his second son succeeded
the throne and the king of Burma, Ava
Sinbyushin, had conquered Ayuthia (the
then capital city of Siam) and brought
back to Burma Siamese scholars and
literature, that Ramāyaṇa came to be
studied and adapted as well as adopted
to be incorporated into Burmese literature
in the reign of his successors, particularly
of Bodawpaya alias_Badôn Mintara (the
founder of Amarapura) and the latter's
successor Bagyidaw alias Sagaing min, in
the late 18th century onwards.
From the foregoing facts, it is obvious
that Burma had received and is still
receiving the immense impact of the attha-
katha literature of the great Buddhaghosa,
Buddhadatta and acariya Dhammapala
and has turned out to be the reliable
repository of atthakatha literature.
It can be safely surmised then, that
in Burma, at least the Jatakatthakatha,
the Buddhavamsa atthakatha and the
Apadana atthakatha', if not all the Pali
commentaries, must have been translated
into nissaya Burmese as far back as the
11th century A.D. if not earlier, since we
have the still standing evidence, at Pagan,
in the mural paintings of square hollow
shrines, which constitute what are virtually
the illustrated editions of all the three
aforesaid atthakathās.
