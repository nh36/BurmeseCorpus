If a foreigner, who was a student of
the English language, were confronted
with such unusual phrases as 'Ton, Pole,
Stone' or 'Fi, Car, Ho' he might well
be puzzled. Even with the aid of an
English dictionary, he would not be able
to make head or tail of them. These six
'words" stand respectively for 'Wellington,
Walpole, Gladstone,' and 'Faith, Charity'
and 'Hope' (from Latin fides, caritas and
Old English hopa). Equally he might be
bewildered if he came across a series of
words such as 'Apple Tree, Peach Flower,
Grape-vine' and 'Melon Leaf' and was
told that these eight words represented
the eight inauspicious days in cryptogram-
matic form, and that these days occur in
pairs in rotation throughout the twelve
months. Finally the foreigner would
perhaps begin to consider seriously whether
there is any point in pursuing the study
of the English tongue beyond the bounds
of the spoken language, if he discovered
that the sentence 'Cannot Simon Roger
Protest at the Normans? Conquest?' was
a chronogram on the Normans' conquest
of England in A.D. 1066. Such features
and many more complicated than these
stand out prominetly in the topography
of Burmese language and literature.
These subjects are presented here in
one article since they are held together
by the common characteristics of being
cryptic. As far as can be ascertained the
making of cryptograms and chronograms
that have gained national stature seems
to have ceased soon after the downfall
of the Burmese monarchy in 1886. Abbre-
viations however have grown in numbers
since their earliest occurence in Burmese
literature, and are still increasing.
The principles followed in abbreviating.
the words are two: abbreviation (1) by
the syllables, as in Pail and (2) by the
initial letters, as in English. These can
Conveniently be discussed by giving repre-
sentative examples from the three stages
of the growth and development of
abbreviations.

III CHRONOGRAMS
Many readers will know about a
pamphlet published in 1666, when an
engagement between the English and Dutch
navies was expected. It has in place of
the imprint of the year the sentence:
LorD have MerCle Vpon Vs. The date
is indicated by certain letters LDVMCIVV
in the sentence, written in capitals so as
to stand out. By re-arranging the letters
we get MDC/LVV/VI = 1666. This is an
English chronogram. The chronograms in
Burmese are different; but for the subject
matter they would have been classed as
cryptograms.
393
The XVIIIth and XIXth centuries in
Burma saw the beginning of a spate of
chronicles, written both in prose and in
verse forms. Among them there is a
verse-chronicle, which is epigrammatic in
form and cryptic in language. It was
composed by a monk-poet, Mon-ywe
Saya-daw, sometime in the first half of
the XIXth century: it is a thumb-nail
sketch of the history of Burma down to
A. D. 1782; the founding of the city of
Amarapura.
The chronicle is divied into 14 sections,
each section dealing with one dynasty,
and each event in one dynasty is expressed
in an epigram. Each epigram consists of
two or more lines, with jingling syllables:
one line, usually the first line, signifies
the date and the rest state the event such
as the founding or the end of a city,
the accession to the throne or the regnal
years of a king, and the key line often
has a meaning in clear. Each epigram
is also linked with the next by means
of rhyme throughout the 14 sections. This
chronicle is in fact a series of epigrams
bound together by poetical ties as well
as by the sequence of historical events.
These chronograms, as with other
chronograms and cryptograms depend
mainly on the letters of the alphabet
which represent the numbers in the days
of the week series (Table 1) or Pali and
Sanskrit syllables or words denoting
numbers (Tables 2 and 3). In some cases
Burmese numbers are also introduced.
