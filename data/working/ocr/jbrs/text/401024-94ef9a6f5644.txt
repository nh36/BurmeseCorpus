SAFETY FIRST
A STUDY IN THE ECONOMIC HISTORY OF BURMA '
by
J. S. Furnivall
A few years ago one saw everywhere in Rangoon a very striking
poster intended to impress children with the need for 'Safety First'.
It showed a group of children about to cross the road, looking carefully
in each direction, forward and backward. I want to suggest that the
same principle applies in social and economic development; it is unsafe.
to look forward without looking backward. Many Burmans are full
of plans and projects for the future, but not many seem to care much
how they fit in with the past. That is true not only of Burmans. It
is generally true of this brave new world where international experts
find profitable employment in making plans for other people's welfare.
There is a general tendency to regard social and economic development
as a very simple matter. Pump in capital and hire a few experts and
the desert will blossom like a rose; Pagan will grow rich crops of rice
and factory chimneys will transmogrify Rangoon into a delectable city
like Birmingham or Pittsburgh.
Is economic development, to say nothing of social development,
such an easy matter? In the newspapers one may read advertisements
of sayas who promise to cure everything, from cancer to consumption,
with a bottle of medicine or a box of pills, - and without seeing the
patient. Doctors call them quacks. Are not experts who undertake
to promote the welfare of a country without studying its social and
economic constitution rather like quack doctors who undertake to cure
patients without seeing them? The quack's box of pills may cure a
patient; but it may also kill him. Such remedies are dangerous.
What lies ahead depends on what has happened in the past and, as
with children crossing the road, safety first requires that we look in
both directions, backward as well as forward. In other words, if
we want to promote the development of Burma on sound lines,
we must study its social and economic history.

I do not want to suggest that history repeats itself, or that history
alone will tell us what to do. But I do suggest that a knowledge of
the past will help us to understand the present and therefore offer
guidance for the future. And I do not wish to minimize the need for
expert knowledge. But the expert on agriculture or on labour problems,
or on sanitation, or education or whatever it may be, is not an expert
on Burma. It is our job to provide the knowledge about Burma that
experts need if they are to make a success of their job. The chief
difference between a modern doctor and a Burman se-saya is that
modern medical science has a firm basis in anatomy and physiology.
The modern doctor knows more than a se-saya about disease and
should have a better chance of curing his patient. But that does not
always happen. You may remember the doctor in Shaw's Doctor's
Dilemma who stimulated the phagocytes to consume the poisonous
virus of disease by pumping in vaccines. But sometimes the vaccine
stimulated the virus instead of the phagocytes, and the patient died.
That was because the doctor did not know enough about the constitu-
tion of his patient and pumped in vaccines at the wrong time.
But on
the other hand where a modern doctor has failed a se-saya may effect
a cure although he knows less about disease because he knows more
about his patient. An expert who does not understand the conditions for
which he is prescribing remedies may do harm instead of good. That
is where a study of social and economic development may be useful.
It should help the experts in various fields trying to prescribe remedies
for what seems wrong, and also help the statesman whose task it is to
mould the future.
This is the thesis which I wish to illustrate by drawing attention to
some aspects of the social and economic development of British Burma
from 1861, when it was first constituted by the amalgamation of
Arakan, Pegu and Tenasserim, previously separate provinces, until
1886 when, by the annexation of Upper Burma, it lost its separate
identity. This, you may think, is a long, long time ago, ancient history;
what can it signify to modern Burmans at the present day? But, for
three reasons, it concerns us very closely, here and now. In 1869, part
way through this period, the opening of the Suez Canal transformed
the economic relations of East and West, and opened a new chapter in
world history, and, consequently, in the history of Burma. Briefly, it
transformed urma from a customary society into a capitalist society;.
secondly, it determined the main line of social and economic develop-

history of Burma during this period is, indeed, of more than local

in comparison with similar case histories for other countries in south-

I have just said that one consequence of the Suez Canal was the
transformation of Burma from a customary society into a capitalist
society. It should hardly be necessary to explain that a customary
society is one in which social and economic relations are regulated by
custom. Similarly a capitalist society is one in which economic and
social relations are regulated by capital. But this perhaps requires
some explanation; for the terms capital, capitalist and capitalism are
often used so loosely that they tend to be misleading unless carefully
defined - a precaution, unfortunately, too frequently neglected. Even at
the risk of being tedious I wish to make this point quite clear. Ordinarily,
when a cultivator sows his land with rice, the land yields more than is
required to maintain him until the following harvest, This is true of
material production in general; production involves the expenditure of
economic energy and normally yields a surplus over the energy expended.
Custom may dictate inefficient methods of production; but common
sense and the general interest require that production; should be
efficient and should yield as large a surplus as is consistent with social
welfare. The surplus may be neglected, or wasted, or else stored as
wealth. Formerly cultivators in Lower Burma did not trouble some-
times to reap all their rice. I have myself seen millet grain left to be
eaten by the birds because the cultivator had enough rice and needed
only the stalks of his millet. Efficient production however, as common-
sense and the general interest require, does not neglect by-products but
finds some use for them. Custom may dictate that the surplus should
be squandered on Pwes, Ahlus or cocktail parties; but common sense
and the general interest deprecate useless waste, and require that any
surplus from production should be set aside to swell the general stock

of wealth. In a customary society the wealth is merely hoarded; but
common sense and the general interest require that much of it should
be used as capital for the further increase of production. Now the
characteristic feature of capital is that it responds solely to economic
forces without regard to ethical, aesthetic or any other non-economic
consideration. In a capitalist economy, so far as it is purely capitalist,
free play is given to this tendency of capital; economic relations are
divorced from non-economic considerations, and business is business.
Where the capital is privately owned, it tends to flow in the direction
that the owner of capital, the capitalist, expects to yield the maximum
surplus for his individual advantage or, in other words, towards the
greatest profit. Where production is left to free enterprise, it is
governed, and restricted, by the interest of capitalists. Production may
be stimulated by the hope of gain, but it is restricted through being
subordinated to profit.
In a socialist economy the production of surplus wealth, and the
use of it as capital, is no less important than in a capitalist economy.
Public bodies which undertake business should run their business on
business lines with a view to the maximum production at the minimum
cost consistent with public welfare. In a socialist economy production
is not restricted by considerations of individual profit, it is directed to
the increase of wealth, the national dividend in which
all may share according to their need. With suitable
machinery for the encouragement of production, there
should be a larger output of wealth and a larger surplus available for
use as capital. In practice, of course, this sharp distinction between a
socialist economy and a capitalist economy does not ordinarily exist.
For even where capital is privately owned and free enterprise prevails,
the owners of capital, the capitalists, are not actuated solely by the hope
of gain, even in their business transactions. Still, it remains true that
capital, as such, has no concern for non-economic values. For that
very reason a capitalist economy tends to become a capitalist society, a
society in which not only economic relations but also social relations
are governed solely by economic considerations. In most societies the
tendency for a capitalist economy to become a capitalist society is
checked by common social, national or religious interests, which play
the part of custom in a customary society. But in Burma under foreign

Let us take a hurried glance at Burma before the opening of the
Canal. We are accustomed to think of Rangoon as much the largest
town in Burma and, before the war, a predominantly Indian town. Yet
in 1861 and until 1869 the population of Moulmein was as large as that
of Rangoon, and perhaps larger. And Rangoon was still a Burman
town. According to the annual Administration Report for 1867-68
the last year for which figures by race were given, there were only
16,518 Indians out of a population of about 1 million in the whole
Pegu Division, which then comprised not only Rangoon town and the
district of Rangoon but the whole of Lower Burma except Arakan and
Tenasserim, whereas Arakan and Tenasserim each had twice as many
Indians as a population of less than half a million. Then, as for many
years later, rice and teak were the chief exports; in 1861 the export of
teak was nearly as valuable as the export of rice. It was however the
cultivation of rice on which the great mass of the people depended for
a living, and the conditions of rice cultivation were very different before
and after the opening of the Canal. Before 1869 the cultivators grew
rice primarily for their own family. The cultivation was rather on the
taungya system; they shifted almost annually from one plot to another
within large holdings which had no clearly defined boundaries; some-
times they would migrate to a wholly new settlement, or they might
abandon the land and take to boating, or petty trade, or some other
occupation. The capricious abandonment of land was one of the chief
problems of revenue officers, and the Government adopted various
devices, which I need not mention here, to check it. If the land yielded
more than cultivators required, they might not even trouble to reap the
surplus. But the important fact to note is that they lived throughout the
year on last year's harvest. The steadiest demand was for Upper Burma
and Prome and the adjacent districts came under regular cultivation
before the delta; that is why the holdings in that part of the country
are so much smaller than near Rangoon and Bassein which supplied
the sea-borne trade. The over-sea trade was very irregular but, if
there were a sudden demand, owing to famine in India or China, the
cultivators had a sufficient store of grain from their last harvest to

Merchants bought more or less paddy according to the demand, and
paid cash for what they bought. An interesting contrast is drawn in
one Report between the timber trade and the rice trade; the timber
trade was highly speculative, if not a gamble, but the rice trade was

From about 1857 onwards there was a seasonal immigration of
coolies from Upper Burma. These supplied such agricultural labour
as was needed, though in general there was little hired labour on the
land. Most of them found employment on earthwork for the large
embankments that the Government was building; others worked on
the wharves or in the thousands of hand rice-mills along the river
bank with a perpetual clatter that was regarded as one of the characte-
ristic noises of Rangoon. None of these occupations had yet passed
into Indian hands. Other important occupations were salt-boiling and
fish-curing these had not yet been killed by imports. Both crime and
civil litigation remained at about the same level, and most petty disputes,
civil or criminal, were settled by arbitration according to custom before
local elders. With the growth of population there was an increase in
the area under cultivation, and therefore in the volume of production.
But the population increased more rapidly than the area under cultiva-
tion, and there was no steady growth of exports, which fluctuated
violently according to the foreign demand for rice. Cultivators worked
land, as they had always done, primarily for their own families; there
were no brokers to buy their rice and no Indian money-lenders to
tempt them into debt and drag them into court. They hardly knew
that they were under foreign rule. For in Pegu there were only four
deputy commissioners where now there are thirteen, and these few
officials could not visit the villages for lack of railways, stcam-launches
and roads; they could only get about in country boats dependent on
the tides. In the villages life went on in accordance with established
custom very much as before. Burma was still a customary society.
All this was changed with the opening of the Suez Canal. One of
the earliest consequences of the Canal was a stimulus to the import of
machinery: The first steam rice-mill was put up in 1861; presumably
it was unprofitable, for no more were erected until 1867. In 1869
there were still only three mills and the bulk of the crop was hand-
milled. By 1872, however, there were 26. A rice-mill might cost as

who had invested so much money in a mill could not afford to keep it
idle. He had to make certain of his supply of paddy, and he sent
brokers into the districts to buy it. Formerly cultivators had been
uncertain of finding a buyer for their crop; now they could sell all they
could grow, and the more land they could secure the more rice they
could grow. There was no longer any problem of the capricious
abandonment of land; on the contrary there was a problem of how to
deal with land-grabbers, cultivators who got hold of more land than
they could bring under cultivation and speculators who took up land
which they could let to tenants or hold on to for selling at a higher
price. Formerly the extension of cultivation barely kept pace with the
growth of population; now, it went ahead with giant strides. Between 1869
and 1886 the population grew from about 2.47 million to 4.16 million, by
not quite 70 per cent, but the area under rice grew from about 1.67 million
acres to 3.81 million, about 130 per cent, nearly twice as fast, and the area
shown as occupied from about 1.94 million to 4.48 million about 180 per
cent, almost three times as fast. The figures in the reports are only very
rough approximations but they are sufficiently accurate for a comparison
illustrating the rush to take up land. The only way for a cultivator to
make good his claim to land was to bring it under cultivation as
rapidly as possible with hired labour. If his stock of paddy from last
year's harvest did not suffice to pay the wages he could take advances
from the broker on the security of the crop. Formerly the thrifty
cutivator stored enough paddy to see him through the year; now thrift
seemed folly, and it paid better to borrow money to get a larger crop.
The cultivator began to live on next year's harvest instead of on the
last. It seemed still more profitable to take up land that he would not
be able to bring under cultivation for some years. Here, again, matters
were made easy for him. Rangoon money-lenders, chettyars from
Madras, began to send out agents to provide the funds, and cultivators
took to living not merely on the next harvest but on the future value
'of their land. Not infrequently the land was taken over by the money
lender before it had all been cleared. So far back as the early Eighties
the Government became apprehensive lest the peasants should lose
their land but, after making some enquiries, decided, father prematurely,
that there was no danger of the land passing into the hands of foreign
landlords. What in fact was happening was that, as soon as land was
forfeited to a money-lender, he sold it to another cultivator with a
mortgage on it for part of the purchase price. On this system the land

So much for agriculture. Let us turn to industry, commerce and
the towns. The steam rice mills in the ports deprived the Burman
hand-millers of employment. The millers did not employ Burmans as
they could get cheaper and more docile labour from India. They could
not get as many Indians as they wanted, until the Government subsidized
the import of Indian coolies. After three years a subsidy was no longer
needed, because it had given the requisite impulse to the annual seasonal
influx of Indian immigrants which remained a characteristic feature of
the economy of Burma until the depression of 1930. (It is of some
interest to note that, as the steam mills improved, they put the rice-
millers in England out of business; there was one law for the English
capitalist and for the Burman coolie.) In Burma, Indian coolies dis-
placed Burman labour not only in the milling industry but in the
stevedoring business and in earthwork and manual labour generally;
when the railway was built the labourers were not Burmans but Indians
imported to relieve famine in Bengal, again, Burman economic activities
had to face the competitions not only of foreign immigrants but of
foreign goods. Formerly salt and salt fish and ngapi from Lower
Burma sufficed not only for local wants but were exported to Upper
Burma and even to western China. These were replaced by salt from
Cheshire, and by salt fish and ngapi from Singapore to the prejudice
of the salt-boilers and fish-curers. The Burmese kings had discouraged
foreign trade; consequently Burmans did not know how or where to
sell goods abroad or how or where to buy goods. (That is still one
of our difficulties.) Consequently the whole export and import trade
were handled by foreigners. And, because they imported foreign

As a result, then, of the opening of the Canal, the economic
organisation of Burma took on its modern features; what about the
social organisation? In the trade statistics there is another suggestive
feature. In 1864-65 there was a large but temporary rise in the exports
of rice to meet a famine in China. This put much more money than
ever before into the pockets of the cultivators. But they had not yet
learned to spend money. There was a large import of treasure but not
of goods, and they hoarded their gains in the form of silver or jewellery.
The same thing happened in 1874, when there was a sudden large
increase of exports to meet a famine in India. By this time however
the Suez Canal was beginning to facilitate the import of consumption
goods, petty luxuries, and from about 1875 the import of such goods
kept pace with the rise of exports. The new conditions encouraged
Burmans to spend money; they encouraged thriftlessness. Also,
because Burmans had more money than they knew how to use prudently,
the annual reports tell of the rapid growth of gambling. Incidentally,
it was not only among Burmans that the new conditions encouraged
recklessness. The import trade was very profitable, and the importers,
in a hurry to get rich, allowed easy credit, especially to Chinese
merchants of little substance. In 1884 there was a slump; many Chinese
firms could not pay their bills and some of the import firms went
bankrupt. This was a lesson in prudence to the importers and for
some time afterwards they sold only for cash. But there was not the
same lesson in prudence to Burman cultivators, because every year
new immigrants flocked down from Upper Burma, and they could
always obtain loans on the security of their crops and land.

western law. The ordinary villager, however, preferred arbitration
before local elders to proceedings in a western court. But, when Indian
money lenders wanted to recover their dues, they went to a western
court for western law instead of trusting to arbitration according to
Burmese custom. This undermined the authority of the circle head-men
and local elders; also it cut at the root of Burman ideas regarding
property in land, and in both ways it loosened the ties of social order.
Between 1875 and 1885 the number of civil suits increased by 50 per
cent, though most village disputes were still settled by local elders.
Even more illuminating are the figures relating to the growth of
crime. Before the opening of the Canal the increase in the number of
crimes reported was slower than the increase in the population; in
1869 the number of offences reported was just over 20,000 and by
1872 the number had dropped to 17,000. Then the dislocation of
the agricultural economy consequent on the rush for land began to be
felt, and by 1878 the number of offences reported had risen to 27,000.
It was however from about 1880 that the increase of crime was most
striking. In 1882 about 35,000 offences were reported and 41,000
persons brought to trial. By 1885 the number of offences had risen to
49,000 and over 67,000 persons were brought to trial. It can hardly
be without significance that this rapid increase of crime coincided with
the import of Indian coolies. driving Burmans out of employment in
the towns. It was suggested in the annual report of 1879 that the
national character of Burmans was changing but the change would seem
to have been rather in the economic and social conditions. The figures
for dacoity are of special interest. For some years after the occupation
of Pegu dacoity flourished, but this was recognized as in part a

The disorganisation of society, of which the growth of litigation
and crime were symptoms, was merely one aspect of a new type of
social organisation. Industry and commerce were in foreign hands,
and the towns were largely foreign settlements, while Burmans were
restricted to agriculture in the villages. The foreign element comprised
Europeans, Indians and Chinese. Each of these major components was
further subdivided. The Indians included both Hindus and Muslims;
the European group was largely Eurasian; and among the Chinese
there were three or four distinct sub-groups. In each group and sub-
group there were numerous subdivisions with their own particular
economic functions. The various groups and sub-groups had little or
nothing in common in their social life, but they all had in common
the desire to make more money. By 1886 Burma was transformed
into what is now often termed a plural society, with a mixture
of peoples living side by side, but separately, and meeting only
in the market place. For lack of any common social tie such a society
is dominated by economic forces; it is essentially a capitalist society.
By 1886 there had already emerged in Burma the type of social organi-
sation which it retained up to the Japanese invasion.

feature of Burmese social tradition. In 1864 the authorities in

In these remarks I have touched on only a few aspects of the
transformation of British Burma between 1861 and 1885 as a result of
the opening of the Suez Canal. In the documents from which I have
been quoting there is a vast mass of other interesting and instructive
information. It was during this period that the whole machinery of
administration took on its modern character. It is hardly too much to
say that everything that happened after 1885 up to the introduction of
constitutional reforms in 1923, or even up to the time of the Japanese
invasion, was merely a development of conditions already in existence
in 1885. It was during this period that the system of land revenue
assumed the characteristic features which distinguished it from land
revenue in India generally; the system which originated in the days when
taungya cultivation prevailed in the rice plains of the delta
subsequently applied, though quite unsuitable, to Upper Burma. During
the same period forest administration took on its later features, and
the working of the forests passed from Burmans and Karens to foreign
firms. Also it was then that there were the first experiments in so-called.
self-government in municipalities, which throw much light on the deve-
lopment of local government after 1923, and are still instructive with
reference to local administration at the present day. One main
object of local government was to promote local welfare. But when

Some of these sidelights on a social condition may seem trivial.
But it is not always immediately apparent what is significant and what
is insignificant, You will remember that Fleming discovered penicillin
by looking into something that many observers would have dismissed
as unimportant. There is very little that the research worker in social
and economic history can safely disregard as unimportant. His function
is to ascertain the facts, to find out with regard to some particular
subject at some particular time, exactly what did happen. Then one can
go on to examine the causes and the consequences. Research may
have fruitful results, and it may legitimately be directed towards
producing such results. But the research worker must not be biassed by
the possible results: his aim should be to ascertain the facts
regardless of the consequences. Research conducted in that spirit can
do much to provide a solid foundation for applied political science.
Even the foregoing very superficial survey of certain aspects of the
social evolution of British Burma between 1861 and 1885 is, I suggest,
instructive. It reveals this period as the most critical stage in the
history of modern Burma during which modern Burma first came into
being. By 1885 the social and economic structure of Burma had
already assumed the features that remained its distinctive character up
to the time of the Japanese invasion; notably, social disintegration,
the loss of land by the peasantry and their consequent impoverishment,
and the growth of crime. Conditions so deep-rooted in history cannot
be transformed by remedies that do not go down to the roots. One
learns also from these records that many of the tendencies and conse-
quences were already recognized and deplored by the Government. It
tried to correct them and sometimes claimed success. "Dacoity was
on the verge of extinction"; there was "no danger that the land would
pass to Indian money-lender " But attempts to control the course of
affairs were unavailing. All this, you may say, is ancient history. Now
