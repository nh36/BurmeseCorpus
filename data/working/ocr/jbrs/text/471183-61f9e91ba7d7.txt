PETROGRAPHY AND ORIGIN OF TAUNGTHA FORMATION
EXPOSED AT TAUNGTHA, MYINGYAN DISTRICT
Dev D. Sarin*
ABSTRACT
The petrography of the sedimentary rocks exposed in the neigh-
bourhood of Taungtha, Myingyan District, is described. Arkoses, which
constitute an important part of the Taungtha Formation, are considered to
have been derived from a belt of plutonic rocks. It appears that these
sediments were deposited in deltaic or estuarine environment; that this
environment was oxidising is indicated by the presence of red pigment.
INTRODUCTION
Taungtha hills form a prominent physiographic 'feature in the neighbourhood of
Myingyan. This landmark, which can be seen from miles, is inferior only to Mount Popa
in Central Burma. The range of hills is about six miles long and extends in NNW-SSE
direction.
According to Cotter (1908), who has described the structure of Taungtha hills in
detail, the main Taungtha hill (E1. 1,788 feet) is a flat-topped elliptical dome. He
visualises a similar structure in the northern end of the Taungtha hills. Intervening hills
according to him are highly complicated structurally, due to folding and faulting.

The aim of this study is to describe the petrography of the sedimentary rocks
exposed in the Taungtha hills. For this purpose two sections were measured: one along
the main Taungtha hill (El. 1,788 feet) while proceeding from the village to the axis of the
anticline across the strike of the rocks; second along the Taungtha-Myingyan road where
the road cuts across the ridge.
The first section was unsuitable for detailed petrographic studies because of repea-
ted zones of cover, which separate the outcrops. The second section, though not complete,
was devoid of much cover, and so more suitable for detailed petrographic studies, because
continuous sampling was possible.
Methods of Study
Both sections were measured following the standard stratigraphic procedures
suggested by Krumbein and Sloss (1951). Results have been presented in the form of a
stratigraphic column in Fig. 2.
Samples of sandstones, siltstones and shales were collected at regular intervals of
10-15 feet along the sections measured. Due to lack of facilities for the mineralogic study
of clay-sized minerals only a few shale samples were collected.
Thin sections of sandstones and siltstones were made for petrographic studies.
Megascopic specimens were studied with the aid of binocular microscope. To obtain
heavy mineral grains and mechanical composition, sandstones and siltstones were dis-
aggregated by immersion in dilute HC1. Where this method of disaggregation failed, the
specimens were subjected to crushing in the mortar. The sands thus obtained were dried
and sieved for a period of ten minutes, using B.S. sieves. From the sand fraction held in
B.S. sieve No. 120 (Sieve opening 0.124 mm), heavy mineral grains were obtained, using
the methods suggested by Krumbein and Pettijohn (1938), and mounted following the
techniques suggested by Sarin (1960).
Roundness was obtained from thin sections of the rocks by visual estimation
following the techniques suggested by Krumbein (1941).
Percentages of detrital grains were obtained from the thin sections by 500-grain
counts (at least) with the aid of a mechanical stage. Percentages of heavy minerals were
similarly obtained by 300-grain counts.

According to standard American usage each formation name is composed of two
parts. First part is the name of locality at or near which the formation is typically
exposed; the second part describes the chief lithologic type constituting the formation; if
no lithologic type is dominant, then the word formation is used.
The sediments of Taungtha hills which form the subject matter of this paper are
interbedded sandstones, siltstones and shales. Since no rock type was considered as
dominant, these sediments have been assigned the name Taungtha Formation.
Red shales (unit 7 of Taungtha hill section) have been designated as RED SHALE
MEMBER of the Taungtha Formation; while the sandstone-shale sequence underlying it
with sand/shale ratio of 7:2, has been designated as LOWER SANDSTONE-SHALE
MEMBER of the Taungtha Formation. The sandstone-shale sequence overlying the Red
Shale member with a sand/shale ratio of 2:5, has been designated as UPPER SANDSTONE-
SHALE MEMBER of the Taungtha Formation.
Division of the Taungtha Formation into three members was necessitated by the
presence of Red Beds (Red shales of Red Shale member). These red beds indicate a particu-
lar sedimentary environment which does not seem to have prevailed either before or after
their deposition, for sufficient period. No doubt, thin red beds appear both in the Lower
and Upper sandstone-shale members of the Taungtha Formation but these beds constitute
a negligible percentage of these members and seem to indicate a periodic and brief return
of the said environment.
Field Description
The Taungtha Formation as aforementioned is composed of interbedded sandstones,
siltstones and shales. Distinction between siltstones and shales is arbitrary and not
necessarily accurate, because it is not always possible to distinguish between the two in
the field as most shales are mixtures of clastics of silt and clay size. For the calculation
of sand/shale ratios, siltstones have been grouped with shales.
Cotter (1908, p. 152) describes the presence of a red conglomeratic band close to
the southwest base of the Taungtha hill. The author was not able to locate this outcrop,
even after careful search. It may be that this outcrop is now under cover and cannot
be seen.
Sandstones tend to occur in massive beds which on an average have a thickness of
one foot.
They show variation in colour from brick-red to greyish-brown, with all inter-
vening shades present. The red sandstones are associated with the red beds. Their
colour is seen to diminish gradually as we pass upward in the section from the red beds.
Commonly the sandstones are light brown to brownish-grey in colour, with "salt and
pepper" appearance. Their grain size varies from medium to very fine sands. Concre-

General Remarks:
As it was not possible to trace the beds laterally from the Taungtha hill section to
the Taungtha-Myingyan road section or vice-versa, due to structural complications, the
two sections have been correlated on the basis of their field, petrographic, and heavy
mineral characteristics. The unit 15 of the Taungtha-Myingyan road section has been
correlated with the lower part of the unit 5 of the Taungtha hill section.

The lower limit of the Taungtha formation may be unit 15 of the Taungtha hill
section. On the hill itself this limit may be recognized along the crest of the asymme-
trical anticline. The upper limit of the Taungtha formation is above unit 2 of the
Taungtha hill section. The lower reaches of the hill are composed of “Pliocene" sediments
according to Cotter (1908). They dip West, in the same direction as does the Taungtha
formation, but whereas the latter dips steeply (50-75°) the former dips moderately: 30º on
an average. This discordance was apparently not noted by Cotter (1908). The contact
between the two is under cover (unit 1 of Taungtha hill section) and as such the contact
has been arbitrarily fixed in between the last “Pliocene" outcrop and the first Taungtha
Formation outcrop.
Thickness:
The total thickness of Taungtha formation as measured at Taungtha hill section is
1030 (plus-minus 100) feet.
Grain Size Distribution
All sandstones were disaggregated using a rubber cork and porcelain mortar, and
sieved with Ro-Tap machine for 10 minutes. Sieves used were B.S. sieves with the
following openings in microns: 2057, 1093, 500, 251, 124, and 64. After separation,
individual fractions were weighed to 0.01 grams. Each fraction was then examined under
a binocular microscope for determining the percentage of aggregates, if any, which was
then deducted from the weight of the sieve fraction. Cumulative percentages were plotted
on semi-logarithmic paper and thus a cumulative curve was obtained for each sample
of the sand.
Critical values obtained from these curves were 10, 25, 50, 75, and 90 percentiles.

Sedimentary Structures
Several sedimentary structures were noted in the Taungtha Formation, besides
normal stratification. They are ripple marks, cross-stratification and intra-stratal contor-
tions.
Ripple Marks:
The
Current and interference ripples were observed in the Taungtha Formation.
latter type was observed only in one instance; on the eastern flank of the Taungtha ridge,
in the section exposed along Taungtha-Myingyan road. Current ripples, however, are
more common, being more common in the Upper sandstone-shale member of the
Taungtha Formation.
Internal structure of the current ripples, which is exposed in Section 2, was studied
in detail because of its relationship with cross-stratification. In many cases, it was noted
that the current ripples were internally cross-stratified. These cross-laminated ripples,
where preserved, show how cross-lamination may be developed, due to migration of fine
sand and silt-sized sediments over the gentle up-current side of the ripple and deposition
on the down-current side. Fig. 3 diagramatically explains this relationship between
cross-lamination and ripple marks.

Cross Stratification:
Planar type of cross-stratification was found in the Taungtha Formation. It is
much more evident in the Upper sandstone-shale member of the Formation than in the
Lower member. It was not noted in the Red shale member. The cross-stratified units
varied in their thickness from 12 to 18 inches. The cross stratified strata varied in their
thickness from less than one mm to two cms. When such strata are less than one cm
thick, they are distinguished as laminae and the cross-stratification is distinguished as
cross-lamination; the cross-stratification is recognized as cross-bedding when the cross-
stratified strata are more than one cm thick.
Though the orientation of cross-stratified layers is an important directional struc-
ture to determine the regional current pattern, no such attempt has been made although
data was collected. This study is much too localised and any attempt to determine
regional current pattern from this data may be misleading. As such no directional
information is presented.
Intra-Stratal Contortions:
Rich (1950) during his studies in Wales, noticed intricate foldings and crumplings
within siltstone beds; this structure was named intra-stratal contortions by him. This
folding did not affect the beds above or below, nor did it affect the smoothness of the
upper and lower bedding planes. There was no evidence to indicate that the siltstone
beds, showing this structure, were eroded before the deposition of the next succeeding bed.
Rich (1950, p. 729) states, "This proves conclusively that the crumpling took place within
the silt bed after the beds above had been laid down, and is not a case of crumpling of a
bed at the surface of the sea bottom followed by erosion of the crumpled irregularities
before the next bed was deposited as has been the interpretation in connection with some-
what similar interstratal flowage phenomena." He suggested that these contortions
resulted because of gliding of the mass of sediments, overlying the siltstone bed in ques-
tion, while the latter bed was in the condition of quicksand, being full of water.
Similar structures in siltstone beds of the Taungtha Formation were observed, both
in the Taungtha hill section and the Taungtha-Myingyan road section. In section the
intrastratal contortions range from gentle corrugations to folded patterns. (Fig. 4) The
deformed zone includes most of the top part of the bed. Beds showing this structure
were less than four inches thick. This structure was noticed only in siltstone or fine
sandstone beds.

as such petrographically. The author failed to observe any phyllites. If, as has been
suggested by Cotter (1908), these contortions are due to Post-Pliocene pressures, then why
were these contortions confined to certain beds only. These contortions are due to soft-
sediment deformation and not to post-depositional earth movements.
Cone-in-Cone Structure:
Present in the stream, which flows parallel to the Taungtha-Myingyan road where
it cuts across the Taungtha ridge, were shale boulders, apparently of local derivation,
showing cone-in-cone structure. Since it was not possible to trace the source bed, it is
assumed that it must be under the cover.

These sandstones, which are well sorted as is indicated by their coefficient sorting
(quartile deviation), are composed of a detrital framework of sand-sized grains held
together by a chemical cement or matrix. The framework, which is grain
supported and forms 60 to 66 percent of the rock, is composed of quartz,
feldspars, micas, rock fragments and heavy minerals. Majority of the grains vary
in their long diameter: from 0.4 to 0.7 mms; shale pebbles occasionally seen
in some sandstones may be as long as 3.5 cms. These pebbles are rare and
when present form only an insignificant part of the rock (less than five per cent).
Many detrital
shapes have been modified by corrosion by carbonate cement,
which has corroded quartz and feldspar grains alike. Most grains are sub-angular to
sub-rounded and their rounding index, determined by visual estimation, varies from *3 to ‘5.
Grain contact are always tangential and there is no evidence of pressure solution along
grain contacts.
Quartz comprises 50 to 60 percent of the framework. It's grain diameter rarely
exceeds 0.8 mm and average between 0.3 and 0.5 mm. Most prominent is normal igneous
quartz which is free of strain shadows and shows no signs of elongation. Mineral inclu-
sions in these grains are very rare; zircon was the only mineral inclusion noted. Gaseous
and fluid inclusions, arranged in planes, were observed in 10 percent of the igneous quartz
grains. Metamorphic quartzes, characterized by strain shadows, notable elongation and
mineral inclusions, form less than five percent of the quartz fraction. Vein quartz grains,
characterized by composite extinction, form less than one percent of the quartz fraction.
Feldspars, which constitute 21 to 36 percent of the framework, include orthoclase,
sodic plagioclase and microcline. Orthoclase feldspars are most dominant and constitute
over 60 percent of the total feldspar content. Microcline feldspars are rare. The rest is
composed of soda-rich plagioclases. Most feldspars are slightly altered, but about
one-fifth of the grains show more extensive alteration. Inclusions of muscovite are
common in orthoclase feldspars. Compared with quartz, fluid and gaseous inclusions are
more common in orthoclase feldspars.
Muscovite is quite prominent in these sandstones and composes one to eight
percent of the framework. It occurs as long shreds and plates; many of which are bent
or broken around other detrital grains. Biotite is not common, and when present is
usually altered to chlorite. All micas are characterized by their alignment parallel to
bedding.
Rock fragments which comprise six to twenty two percent of the framework, are
composed of shale, slate, phyllite and chert grains. Volcanic rock fragments were noted
in only two slides. Most abundant are shale fragments and constitute more than 60
percent of the total rock fragment content. The average size of the rock fragment is less
than that of quartz and feldspar grains. They are further characterized by being better
rounded, except where they have been squashed between resistant grains.

Tabular pebbles of shale are present in a few beds of sandstone and occur as

Heavy minerals form less than three percent of the framework. Garnet, tourma-
line, zircon, micas, and opaques are most commonly occuring heavy grains. They are
discussed in a separate section below.
Mineralogically siltstones are similar to sandstones. Texturally they differ in being
composed of more angular fragments. They, usually have a matrix of clay-sized grains
intermixed with haematite, in which silt-sized grains are scattered. Carbonate cement is
usually absent or occurs in a very small quantity, probably due to their lower permeability.
Cement:
The Taungtha sandstones have been cemented by haematite, calcite or by both
together, in varying proportions.
Haematite:
It is a common binding agent in some sandstones, particularly those that are
associated with red beds. It occurs as a fine grained matrix which partly or completely
fills the available pore space. It is also present as coatings around the detrital grains.
Calcite is often associated as a cement in these beds. Where both calcite and haematite
are present, the latter is found to be around the detrital grains, while the former occupies
the remaining pore space. The fact that haematite is present as coatings around the
detrital grains indicates that it was precipitated contemporaneous with sedimentation of
the detrital grains.
Calcite:
It is the more common cementing agent in Taungtha sandstones and occurs in
original pore space and also as a replacement of detrital grains and primary haematite
matrix. It occurs as anhedral interlocking crystals 0.05 to 0.1 mm in diameter and most
pore spaces are filled by one to three crystals of calcite.
Calcite is undeformed, yet is seen around bent micas and fractured rock fragments
and feldspars, which indicates that the calcite cementation took place after initial compac-
tion. A similar situation may also develop due to recrystallisation of calcite from a
primary calcite matrix. Evidence of this is lacking and the former sequence is more
probable.
The textural relationship of haematite and calcite also indicates that the latter is
secondary. In sandstones in which both occur as cementing agents it appears that calcite
was introduced later, because (a) calcite occupies the middle part of the pore space,
(b) inclusions of haematite can be seen in calcite, and (c) haematite is found to be around

Nomenclature of the Sandstones:
The average Taungtha Formation sandstone may be classified as an ARKOSE,
after Pettijohn's (1957) classification, because quartz constitutes less than 75 percent and
feldspars constitute more than 25 percent of the detrital framework and are in excess of
rock fragments. In two cases sandstones may be classified as SUBGRAYWACKE,
because while the quartz content is same, percentage of rock fragments exceeds that of
feldspars.

Weight of heavy mineral grains obtained from sand fraction finer than 0.25 mm and

Amphibole:

They constitute 46 to 71 percent of the heavy fraction. They all show same shade
of pink and occur as sub-angular equant grains. Surface etching is a common feature in
these grains; more pronounced in some than in others.

General Discussion
Depositional Environment:
Good sorting, ripple marking and cross-stratification indicate that the great bulk of
the Taungtha Formation was deposited in shallow waters above wave base. That the
environment was oxidising is suggested by the presence of haematite as red pigment in
these sediments, though in varying quantities.

While the Red shale member of the Taungtha Formation is wholly composed of
red beds, the Lower and Upper sandstone-shale members are characterized by less abun-
dance of haematite. In the Lower and Upper sandstone-shale members, in which only a
few red bed sequences are present, sandstones have varying quantity of haematite present
as matrix between detrital grains. As indicated earlier, petrographic evidence suggests
that some of the original haematite, which occured as detrital matrix in these sandstones,
has been replaced by calcite cement, though the amount thus lost cannot be determined.
Some of the shales of the Upper and Lower sandstone-shale members are characterized by
complete lack of iron pigment. It appears that no iron pigment was deposited with these
sediments. From the characteristics described above it may be concluded that (a) during
the deposition of the Taungtha Formation haematite was being deposited with the sedi-
ments, though there were brief periods when no such pigment was deposited and (b) there
has been some loss of haematite after deposition due to replacement. This indicates that
the Taungtha Formation was deposited in an oxidising environment. The variation in
the haematite content does not indicate a reducing environment, for if such an environ-
ment has existed then the sediments would have been coloured greyish due to the presence
of reduced iron oxides. The fact that we have shales which do not bear any iron pigment
indicates that no iron pigment had come from the source area, The variability in
haematite content within the formation may be due to (a) lack of supply from the source
area and (b) loss due to replacement.
Palaeoclimate:
Discussing the origin of red beds, Van Houten (1948) concluded that the red
pigment was derived from the red soils developed in warm humid regions. Once such
soils were formed they will colour all the sediments derived from such a region, and this
colouration will be preserved, provided the pigment is not reduced from ferric to ferrous
iron during deposition. He also suggested that since the red pigment is fine grained, it
would be concentrated in fine grained sediments.
Primary haematite, present as pigment in the Taungtha Formation, seems to have
been derived from the upland soils formed in warm humid regions, as has been suggested
by Van Houten (1948) and earlier suggested by Tomlinson (1916) and Raymond (1942).
Raymond (1942) had also suggested that the red beds were deposited subsequent to rapid
uplift and erosion of red regolith bearing regions. This view is supported by the presence
of sands of arkosic composition in the Taungtha Formation. According to Krynine
(1935) arkoses are derived by rapid erosion of newly uplifted regions.
Taungtha Formation Marine or Non-Marine?
Cotter (1908) implies that the sediments exposed at Taungtha are marine. His
assumption is based on (a) general aspect of the rocks, (b) presence of selenite and (c)
occurence of marine fossils. As far as fossils are concerned the author has failed to
locate any.
It is apparent from Cotter's (1908) paper that the fossil evidence is very
fragmentry. Selenite is present in very small quantity; but more common is efflorescence.
Sarin (paper in press) has described similar efflorescence, common in Chauk and Upper
Burma, particularly in dry stream beds, whenever Peguan rocks are exposed.

Following criteria have been listed by Dunbar and Rodgers (1957) for distinguishing
marine and non-marine deposits: (a) fossils, (b) red colour, (c) clay galls, (d) mud-cracks,
(e) channel sands, (f) cross-stratification, (g) size-sorting, and (h) clay mineral composition.
Lack of fossils has made it difficult to use this excellent criterion. Dunbar and
Rodgers (1957) have, however, advised against using this criterion where fossil evidence is
scanty. Even though Cotter (1908) has listed a few genera he admits that they are badly
preserved or broken.
Red beds are usually unfossiliferrous and this explains the lack of fossils in the
Taungtha Formation. Red colour has been noted in marine and non-marine deposits,
though more common in the latter.
Dunbar and Rodgers (1957) consider clay galls as an excellent criterion for non-
marine deposits. This is the only conclusive evidence obtained from the Taungtha Form-
ation that suggests a non-marine origin.
Data obtained from cross-stratification and size-sorting of the Taungtha sediments
do not yield conclusive evidence for or against marine or non-marine deposition. Mud
cracks were not noted and so cannot be used as a criterion. As there is no evidence to
indicate that the Taungtha Formation is a channel deposit, this useful criterion is also
non-applicable. Clay mineral composition could not be used as a criterion because of the
lack of facilities for studying clay mineralogy.
In summary it may be stated that the sedimentary criteria suggest a non-marine
origin, though not conclusively. Folk (1960) assigned continental origin to Juniata
Formation because of their red colour, consistent cross-stratification and lack of fossils.
Clay galls, being composed of soft sediments, cannot be preserved in high energy
environment where they would be destroyed. Their presence indicates sluggish currents.
That the currents did operate is indicated by separation of mud from sand fraction,
cross-stratification, ripple marking and concentration of iron pigment in clay-sized
sediments. Lamination, indicating lack of current action, has also been observed, though
in a very few beds. The above characteristics of the Taungtha Formation may be explained
by assuming an environment which is seemingly on the borderline between a continental
and marine environment; possibly deltaic or estuarine. Rapid deposition within the basin
would explain the presence of shales and the preservation of the red pigment.
Source Rocks:
Sandstones of the Taungtha Formation are characterized by the following: (a) Lack
of rounding is displayed by detrital grains. (b) Compositional immaturity is displayed by.
high feldspar and rock-fragment content. (c) Quartz is predominantly normal igneous
quartz. (d) With the exception of garnet, which suggests its derivation from a high-rank
metamorphic rocks, all other heavy minerals indicate acid igneous source rocks.

From the above it appears that the source rocks of the Taungtha Formation were

been attributed to high relief by Krynine (1935), a suggestion with which most students of
arkoses agree.
The mineralogy of the sandstones does not indicate any major changes in the source
areas or within the source area, as the mineral composition remains quite uniform.

Such a source area in Burma could be (a) Mogok area or (b) belt of plutonics
along the western edge of the Shan Plateau associated with which are metamorphics.
Age of the Taungtha Formation:
,,
Discussing the age of the Taungtha sedimentary rocks, Cotter (1908) states: "The
general aspect of the rocks, the presence of selenite, and the occurence of marine fossils
indicate the rock to be of Upper Miocene age. Although the evidence is somewhat
meagre, I think it probable that the Lower Miocene is not exposed here. Selenite, which is
found in some of the lowest beds, is characteristic of the Upper Miocene at Yenangyaung.”
It appears the evidence to suggest that the Taungtha Formation is Upper Miocene in age
is not very convincing. The above-mentioned characters, excepting fossils, only reflect
the conditions within the sedimentary basin, and in as much as these conditions in diffe-
rent parts of the basin appear at different times, they mislead stratigraphers who fail to
recognize that the time span represented by a formation at one place has no necessary
relationship to that represented at another.
It is apparent that these sediments do not belong to the Irrawaddy system because
of lack of (a) fossil wood, (b) gravelly beds and (c) large scale cross-bedding. In their
heavy mineral content also these sediments are different from Irrawaddians of the neigh-
bouring area. As these sediments appear to be deltaic or estuarine and are similar, in
mineral composition and textures, to the Upper Peguan sediments of Chauk, they may
be of Miocene age. There is, however, no evidence to indicate whether the Taungtha
Formation is Lower, Middle, or Upper Miocene in age.
Sarin (paper in press), referring to such situations, had pointed out that lack of
fossil evidence in most localities, absence of recognized key beds and north-south facies
change, create difficulties for stratigraphers in the Tertiary basin of Burma. It may be
possible to distinguish Peguan from Irrawaddian sediments, but it certainly is extremely
difficult to distinguish the horizon of the Peguan or Irrawaddian, which is exposed. Most
of the literature on Burmese Tertiary geology is based on reconaisance studies and there
is no published record of any detailed stratigraphic studies. Without such studies and
subdivisions of rocks into recognizable lithologic units, it will not be possible to correlate
the strata exposed at any one place with strata elsewhere. With this aim a complete
petrographic account of the Taungtha Formation has been given. The author
looks forward to detailed stratigraphic work of this nature being undertaken in Burma,
which alone will enable the stratigraphers to reconstruct the true history of the Burmese
Tertiary geosyncline.
