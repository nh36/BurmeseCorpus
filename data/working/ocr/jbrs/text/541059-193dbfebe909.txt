BURMESE POETRY (1300–1971 ) *
Dr. Hla Pe **
1. Its birth, development, scope and nature
I shall deal with Burmese poetry
under two heads: classical and modern.
It exists on stone; on palm leaf and on
printed paper. In time it spans an era
of nearly seven hundred years; in scope
it comprises over fifty genres; and it has
its own tradition and values. To attempt
to cover this vast ground within the
allotted time would be like trying to
force a kilo-litre of wine into a litre
bottle.
A satisfactory solution to this for-
midable problem is to present you with
a wide spectrum of Burmese poetry,
high-lighting its essential features, and
characteristics. My tentative approach is
to allocate the following component parts
to each of the three lectures. (1) a brief
survey of the significant poctical works
in the context of various agencies that
have moulded poetry into its present
pattern and determined its course; (2)
the form and content of each individual
genre; and finally (3) a few examples to
re-inforce the fundamental points made
in the two previous lectures.
1. Classical poetry, 1300-1885
1.1 Contributory factors
Four major contributory factors
shared the responsibility for the birth,
development, scope and nature of the
classical poetry. These are Buddhism,
Burmese monarchy, poets' reactions to
their environment and contact with neigh-
bouring cultures.

1.1.1 Buddhism
Theravada (The Teaching of the Elders)
or Conservative School of Buddhism was
introduced to Burma probably in the 11th
century A.D. This has already to its
credit wherever it has been established,
the disemination of education not only
in the religious language, Pali, but also
in the language of the country. The prime
object of the education is to inculcate
an accurate knowledge of the Religion
and to preserve its purity. The moral
preceptors and intellectual leaders were
the members of the Order of the monks,
who availed themselves of the vast corpus
of the Buddhist Scriptures.
1.1.2 Burmese monarchy
The king of Burma, often a despotic
monarch, was the supporter of the Faith.
The Church welcomed his support and
he would not have maintained his posi-
tion in the country except as the pro-
fessed Buddhist. His authority at least
in theory, was all-pervasive and his
recognition was sought in all secular
matters; and his action or lack of it
often decided the fae of the country.
The Court together with the Capital city
was naturally the hive of culture around
which swarmed the li erary bees. Many
such intellectual centres grew up in the
four principal periods of the Burmese
monarchy: (1) the Pagan period, 1044-
1364, embracing also the two minor periods
of Sagaing and Pin-ya; (2) the Ava
period, 1364-1555; (3) the Toungoo period,

1.1.3 Poets' reactions to their environ-
ment
The reactions of the poets to their
environment varied according to the
climate of religious devotion, political
conditions, intellectual activities and
human relationships. Their creative talents
were, however, circumscribed by their
faith in Buddhism and loyalty to the
king, and their mode of education.
Occasionally their imaginative faculties
were let loose by their spirit of adventure
or the impact of another culture.
1.1.4 Cultural contacts
Burma has had cultural contacts with
India and Ceylon since before the 11th
century A.D., with Thailand from the
16th century and finally with the West
in the 19th century. Besides, since the
the Burmese came to Burma sometime in
the 9th century, they have been in close
touch with the Mons in every sphere of
life.
All these factors and others are
reflected in the poetry composed in the
four periods referred to.
1.2 Pagan period, 1014-1364
This was the age of Temple Builders.
Members of the royalty, and courtiers and
their families carried out in durable form
the religious offerings which together with
observing moral precepts and meditation
are the three primary duties of every
lay-man. They built religious edifices to
which they dedicated paddy-fields and
orchards, and slaves and domestic animals.
The records of their works of merit were
put on stone for posterity soon after
Burmese was reduced to writing sometime
in the 11th century, borrowing letters of
the Alphabet from the Mons who had
been in Burma long before the Burmans.

in all, were either Shans or descended
from Shans. Their suzerainty held over
only Upper Burma in name: to the east
were the Shan Saw-bwas (or chiefs) in
separate States, to the south, Lower
Burma was under the Mon kings; and to
the west, the independent state of Arakan.
In this new centre there appeared a
new stream of ‘book-literature' on palm-
leaf scratched with a stylus. This phase
of book literature, originating under the
auspices of the Buddhist monarchs flouri-
shed from the 15th century until after
printing became prevalent in the 19th
century. The authors were monks, or men
who had spend long years of study in
the monasteries, and a few ladies of the
Court. Like most early literature it looked
to the Court for appreciation and patron-
age. Its predominant qualities were there-
fore puritanism of thought and Courtly
refinement of language. Its principal form
was an account of some events from the
life of the Buddha-often a rendering in
verse of one or the other of the Jātaka
or Buddha's Birth Stories. Add accounts
of national events and the deeds of the
king's ancestors in verse together with a
number of didactic and nature and love
poems together with the accretion of
bucolic and folk songs, and we have a
conspectus of Burmese poetry till the
downfall of the monarchy in 1885. This
is the Burmese heritage in which are
reflected its tradition and values that are
mainly for moral uplift and spiritual sti-
mulation though not excluding intellectual
enjoyment.
Six major genres were first evolved
in this period. Five of these are four-
syllable lines with regular climbing rhymes:
e-gyin (historical ballad), maw-gun (panegy-
ric poem), pyo (poem on Buddha's Birth
Story or homilies), yadu (lyrical ode on
nature and love), and lin-ga (non-generic
verse); and sixth is myit-taza (epistle) in
the mixed style of verse and prose form.

1.4 Toungoo period, 1515-1750
Toungoo had been gaining strength
and reputation as a liberal Burmese state
while Ava was heading for disaster. Under
Min-Kyi Nyo, 1486-1531, a contemporary
of Min-gaung, the Second, and Shwe-nan-
kyaw-shin of Ava, it became an indepen-
dent princedom. Many refugees from Ava,
including poets, flocked to this haven,
which was to rule over the whole of Burma
except Arakan for two hundred years.
Four out of the fourteen kings in this
dynasty, Tabin-shwe-hti, 1531-50, Bayin-
naung, 1551–81, Anauk-hpet-lun, 1605–28
and Maha-dhamma-ya-za-di-pati, 1733-50,
deserve our gratitude, since they inspired
the poets about ten in number, to record
their thoughts on palm-leaf. Toungoo
was the first seat of culture from 1515-41,
and was superseded by Pegu until Ava
resumed her former role in 1635.
This was the age of e-gyin and yadu,
which reigned supreme to the exclusion of
the other genres for over one hundred
years from 1530-1630. After a hiatus of
another one hundred years, new genres
emerged side by side with the old in 1730.

1.4 Kan-boung period, 1752-1885
After an interregnum of two years,
during which the long drawn out struggle
between the Burmese and the Mon was
settled for good. Alaung-hpa-ya, 1752-50,
the victorious Burmese leader, ascended
the throne at Kon-baung, an old name
of the present town of Shwe-bo. This
dynasty produced ten kings under whom
four cities shared the honour of being
the capital of the kingdom at one time
or another. These are Kon-baung, 1752-
65; Ava, 1765-83; Amara-pura, 1783-1823;
Ava (for the second time) 1823-37;
Amara-pura, again, 1837-57; and finally
Man-dalay, 1857-85.
This was the twilight of the Burmese
poetry as much as the Ava period was
the dawn: In contradistinction to that
age too, this was the era of the lay-
poets-the culmination of a process that
started during the Toungoo dynasty.
Admittedly all the old genres began to
burgeon forth again after a spell of reces-
sion, but at the same time there was an
increasing variety of form and content
and treatment in Burmese poetry, the
chief causes for which will be put forward
tentatively at the appropriate juncture.
Sein-dakyaw-thu U Aw, 1736-71, the
poet-laureate to Alaung-hpa-ya, composed
over forty yadu-poems, Most of these
naturally are panegyrics on his master,
but some of the best poems are those in
which royalty is subordinated to love or
nature, or to both: human elements are
injected into them and the style is less
elevated, and as such lend themselves to
emotive imageries. Sein-dakyaw-thu was
the first court yaduist who ventured to
enter into the spirit of an ordinary man
and wrote charming poems such as 'on
dream', ‘on a campaign in Lower Burma'
and 'on the Royal Maha-nan-da Lake'.
He was as much underrated as Nat-shin-
naung of Toungoo was overrated by the

II. Content and form
My first lecture concerned itself with
the birth, development, scope and nature
of Burmese poetry. This second lecture
will be devoted entirely to the content and
form of most of the genres dealt with
therein.
The popular generic term for poetry
in Burmese is Kabya lin-ga, derived from
the two Sanskrit words kāvya ‘poetry' and
alamkāra ‘ornamentation' of the sound or
sense. Burmese poetry tried to live up to
the meaning of alamkāra.
The primary device used to achieve
verbal melody in early Burmese verse, in
which the basic number of words or
syllables in the line is four, is rhyme.
Unlike Sanskrit or Pali, vowel length and
stress play virtually no part in its structure.
Burmese is a tonal language: syllables are
differentiated from each other not only by
the consonantal and vocalic elements of
which they are composed, but also by pitch
and voice quality; and it is largely mono-
syllabic, that is, broadly speaking, each
syllable has a 'meaning'--and can be used
as a word.

These embellishments and ingenious

it is. The strict requirements of the four
syllable line, and the extreme flexibility of
the Burmese language with it immense
vocabulary of monosyllables, often entail
distorting words, phrases and syntax.
To accommodate a rhyme, the poet had
often to alter a tone, of truncate poly-

together with the figures of speech, erudite
allusions and verbal gymnastics are part of
the esoteric character of the verse, much
of which is completely intelligible only to
the small number of the initiated.

1.1 Pyo

1.2 Maw-gun
A maw-gun is a record usually of
a notable event in the public life of
the king. It covers subjects as the com-
pletion of a canal, the building of
religious edifices, the successful outcome
of a mititary campaign, the coronation
of the king, the arrival of foreign am-
bassadors, or the acquisition by the king
of a white elephant though in poems
after the 18th century the theme extends
itself to such subjects as an essay on
Cosmography and the life of a hermit.
Like pyo, the maw-gun have all the
embellishments. They are from 10 to 25
pages long in the first two hundred years
and longer later. But unlike pyo which,
up to mid-16th century, were written
only by monks, and e-gyin, which were
written only by lay-Courtiers, maw-gun
were written by monks and lay-men
alike. The monks, with the exception
of two, generally confined their maw-
gun to subjects with religious associations,
while lay-men wrote on secular subjects
only. The poets often used notable events
as a warp and the power and glory of
the king as a woof.

2.7 Yagan (see the example)
Yagan, like the pyo, is a poem which
usually tells a story; but here the similarity
ends: whereas the purpose of the pyo is
mainly religious exposition, the essence of
the yagan is its bite and satire, and many
writers take the opportunity to praise
their master. Some yagan are based on
Jataka stories, but most, and among them
the best, are based on Mon or Indian
legends.
The poet's skill and the content of
the poem are however subordinate in
interest to the witty abusive references to
other writers and contemporary figures.
A yagan may run from 1 to over 300
pages.
The rhyme scheme is less strict than
that of the four syllable lines, as there
are also lines of more or less than four
syllables, but it is not so freely varied as
in some of the songs. Parts of it may be
chanted and parts sung.

tos. Each canto is made up of many
