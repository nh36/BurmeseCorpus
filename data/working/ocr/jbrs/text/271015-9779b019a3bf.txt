BURMESE RULE ON THE TOUNGOO FRONTIER.
1. GEOGRAPHICAL,
The present article deals with Burmese rule between 1852
and 1885 in what is now the Pyinmana Subdivision, and is
based on enquiries made by the author in that region in 1929-1930
and on the sources quoted at the end. Till the end of the second
Anglo-Burmese War, the territory was part of that ruled by the
Toungoo-Yamèthin Hna-Myo Wun, whose headquarters varied
between Toungoo, Ningyan (which the British named Pyinmana)
and Yamèthin. The Toungoo jurisdiction reached to the river
Ngalaik and consisted of fifty-two circles (myos and daings).
At the end of the second Burmese War, all the circles in the
modern Toungoo district became part of British Burma, while the
twelve northernmost were joined to the Yamèthin charge, which
continued to be called Toungoo-Yamèthin. The Burmese called
Ningyan itself Toungoo, and this became the normal headquarters
of the Wun and latterly of his over-lord the Kayaing Wun. The
twelve circles were Ningyan, Lèma, Yanaungmyin, Wanwègon
(Lowe), Minbyin, Wekkabu, Zeyathein (Ela), Kyaukchet, which
were under Myothugyis, and the daings of Chaunggwa, Taung-
win, Taungnyo, and Kyidaung. Latterly, Thittala (Seiknandon)
was made into a separate Myo. They were called the Toungoo
Nè-gyan, from which by some accounts Ningyan derived its name.
At the end of the war, the boundary was formed by the
Myedè Parallel of latitude, which was drawn across the maps
from a point on the left bank of the Irrawaddy six statute miles
north of the flag-staff in the British fort of Myede, due east to
Méhaw on the Sittang, forty eight miles north of Toungoo, cross-
ing the Yomas at Tattay. Thence it ran to the summit of the
Paunglaung hills, east of the Sittang, beyond which the boundary
was not demarcated. From Myedè to the Yomas was forty miles,
and another forty to the Sittang. In the eastern portions it remains.
approximately the boundary between Yamèthin and Toungoo dis-
tricts to this day, the boundaries of the latter district having
never been officially notified. The chief villages on the Toungoo side
were Payagon, Bawlawgyi, Bawbwebin, Zibyubin, Taungmyogale
and Letaunggaw. The latitude is about 19° 28" north. On the
east of the Ningyan territory Karen tribes lived in the hills,

On the north of the Ne-gyan lay Yamèthin, and on the west
Taungdwingyi, the latter ruled by its own Myowun. The main
road of Burma ran through Ningyan, the Royal Road or Min Lan
that was built from Ava to Pegu by either Alompra or Bodawpaya.
From British Toungoo it went through Thawatti, Nyobin, Pyin-
mana (now Pyinmana-Paung), Ningyan, Kyiddaunggan, Pokkon-

gale, Zalokgyi, Lèdagyi and the Palwè valley, going thence over
the Pegu Yomas to Kokkogon and Taungdwingyi.
The Royal
Road was the best in Burma, but was normally in wretched con-
dition, a bridle-road continually encroached on by the jungle and
seldom repaired. Its upkeep was a rarely performed village duty,
though labour on it was supposed to be compulsory. Occasionally it
would be repaired when a Wun or other big official was about to
pass along it, or else an energetic headman would take action, or
an individual or a group would repair it as a work of merit. There
were very few bridges, and these were nearly all works of merit;
they were not kept in order and soon decayed. The most import-
ant was the bridge over the Ngalaik at Ningyan, built by the Myo-
thugyi Tha Doke Gyi in 1832. The journey from Ningyan to
Mandalay took ten days just prior to the annexation. In A People
at School Mr. Fielding Hall describes the road from Ningyan to
Sinthewa on the Sittang, along which he escaped when war broke
out in October 1885. There was deep mud everywhere, creek
water overflowed the fields and road, and the grass on the road
was eight or nine feet high. It was only five miles, but the carts
took five hours to make the journey. There was another well-
known road, the Thieves' Road, which ran approximately parallel
to the main frontier road, through unfrequented jungles along the

2. BURMESE ADMINISTRATION.
The four principal officials were the Kayaing Wun (after
1884), the Myowun, the Lèwun, and the Thittaw Wun. Techni-
cally the power of life and death rested with the King only, but
practically all these officials exercised the fullest powers. Bribery
prevailed in every case, for the judge's salary depended on it.
Torture was always practised, but was more openly carried out in
King Thibaw's time. Officials obtained their positions by bribes,
it might almost be said by purchase. The following was the local

The post of Kayaing Wun was only created in about 1884.
Burma was made into ten Divisions, and "Toungoo" was the

Yamèthin, Ningyan, and four smaller districts, according to Kon-

Kyaukpadaung. This appears to be wrong, as Konbaungset shows
the three latter in the Taungdwingyi or fourth Division. This
official was also called the Komyo Wun, and usually had his head-
quarters at Ningyan. He could try civil suits up to a value of

The Myowun was the officer in general charge of justice,
police-work, revenue, and military officers. He, too, tried civil
suits of a value not exceeding Rs. 1,000, and usually had his
headquarters at Ningyan. U Taung Bo was Yamèthin Myowun
at the time of the second war, but he quarrelled with the British
about the demarcation of the frontier in February, 1854, and was
recalled in disgrace, reaching Amarapura on the 15th April. His
successor was U Bwa, formerly Wun of Martaban. He left
Amarapura on 13th March 1854 with orders to send back all
Burmese troops from the frontier except a garrison of about 500.
The troops reached Amarapura on about 20th April and were dis-
banded, and in May orders were given for the withdrawal of the
remainder, about 550 in number. In November Mr. Spears
reported that U Bwa was keeping good order in his charge, and
he was still there in March 1855.

Most of the trade between Upper and Lower Burma went by
the Irrawaddy, but a little went from Sinthewa on the Sittang to
Toungoo and probably still less by the Royal Road or the Thieves'
Road. There were twenty-four customs houses between Taung-
dwingyi and the Sittang, at all places where goods were likely to
pass. They were usually military kins as well, of which there
were fifteen in Ningyan, and they were worked by soldiers.
Mr. Hall in his A People at School describes the one on the
Royal Road. The chief kin was at Ningyan itself, where goods.
from the Shan States there were taxed if they had not been taxed
before--and sometimes even though they had been. There were two
on the Sittang, at Mchaw and Kyauktaing; and another at Payagon
in the Shan State of Mong Pai. The rates of duty varied, and
there was always a squeeze demanded by the customs men.
Caravans from the hills would frequently be stopped for a whole
dry season by a sudden increase in the duties. The yield from
them was Rs. 60,000 in 1884 in the 'Toungoo' area. Normally the
merchant received a palm-leaf acknowledgment of payment at
Ningyan, which he handed over at the frontier. Conditions of
trans-frontier trade were not satisfactory, and Sir Arthur Phayre
tried to induce King Mindon in 1854 to sign a trade treaty but
failed. He succeeded in 1862, though the results were poor. The
King would consider abolishing his customs duties within a few
years if the British abolished theirs within one year. The British
did so, but the Burmese duties remained. Better results were
achieved five years later when all Burma monopolies were abolished
except those of oil, timber, and precious stones. The duties on all
merchandise crossing the frontier were reduced to a uniform rate of
five per cent. of their value. The royal proclamation was issued on
16th April 1867 and was to be in force for ten years. There
was an export tax of twenty rupees on every pony that left Upper
Burma, and in 1868 the King gave the proceeds of this tax to
Mr. Manook. The treaty lapsed in 1877, and the Burmese evinced
no desire to make a new one.

5. MILITARY MATTERS.
Soldiers were employed in the Ne-gyan in two capacities, to
guard the frontier against the British and to act as policemen in
the hinterland. In 1885 the Sayegyi of the Shan Cavalry regiment
had four hundred men under him, of whom ten were stationed in
each of fifteen kins while the remainder toured the countryside on
police duty. At that time the Colonel of the Myinsugyi cavalry
regiment provided a further force of two non-commissioned officers
and 112 men, partly Burmans, and partly Shans. Under King
Mindon's orders, all troops in Ningyan were recalled in 1854 except
five hundred odd, and the number was usually small in his reign.
Soon after his son's accession, they increased, a new Myowun
entering Ningyan in 1879 with about two thousand men. For their
support he levied four rupees and two baskets of rice from each
household, and large barracks were erected. And the Thitkyeik
Myosa came down from the hills with a thousand Shans. In
December of the same year, a telegraph line was commenced from
wingyi to Ningyan. Disorder increased, and the troops'
aries fell into arrears, the Myinsugyi Wun informing the Hluttaw
that his men in the Toungoo-Yamethin country had received only
three months' pay in the last eight months, and the troops

Two cavalry regiments have been mentioned as being on duty

6. BRITISH ADMINISTRATION ON THE FRONTIER.
A few remarks about administration on the other side of the
frontier may help to complete the picture. The British occupied
Toungoo in February 1853 and soon extended their authority to
the frontier, not without opposition as will be described in the
next chapter. A fort was built at Toungoo, which is still there,
being occupied by the military police not long ago, and timber
for cantonment houses was bought from the Ningyan Myo-
wun in 1855. There were police posts at intervals of every four
or five miles along the frontier, consisting of a station-house, a
watch tower, and dwelling-huts, in a compound surrounded by
a kya-hlan of bamboos. Among them were Bawlawgyi and
Bawbwebin. The garrisons were Burmans armed with dahs and
muskets and dressed for the most part according to the taste
of the Superintendent of Police, the only official uniform being a
red-striped longyi, Toungoo was included in the Rangoon district
(which proved too large and had to be split up in due course),
Mr. E. O'Riley being posted there as Assistant Commissioner
under the Commissioner of the Pegu Division by a notification of
5th May 1853. When Toungoo district was formed, the Toungoo
subdivision occupied the country west of the Sittang, and the
Leiktho or Karen Hill Tracts subdivision, with headquarters at
Leiktho, lay to the east. A metalled road was made to the fron-
tier along the old Min I.an with all speed. The railway from
Rangoon reached Toungoo early in 1885, after which the met-
alling of the road was not kept. The train at first took a whole.
day to do the 167 miles from Rangoon. There was also to be
a road from Toungoo to Thayetmyo, and the Commander of the
Madras Army and his staff marched over the track in February
1875. The road was begun before 1878, but was never finished.
When war seemed imminent, Sir Frederick (later Lord) Roberts
came over from Madras in 1882 and marched along the frontier
on a tour of inspection.

two to ten miles, with police outposts at intervals. The Burmese
threw down some of these pillars, while others were destroyed by
jungle growth, but some remain to the present day. Sir Arthur
Phayre, the Chief Commissioner of British Burma, inspected
them when he made a tour along the frontier in April 1855.
There were no pillars east of the Sittang for the Yimbaws
and Padoungs" who dwelt in the hills were supposed to be
fierce tribesmen who had never acknowledged Burmese suzerainty
and would object equally to the British. Subsequently, mission-
aries from Toungoo found the hillmen less savage than they had
expected, and British influence extended to the north of what was
really the frontier into the small thinly-populated Karen state of
Alègyaung-Bawgata. This was governed by its own taungsa but
paid a tribute of four annas a household each year to the Myowun
at Ningyan. When the taungsa died in 1880, it was not the
Burmese but McClear, Deputy Commissioner of Toungoo, who
appointed a Regent during the minority of Ethataung, the new
ruler, and had the child educated in the Roman Catholic Mission
School in Toungoo. Ethataung returned home presently and
ruled independently of any over-lord. When the British began
to pacify Upper Burma in 1886, he gave assistance to dacoit
bohs, who used his state as a refuge from pursuing columns and a
headquarters for further raids. The Revd. A. Bunker, of the
Toungoo American Baptist Mission, had acquired some influence
over him and persuaded him to submit in 1887, but he again made
trouble and was not finally pacified till 1889.

migrate to north of the border, and sometimes succeeded. Yule,
