DANUBYU
A Forgotten Episode
Of all the small Wars that the Empire has fought within the
last 125 years the first Burmese War is among the most obscure and
the least known. Few, even nowadays, are sure where the Province
of Burma itself is situated and fewer still know anything concerning
any war in Burma with the exception of the Annexation of Upper
Burma in 1884-85. Yet not only is the War of 1824-26 notable as
being the first in which the Company's sepoys were engaged East
of the Ganges but also because it includes the amazing 500 mile
march of an Army from Rangoon to Yandabo, 45 miles South of Ava,
which conducted as it was through unknown, unmapped and un-
developed country and contending as it did with difficulties of supply
and transport and unhealthiness of climate, can have few parallels.
It is not however, my purpose to write of the War as a whole but of
one striking episode, the attacks on, the siege and the final occupation
of Danubyu.
The transports had appeared_before_ Rangoon on May 11th,
1824; the city and the great Shwe Dagon Pagoda had been occupied
and in a series of hard-fought engagements the Burmese forces had
been driven from their entrenchments and stockades and forced
back several miles. The Government of Ava, not yet convinced of
the necessity of making peace, thereupon decided to call back their
best commander, the famous Maha Bandula, who was then facing
the British in Arakan and entrust to him the duty of driving the
"insolent barbarians" from Rangoon. Incidentally " Bandula
is a title or nickname and not his real name in an English sense.
"
In obedience to orders Bandula and his Army commenced their
homeward march. The month was August, in the depth of the
rains, but the Burmese forces left the old Town of Arakan, marched
down the flat coast and then poured over the An pass into Burma.
After a march which for sheer bodily discomfort alone must have
constituted a record they appeared before Rangoon ready and willing
to fight. It had been a magnificent feat. Between Burma proper
and Arakan lie the Yomahs, a long range of tumbled hills mounting
to peaks 3,000 feet high. In August they are covered with sodden
uninhabited jungle, the rain pours down incessantly, a thick mist
drifts daylong across the hillsides and it is, comparatively speaking,
bitterly cold. All the streams are in flood; leeches abound and
there is little if any, shelter; the anopoheles mosquito, carrying in
Arakan a particularly unpleasant type of malaria which affects the
digestive organs, is abroad in the shadow of the jungle day and night
and is reinforced by un-numbered quantities of other, non-malarial

since those days and few even now would care to make the march even
though provided with proper equipment and transport; roads
there are none and the An pass itself is crossed by a jungle track
only. Local legend still speaks of the epic journey of Bandula's men
and records vaguely that many died. They undoubtedly did;
it must have been a water-logged nightmare.
Good general though he was and brave in their own way as were
his troops (many of them believed to be and believing they were,
invulnerable) Bandula fared no better than his predecessors and was
in his turn driven off. Accepting the fait accompli he retired
back along the road to Upper Burma and entrenching himself at
Danubyu announced that he was determined to fight it out there.
By this time (December 1824) the British had realised that
peace could only be purchased at the price of an advance on Ava
the capital, a good 500 miles away by the route actually followed.
Accordingly, the first set of operations was to take possession of the
chief towns of the Irrawaddy Delta and clear Bandula and his army
out of the way. When this had been done the various forces were
to combine and march on Prome, the first big step towards Ava.
The great question immediately arises as to what was the value
of Danubyu and why did Bandula determine to hold it. From a
consideration of the routes followed by the Expedition, the state of
the Delta in 1824 compared with the conditions today together with
the spoils found in Danubyu after capture, the importance is mani-
fest; what is not quite so clear in the light of after events, is Bandula's
reason for the stand there.
The Delta of the Irrawaddy is a huge and nowadays thickly-
populated area which for purposes of rough description may be
considered as a triangle, the apex at Prome and the angles of the
base at Rangoon and Bassein respectively; the sides are perhaps
150 miles and the base 200. Therein the land is dead flat, very
little above water level and given up to paddy cultivation and fisheries.
There is very little jungle except along the sea coast where large
Forest Reserves, chiefly of mangrove, ensure the firewood supply;
the whole area is criss-crossed by a network of tidal streams and
traversed by the big main branches of the Irrawaddy itself; trees
are scarce and when found in any quantity mark the course of a river
or stream or the site of a town or village. In the short cold weather
the climate is reasonably cool but never refreshing; in the hot weather
in the absence of any breeze it is close and stifling; in the rains
which last for 6 months of the year it is warm, damp, feverish and
extremely relaxing. The quantities of mosquitoes and insects (who
all appear to bite or to sting; many do both) must be seen and ex-

Nowadays the Delta is divided up into various Districts for
administrative purposes; Danubyu itself, the headquarters of a
township of the same name in the Maubin District, is still a place
of considerable local importance. It is a straggling town, perhaps
a mile and a half long, spreading ribbon-wise along the river bank,
its main industries paddy and tobacco. The river itself, the main
branch of the Irrawaddy, is here a good 800 yards wide.
In 1824, however, the Delta was very different. The population
was sparse and the cultivation little more than small clearings scattered
here and there in thick tree jungle. There was far more swamp
and the streams were deeper and wider while villages were few and
far between. Game, even tiger and elephant, was abundant while
owing to the fact that the country was almost entirely undeveloped,
for many months in the year the lower-lying portions were inundated.
There was little administration except of a crude type and practically
nothing in the way of roads, bridges and ferries. It was an area from
which an army could not hope to draw much in the way of supplies
even though the inhabitants proved friendly. The various pests,
leeches, mosquitoes, ticks, biting and stinging insects and flies of
many varieties were no less plentiful than they are today while over-
head was that enemy of European troops in the East, the sun; the
Delta is but 16 degrees North. It was through this huge obstacle
that the British Army had to travel on its way to Danubyu and Prome,
en route for Ava.
Now, Rangoon is not upon the Irrawaddy at all but on the Hlaing
which nowadays can hardly be called even an offshoot of the main
river. A branch of the Hlaing, the Panhlaing, takes off from the
main channel of the Irrawaddy at the town of Yandoon in the Maubin
District (marked in Snodgrass' map as Yangain Chenah and Custom
House; See a "Narrative of the Burmese War"). Nowadays
during the low water season vessels drawing more than 3 feet of
water cannot emerge from the Panhlaing into the main river owing
to the formation of a bar at the mouth, but in 1824 the Panhlaing was
the chief route from Rangoon to the Irrawaddy, the upper reaches
of the Hlaing being useless. An alternative route was from Rangoon
to Bassein by sea and from there up the Bassein river which takes off
from the main river far above both Yandoon and Danubyu but this
was a long and somewhat dangerous journey. The intricate channels
of the Delta which today provide waterways leading in almost any

As one proceeds up the main river today the East bank is com-
paratively flat and low-lying while the West bank is higher and in
places actually steep. The mouth of the Panhlaing in the Eastern
bank is fringed by sandflats and dunes covered in the dry weather by
coarse grass and patches of cultivation. The West bank however
remains comparatively high and the river, year by year, is cutting
into the earth thereof; at low water season only the funnel of a
steamer can be seen over the top but in the rains the water rises from
30 to 40 feet. A system of bunds or embankments controls the
spread of flood water.
About 10 miles above the Panhlaing mouth situated on the
highest part of the Western bank is Danubyu, an old Talaing town
and at that time a big supply depot and arsenal for the Burmese
forces. The name derives from the Pali word danu a peacock
and the Burmese pyu white. About 30 miles North of Danubyu
on the same bank is Henzada. From Danubyu a road takes off and
runs due West along the highest ground, entering the Bassein District
and ultimately ending at Bassein. This path was in existence in
1824. Finally the low water channel, then as now, ran under the
Western bank thereby bringing all vessels under the command of
the guns in Danubyu, an important matter in those days of muzzle-
loaders, imperfect fuses and short effective ranges.
We are now therefore in a position to appreciate and assess the
strategic value of Danubyu; it is on a high part of the land (heights
in the delta are measured in inches), it commanded what was then
the main water route from Rangoon up-country and it was situated
at the point where the land route from Rangoon to Bassein, roughly
following the course of the Panhlaing, crossed the main Irrawaddy
and continued along the high ground to Bassein. In other words
it was a central junction and supply depot; it was probably the
disinclination to abandon the supplies of all kinds collected there
that determined Bandula to hold the fortress.
To strike therefore at the capital city of Ava the British forces
were faced with two clear alternatives; either they must go by sea
to Bassein, from there ascending the Bassein river until they emerged
far above Danubyu into the main river or else they must follow the
usual route up the Hlaing and the Panhlaing into the Irrawaddy
and so to Henzada and Prome, which necessarily entailed "mopping
up Danubyu on the way. It will of course be appreciated that in
such undeveloped country there could be no question of conveying

The month of December saw the expeditions on the march.
One part, the Main Body, came by land from Rangoon roughly
parallel to the Hlaing but later struck off to the North and passing
East of Danubyu eventually arrived at the bank of the main river at
Tharrawaw opposite Henzada, then a depot of considerable size but
now a small, straggling village where the terminus of the branch
line from Letpadan on the Rangoon-Prome line is situated. From
here the Main Body intended to march on Prome being joined either
here or higher up by the river column. This latter, a flotilla of
boats and vessels carrying all supplies for the main body, passed up
the Hlaing, then through the Panhlaing into the Irrawaddy and so to
Danubyu, the intention being to capture that town and then pass
on to join the Main Body. A third small column which incidentally
included Havelock and his "Saints", the 13th Foot, went to capture
Bassein but played no further part in the Danubyu operations although
they were near enough to hear, far away across the flats, the rumble
of the artillery during the attacks.
In the meanwhile Bandula and his men, assisted of course by
all the labour that they could crimp or impress from the countryside,
had been hard at work. The town of Danubyu was already sur-
rounded by brick ramparts and a ditch as, in fact, were all Burmese
and Talaing towns in that age, the whole town being inside the
ramparts. The line of the fortifications was about 1,000 yards long
on the river face and extended 500 or 600 yards inland, the whole
forming a rough oblong. In front of the brick walls Bandula erected
a Stockade of heavy timbers 17 feet high, firmly embedded in the
earth and connected to the brick walls by cross-beams. In front of
the stockade itself was a ditch from which the earth to make the
bricks had been excavated, 18 feet wide and 8 feet deep. In front
of the ditch was a wooden or bamboo fence or "railing", then a
space of 18 feet sown thick with spikes or panjies, then another
fence, an 18 foot wide abattis and furthermost of all yet another
fence. On the East or river front however most of these defences
were omitted; the ramparts themselves came up almost to the river
bank and the small space in between was occupied by gun batteries
to which the river itself with its 40 foot high precipitous bank formed

This all gives a strength of infantry of about 775 of whom between
500 and 600 were Europeans but no figures are traceable as to the
crews of the guns and mortars carried. Many of course were probably
native gunners (golundazes) but there must have been a proportion
of Europeans. Not all enumerated were present at the first assaults
since a garrison was left en route at Panhlaing village. Considering
what they had to face it canot be considered that the force was
excessive. The actual flotilla consisted of 62 boats and brigs each
have one or more 9, 12 or 24 lb. carronades in the bows. They
were accompanied by the Diana an armed paddle steamer built
by private enterprise but purchased by Government for the War.
Both columns left on the same or succeeding days; the river
column dragged its way up the Hlaing and the Panhlaing rivers,
meeting with opposition here and there and capturing several stock-
ades. At Panhlaing village itself a big stockade was captured and a
garrison of 25 of the Madras Europeans and the 18th Madras Native
Infantry left there. At no place however was the resistance severe
and most of the positions were captured without fighting. On the
27th February the flotilla passed, with some difficulty, out of the
Panhlaing into the Main Irrawaddy and camped at Yangain Chenah,
the modern Yandoon, until the 5th March. On the 6th it proceeded
upriver and anchored at a spot said to be "opposite "Danubyu but
a consideration of the circumstances shows that it was short of the
actual fortress and a little below the outworks mentioned. Bandula
was summoned to surrender but declined to do so; it is significant
however, that his refusal was couched in more courteous terms than
had hitherto been the case in his communications as though the
lessons of the fighting around Rangoon had engrafted courtesy upon
his military abilities. On arrival a reconnaissance was made by a
party of the 89th who landed and surveyed the defences while boats
went up the river. The heaviness of the fire from the fortress how-
ever, much of it from guns of large calibre, compelled withdrawal
though without casualties. As a result it was determined to attack
the two outworks and so remove any danger from them before attempt-
ing the main work.
At daylight on the 7th the first move in the game was made;
about 600 men were employed in the attack which was delivered in
two columns against the pagoda outwork. The right column con-
sisting of the 89th moved along the bank of the river; the second
column consisting of part of the 47th and the Madras Europeans
circled around further inland, the two attacks coming against the
work at two different points, the South and the West. The advance
was covered, inadequately enough, by only two 6-pounders and a
rocket detachment. Once the troops had scrambled up the earth
bank of the river, here over 30 feet high and precipitous, the ground

the trees still in leaf; the sky blue as it had not yet assumed the

open ground the two columns approached, the men upstanding in
their red coats, light trousers and shakoes, the latter covered in all

and then both columns charged straight for the stockade.
They were met with a gallantry no whit inferior to their own.
As they came up to the horrible tangle of abattis, railing and ditch
in front of the stockade a heavy fire was opened upon them not only
of musketry but also from light guns and jingals, much of which,
however, must have gone overhead. Heavy as it was it failed to stop
them. Smashing through the fences and the abattis, floundering
through the ditch and escalading the stockade by sheer human muscle
they burst into the defences, both columns arriving simultaneously.
A short but savage fight ensued chiefly with cold steel, as the defenders
found themselves caught like rats in a trap with the rear face of the
stockade at their backs and the means of egress limited. An officer
who took part states that 230 dead and 374 prisoners, mostly severely
wounded, were found in the outwork, which is nearly a man to every
musket in the attack. Many however managed to get away and took
refuge in the second and larger, outwork. Three guns and many
jingals, muskets, swords, spears and much ammunition were all
captured. The British loss was about 20 men of whom only 4 or 5
were killed. In view of the obstacles and the heavy fire it is amazing
that the loss was not heavier as accounts of men who took part show
that the assault and the ensuing fight were no light matters. The
fact is however, as an officer recorded, that "although the Burmese
soldier aimed with great precision yet his want of discipline prevented
the fire from being sufficiently rapid to do much injury and he seldom
had time to fire more than twice upon storming troops. The
handling of the artillery was no better but many of the footmen
showed desperate courage, grasping their dahs (double-handed sword)
in both hands and fiercely charging the bayonets.
"

Nevertheless it was determined to assault the second outwork. Two
more guns and four mortars were hauled up the bank and after
an artillery preparation which seems to have been all too brief, an

allotted to the attack drawn from the 47th, the 89th and the Madras

and the fire too heavy. The second outwork was just as well built
as the first and in addition was far larger; it contained not only its

three closed works of which it consisted flanked each other; finally

abattis; it was finally forced to retire with the loss of its leader and
about 80 men, including almost every seaman who had accompanied
the advance. There was no counter-attack or pursuit. Brave as
were Bandula's men they had evidently had enough.
The troops held their ground till nightfall but the situation was
not encouraging. Two attacks upon outwork only had cost 5
officers and 130 men casualties, of whom some 15 or 20 were dead.
The commander reasoned that with his depleted numbers there was
little if any, hope of a successful assault on the main defences and
the flotilla thereupon drew off next morning and dropped down the
river to the mouth of the Panhlaing, camping probably on the Western
bank where now is situated the big village of Setkaw.
As is usual in human affairs no single important cause can be
assigned for the failure. The description already given of Outwork
No. 2 shows that it was far too strong to be assaulted successfully
by a weak column, many of the men of which, if not all, had already
taken part in one desperate struggle. The odds were too heavy.
Why therefore, had not a more effectual artillery preparation been
carried out? The flotilla had a heavy train of artillery on board
but at the utmost the advance had been prepared and covered by no
more than 4 six-pounders, 4 mortars and rockets.
The answer to this is to be found partly in the actual circum-
stances. The boats, carrying as they did only carronades, could not
assist; the high precipitous earthen bank prevented them from
bringing these weapons to bear on the defences at close quarters
while little useful practice could be made from an anchorage 200 or

Even if all this be admitted, however, there still remains the
question as to why more use was not made of the 8 pieces already in
position on the bank, aided by the rockets. True, the guns were but
6-pounders and we know from other sources that even 24-pounders
had singularly little breaching effect as regards well-built stockades,
but even had their fire proved ineffective, the mortars and rockets

closed works. It is to be suspected that a part, at any rate, of the
secret is to be found in the ideas underlying a remark made by an
officer after Danubyu had been taken" strange as it may seem I have
felt less satisfaction upon finding ourselves master of it than in gaining
possession of the smallest stockade which we have carried at the
point of the bayonet on former occasions." The fall of Danubyu
was directly due to gun and rocket fire; hence the regrets on the
part of the infantry. One is driven to the conclusion that had the
flotilla been ready to make more use of the artillery available the
chances of success would have been largely increased. The infantry
assault, delivered upon a position of the strength of the inner work,
by an inadequate number of men already tired by their previous
exertions (and the escalade of a stockade is an exhausting and nerve-
raking job) and whose way had not been properly prepared by the
artillery, was foredoomed to failure unless the enemy had been
arrant cowards, which they most decidedly were not.
In fact one
is left with a suspicion that the launching of the attack by only 200
men reflects no credit upon, and pays no tribute to, the ability of
the commander.

The spoils were large. Many unwounded prisoners, as well
as practically all the Burmese wounded, 150 cannons and 250 jingals,
a big magazine of gunpowder and military stores of all kinds, and,

The reason for the abandonment was not far to seek. On April
1st Bandula himself had been killed by a rocket or a mortar shell

take his place although it is related that his brother tried to assume
command with but indifferent success, his failure being rewarded
later by execution. Vain, cruel and overbearing though he was, yet

The British forces marched away on the 3rd April for Henzada,

in the same ruined and depopulated condition in which it had been
left 25 years before.

The terrain at Danubyu has altered greatly with the lapse of
time and it is difficult to identify with any reasonable degree of accu-
racy, the majority of the sites. Not only has the river continued
to erode the Western bank but the spread of cultivation leading to
the clearing of all jungle and the institution of drainage, combined
with the erection of the big bunds or embankments on both sides

obliterating former landmarks. In addition the town has spread
in a ribbon along the bank and many, if not all, of the positions are
now covered by houses and streets. The course of the small stream
that protected the flanking stockade cannot now be ascertained as the
ground around the town is all under paddy cultivation thus altering
the drainage scheme.
