Burma has been for eight or nine
centuries the strong-hold of the Ceylon
or Theravāda (Teaching of the Elders)
school of Buddhism. This has always had
to its credit wherever it has been esta-
blished, the dissemination of education not
only in the religious language, Pali, but
also in the language of the country. The
prime object of this education was to in-
culcate an accurate knowledge of Buddhism
and to preserve its purity. The king was
the Upholder of the Faith. The Order of
monks welcomed his support and he could
hardly have maintained his position in the
country otherwise than as a professed
Buddhist monarch.
Literature, the earliest examples of
which, apart from inscriptions that are
mostly dedicatory in nature, date from
the 15th century, originated under these
auspices. It was written by monks, or by
men who had spent long years of study
in the monasteries, or occasinally by ladies
of the court, and like most early literatures
it looked to the Court for appreciation
and patronage. Its predominant qualities
were therefore puritanism of thought and

spread, and by about the middle of the

The authors of the plays are unknown;
the plays were adaptations of Jātaka,
episodes from the Rama epic, and other
popular stories. Puppets were extremely
popular. The figures were worked with
great skill by strings from above and the
same type of play was performed by them
as by live actors.

A Burmese writer makes the remark
that the British annexation of Upper

It is not easy to agree with this view.
The authors of the plays which we have
been considering were contented citizens
of British Burma and were dependent
only on the publishers and the reading
public, which was now the only patron
of literature in Burma. Literature which
used to flourish under the patronage of
the Burmese kings, now flourished with
the help of the investments of the press
owners in British Burma.
The years that followed the British
annexation in 1886 proved to be barren
of imaginative literature of any quality.

came the virtual end of Court literature

U Nu, an ex-premier of post-war
Burma, has divided his time between
Buddhism, politics and literary pursuits.
He has written many books, of which the
most interesting ones to students of litera-
ture are his play and the memoir 'Five
Years in Burma.2 The story of this memoirs
begins in 1941 with U Nu in jail, impri-
soned by the British Government for his
strong political views. The events move
briskly: the Japanese invasion in 1942,
the setting up of a Burmese Government
under Dr. Ba Maw in which U Nu
served first as Information Minister and
later as Foreign Minister; the Burmese
army revolt against the Japanese; and the
allied reconquest of Burma in 1945. The
story, in which there are many tense
moments, is admirably told, with pun-
gent comments on the Burmese welcoming
of the Japanese liberators from colonial
rule, their disillusionment and despeir:
on the author's difficult dealings with the
Japanese officers, and the frustrations
and obstacles he encountered as a minis-
ter in the puppet Government; and final-
ly his flight with his family before the
invading allied armed forces. The nar
rative is by turns, serious, humorous and
pathetic.

U Nu is a facile writer. He is not
Burmese scholar but he has an ex-
cellent command of the language. His
propensity for blending literary and col-
loquial expressions-sometimes even slang
in both his speeches and his writings
has resulted in a 'Nu style," which is
Very effective at public meetings but
is sometimes irritating to the reader. In
'Five Years in Burma' U Nu missed
an opportunity to write a most
remarkable memoir for posterity; he
confines his attention solely to his
own personal affairs without saying any-
thing about the momentous events which
were then taking place in the world
around him. This parochialism is part
of the Burmese tradition which manifests
itself in the chronicles of Burma. In these
no events, however internationally impor-
tant they were, had any place unless they
fell into the pattern of the king's life.
Tradition dies hard.

Dagon Ta-ya however was one of
those, who defy tradition.
He is a pro-
duct of the Khit-san movement. He also
belongs to the 'school of creative writing'
a term popularly used among young writers
of today. These writers, many of whom
are more acquainted with the English
langugage than their own, deliberatly turn
their backs on tradition and orthodoxy
and 'create' words, expressions, idioms
and images of their own, mainly imitating.
them wholesale from English, disregard-
ing the difference of cultural background
between the two languages.

His 'Profile', a series of sketches of
