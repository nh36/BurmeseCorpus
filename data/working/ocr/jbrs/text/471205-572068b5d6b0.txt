INTRODUCTION
While I was reading the back issues of the Forward magazine, I came across a
reprint of the speech delivered by Brigadier San Yu, Vice Chief of Staff (Army), at the
mass rally in the Resistance Park on Armed Forces Day, March 27, 1963. In the course
of his speech, the Brigadier touched upon two basically important points that deserve a
special attention in building a new socialist society in our country. One is connected with
a defective legal system while the other points to the unjust administration of justice. As
to the former, the Brigadier said:
"The faith that we have is neither blind nor misplaced. We have concrete
proofs to strengthen that faith. Immediately on assumption of responsiblity
the Revolutionary Government applied itself to the reform of archaic and
arbitrary laws. In the past some of those laws worked injustice, being elastic
and flexible, capable of being stretched and used for the protection of vested
interests. The laws were just only for the rich and the privileged; the poor
and the unprivileged received no protection from them. The rich and the
privileged could use their privilege and their wealth to buy justice."
The facts of the foregoing statement were a commonplace to the public, and it is
not nesessary to over-emphasize their truth at this stage. What is important is to give an
analytical view of the relationship of these facts to the general conditions of the time.
This is the purpose of this paper, which I intend to fulfil later on. In the meantime,
I would like to reintroduce another statement of the Brigadier in connection with the penal
system and its philosophy that have been practiced in dealing with the violators of the
said laws in our country. As he said:
"Punishments were not meted out in a curative or creative manner; he who
received punishment was completely crushed and his life as a useful citizen was
completely extinguished. Now, under the Revolutionary Government the aim
of punishment is not to destroy. Punishments without purpose are not
awarded. The welfare of the community and the rehabilitation of the punished
as a useful citizen are the large aims constantly made in determining the nature
and the measure of punishment.”

Burma has earned a name for her "progressive" crime rates since the prewar days.
Many attempts were made to find the means to prevent crime and deal with the criminal.
But it is still an unsolved problem that the new socialist society must face in the right
manner. The foregoing statements suggest that the Revolutionary Government is well
aware of the problem and making a correct approach to solve it. It is in support of this
approach that the following historical material is presented in the light of the theoretical
and philosophical considerations implicit in the science of criminology.
Crime and the Social Order
“Crime is a mode of adaptation to social order”, wrote two American criminological
sociologists, Professors Herbert Bloch and Gilbert Geis. In other words, crime is a behaviour-
al pattern that is conditioned by the socio-economic and political forces in a society. The
available socio-anthropological data fully indicates that there was not much crime in the
traditional societies which thrived on the subsistence agricultural economy.
Burma has a cultural heritage of many hundred years. It is written in our history
that the first Burmese Kingdom in Upper Burma - Tagaung - was founded in 850 B.C.
The legend of Shwedagon tells us about the foundation of our spiritural culture dating
back the days of our Lord Buddha. Most of the western observers wrote about the
stories of the indigenous races of Burma emphasizing on their simplicity, generosity, and
friendliness as their national characteristics.
Our forefathers lived a peaceful life. They were not wealthy materially, but happy
with what they had. The spirit of co-operation and compassion - Metta - were the
foundations of their social order and there was no place for social antagonism and crime.
By the middle of the 18th Century, the Industrial Revolution began to shake the
foundations of the western social order. As the new technologies facilitated their produc-
tive power, fresh industrial societies emerged and trade and commerce prospered, creating
the need for more raw materials and the want for more markets. The discoveries of the
new sea routes to Asia, Africa and America attracted the European men to expand their
market to those continents. Burma was gradually exposed to the western commerce and
expansion, and it was inevitable that the contact with the outside world should bring
about a certain change in the outlook of the people. In the normal social processes, that
change should have come through the national motivation of the Burmese people. In that
case, it could have been adaptive rather than disruptive. What happened in Burma was
not social transformation. It was a social dislocation because of the anti-social economic
and political forces that emanated from the new colonial culture. Our analysis will show
that crime in Burma was a symptomization of a dislocated social order as aggravated by
various anti-social forces.

the growth of crime in the country as a unique phenomenon that appeared during the
British rule. According to the late Mr. J. S. Furnivall, “the abnormal criminality is a

The Rule of Law and a New Legal System

Any society embraces or has to embrace a system of control to maintain order or
the equilibrium. Control, of course, is not the only means to secure order because
socialization, designed to attain conformity, can also achieve the same objectives. There
are many forms of social control, and control through the polity or the governmental
authority is the basic one because it is vested with the power to use sanction. In order
to carry out its functions it becomes incumbent upon the polity to frame a set of laws for
the observance of its members with provisions for punishment for any violation. On its
part, the polity also has to observe the laws of the state in its relationship with its
members. When the observance of the law becomes universal, then the rule of law can
be considered to have been established. This is the basic principle, and the form and
details may take on cultural variations in different cultures.
In the modern sense, the rule of law is essentially of English origin. There was a
long struggle between King and Parliament for constitutional protection. The British rule
closely followed this tradition, and the rule of law was observed during its adminis-
tration in the best sense. On the other hand, both law and its administration are part
of a culture, and “the clevage between law and life leading to many unfortunate results"
was observed as a feature of the British rule in Burma.11
The British attempt to construct a new legal system may be said to start with
Mr. Maingy's approach to adapt the native legal institutions to the English principles
during the early stage of the British administration soon after the annexation of Tenas-
serim. His task was to revive the native law courts. He was, however, unable to
appreciate the Burmese attitude towards their laws which were practiced as the "rules of
conduct,” for the people and the guidance for the judges who were thus given a wider
power of discretion to decide a case taking into consideration different circumstances in
each case.
In the same way, he was puzzled by the working of the native institution of
arbitration which embraced the appointment of a man by the disputants, “who could
help them in arriving at an amicable settlement of their dispute." Mr. Maingy's
inability to appreciate the working of both led him to codify the Burmese laws, giving
them the force of the mechanically fixed legal principles, and constitute the newly
adapted courts to be presided over by the British civil officers assisted by a native
authority. The innovation was intended for the "imaginative application of the Burmese
legal standards”, but not to uproot the whole system. However, as it was observed
later, to the Burmese people, “the whole idea of giving judgement in accordance with
the fixed legal principles was contrary to their custom, and the decisions on this plan
were as foreign as the court.”12 Finally, as the new approach was unsatisfactory to

The decision of the Governor-General became a settled policy for planting the

judicial organisation was a matter of time. The main features of development were:
the extension of the Indian laws and regulations from time to time; the appointment in

the arrangements of 1881 for the Judicial Commissioner and the Recorders of Rangoon

1890 of another Judicial Commissioner for Upper Burma; the establishment in 1900 of
a Chief Court of Judicature at Rangoon; and the creation in 1905 of the Burma
Judicial Service designed to relieve the local civil officers of all their civil and some of
the criminal jurisdictions.
Thus, the idea of the rule of law and the machinery of justice as a steel-frame of
the British administration, designed to maintain peace and order and collect the revenue
in order to promote their administrative policy, originated and grew deeper in Burma.
We shall see later on how it bears upon the social development of the country.
The Conflict of Values and Attitudes
Starting from Arakan and ending with Upper Burma, the British administrators,
in many cases assisted by the native Burmese, laboriously pursued their policy of giving
an efficient administrative system to Burma. It seems, on the whole, the objective was
achieved in establishing “the most benevolent and business-like government" effective
and efficient in the mechanical application of laws and the transaction of the general
business. On the other hand, both the legislative activity and the administrative practice
were not apparently intended to serve a social purpose. In other words, the laws that
were passed were more of criminal and administrative in character rather than social.
Thus the philosophy of social legislation did not come into the purview of the new legis-
lative programmes. Therefore Mr. G. E. Harvey correctly observed that the system as
a whole was "out of touch with its (the native character's) inner and probably finer side"
with the result that the distance between the people and the Government became wider
and wider. It was not merely because of the sentiments of nationalism on the part of
the Burmese, although undoubtedly nationalism later became the most forceful deter-
minant in shaping the relations between the foreign ruler and the natives.
It is proposed here to discuss the sociological phenomenon that emerged from
the administrative policy and practice, reacting upon the personality and culture of the

On the part of the British, the history of their empire in the East indicates
their reluctance in annexing, at least, Upper Burma. They were in the East, following
the course of the world history, and flowed into Burma along with other nations
through the stream of the European expansion. Their main object was to promote
their trade for which war and conquest in almost all the cases became the necessary
evils as an overall strategy in competing with their rivals.
Once a piece of land is acquired, its administration became a problem. But few
in those days would view such a task as a human enterprise conducive to creating a
problem requiring special skills for its solution. It seems an efficient administration
based upon mechanical rules supported by coercive power and military might to obtain
their compliance was considered to constitute the sum total of criterion to become a
good government regardless of social efficiency and healthy growth. In this respect we
cannot fail to recognize that the concept of anthropology in administration received a
growing attention only in recent decades as a result of the past experience in colonial
administration whose essence was to diffuse or impose one administrative culture upon
another, giving rise to the problems of adjustment, which expressed themselves in the
symptoms of political opposition and/or social deterioration.14
Such a conflict of values manifested itself in the Burmese situation from the very
beginning. The resistance movement and the guerilla war put up against the British
after the last war in 1885 were the indications that negated the optimistic views of Lord
Dalhousie who reportedly said that the Burmese people would generally regard with
pleasure the prospects of substituting a strong and orderly government for the "incom-
petent” and a “cruel tyranny” of their former rulers. In this respect the following

Undoubtedly, monarchy is too anarchronistic to have a place in the present day
Burma. On the other hand, its contributions to the cultural continuity of the country

The new rulers of Burma unfortunately failed to be able to appreciate this view
point. They identified the rebellion with a far baser name, “dacoity", and treated the
rebels as dacoits. When the people rendered whatever assistance they could to their
fighting men, the British authorities became tougher and adopted a more punitive course
of action. They reportedly said, “the only course open to us was to make them (the
people) fear us more than the dacoits”. 16 Thus the events of the day came to widen
the gap farther and farther. The liberal ideas of the British rule did not reach the hearts
of the people. Since then, power became almost the only instrument of governance.
The laws were enforced through coercive measures, and the people failed to find any
ethical value and sanctity in their worth in as much as they could not grasp their mean-
ing. Their hearts were filled with distrust. The situation became more complicated
when the administrative measures designed to promote efficiency and welfare ironically
destroyed their social life.
Today Burma has a high regard for the principle of the rule of law.
In the past,
however, the transition from the traditional order built upon the foundations of person-
al authority which permits flexibility to adjust human elements guided by the moral and
customary laws to a mechanical system based upon the fixed principles of the impersonal
laws was too difficult for normal human adjustments.

customary laws were given an entirely different life, the force of command in place of
moral guide to action, which leaves the scope for the learned judge to apply them to
each case, taking into consideration the circumstances involved. The new approach had
changed both the character of the laws and the mode of their application. The role
of the judge also became mechanical and all human touch disappeared in the new
polity, straining the relations between the Government and the people. Moreover, the
rule of law created the need for increasing legislation. In many cases, the laws were
made without apparent need for their utility. Commenting upon the multiplicity of
laws, a Financial Commissioner of the Punjab said in 1899 that the country (India)

The next area of conflict was the attitudes of the old and the new regimes towards
an offender. Burmese as other nations naturally despise crime or any other sin.
the same time, the Burmese accept the imperfection of human soul, and consequently
the impossibility of human nature to be completely immune from sin. On the other
hand, the principle of punishment for any wrong doing is inherent in the Burmese
system. Punishment does not however mean the disablement of the offender for life.
One must atone for one's sins, if necessary undergoing a requisite amount of suffering,
which is a condition for penitence and correcting oneself. After that, the offender is
expected to have become repentent for the evil he has done, and thus purified his soul
and ready for a fresh and healthy life. Once he has paid for his crime, his fellow
neighbours have sympathy for him. That is the fundamental aspect of Karuna
(compassion), one of the Four Sublime States of Mind in the Burmese spiritual system.
On the other hand, the attitude of the new rulers towards crime and punishment was
just the opposite. To this effect, Mr. Fielding-Hall once observed, "To Englishman
punishment was a degradation, and it was difficult for him to restore the defender to his
normal status even if he may have atoned for the offence."*2* We shall see how such
conflict of values affected the administrative policy and practice later on.

and treatment. Even if the types of crime and the circumstances under which those
crimes were committed at the present day may take entirely different forms, an
understanding of their origin should be helpful in planning the said measures.

We shall now consider the administrative aspects of the problem-crime and its
relationship to the machinery of law and order. Crime in any form is a human
behaviour. A crime is constituted when a person violates the criminal law of his
society, which is enacted to protect the society and its members—their lives, properties,
and morals. The government takes action against any person who violates the said
criminal law. It is a function of government to maintain order. For this purpose, it
usually establishes a machinery of law enforcement: police; judiciary and prisons for
arrest, trial, conviction and punishment and/or rehabilitation. In order to protect the
individual against injustice, criminal laws contain specific provisions, governing the
procedures for arrest, trial, evidence, conviction, and punishment and rehabilitation.
These measures are taken with the assumption that if an action is so taken against a law
violator, the offender will be deterred from repeating such a law violation, and in the
same way other members of the community may also be deterred from crime. In this
way, society tries to keep crime under control. On the other hand, supposing the law.
is administered unjustly or erroneously, or if the affairs of the machinery are misma-
naged; in other words, if the policeman does not arrest the one whom he must, but does
so to the one whom he must not; or if the magistrate does not punish the one whom he
must, etc., in all cases because of the mistake of the fact, or of the law, or because of
corruption; or if the prisoner is not properly handled during and after his incarceration,
one can easily imagine what could be the outcome of the system.
It appears that the colonial government did all it could to strengthen the Police
and expand the Magistracy to achieve its main object of maintaining law and order.
Unfortunately, the Police could not command much confidence and respect of the
people. There may be many reasons. At one time, a Police Administration Report
stated that "the people could not co-operate with the Police on account of many sins of
omission and commission on the part of the latter"; apparently conceding to the alle-
gations, the Chief of Police lamented, "After all the Police Force or the bulk of it con-
sists of their own country men and it has been truly said that a country gets the Police it
deserves."39 The difficulties of running a police department as well as of the police work
are the obviously appreciable administrative problems. It will not therefore be fair to
attach any sort of blame to the Police Department, without taking those difficulties into
consideration. The defects of the system are therefore brought up into the purview of this
discussion merely to illustrate how they bear upon the general problem, the solution of
which was its main object.

as a measure to prevent recidivism. However, when the laws were set into motion,
