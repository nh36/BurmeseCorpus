HISTORY OF THE BURMA INDEMNITY (1826-1833).*
By the 5th article of the Treaty of Yandabo, which brought the First
Anglo-Burmese War (1824-1826) to an end, Bagyidaw King of Burma
agreed to pay to the British Government in India an indemnity of one crore
of
rupees. Major-General Sir Archibald Campbell, who imposed the treaty
upon the Burmese King, had at first demanded an indemnity of two crores of
rupees, but later in the negotiations he reduced it to one crore. By an
additional article it was agreed that upon payment of the first instalment of
25 lacs of rupees, the British Army would retire from Yandabo to Rangoon ;
upon payment of a second instalment within 100 days of the date of the
treaty the army would evacuate the dominions of His Majesty "leaving the
remaining moiety of the sum total to be paid by equal annual instalments in
two years, from this Twenty-fourth day of February 1826 A.D., through
the Consul or Resident in Ava or Pegu, on the part of the Honourable East
India Company."1
The King's treasury was empty, and he found it difficult even to pay
the first instalment in order to remove the British Army from close proxi-
mity to his capital. But it was a point of honour to free the country from
foreign occupation, hence the first instalment was quickly paid, and the
army withdrew to Rangoon. The Burma Government was able to scrape
together only 5 lacs of rupees the rest of the 20 lacs being supplied by the
Queen, as a loan. 2 Bagyidaw's grandfather and immediate predecessor
Bodawpaya (1782-1819) had accumulated a treasure of nearly 50,00,000
ticals of flowered silver, but all this money had been expended by Bagyidaw
in the removal of the capital to Ava, in building new palaces, and in the war.
Besides, Bagyidaw had no passion for accumulating treasure, and previous
to the war had not even levied contributions upon his subjects. Finally,
his country was essentially poor, partially cultivated, and thinly peopled
by a race not particularly marked by industry.3
"The financial system of the Government" says Crawfurd, in his
report of his Mission to Burma (1827), "is rude, barbarous, and inefficient,
beyond what can be easily believed. No regular land revenue, as
in other Asiatic countries, is collected on account of the sovereign, the
great majority of the lands being given away in Jageer to the members of
the royal family, to public officers, and to favourites in the form of pensions
or salaries, and a mere trifling being reserved for the King... No dis-
bursements in the shape of money are almost ever made from the treasury
as no money salary is paid to any officers from the highest to the lowest,-
all those who have no lands living as they can upon the produce of fees,
perquisites and extortions. Even the Government itself does not touch

The most considerable item of revenue was the port of Rangoon of
which the King's share did not exceed 3 lacs of ticals per year. 3 It was of
the utmost importance, therefore, to pay up the second instalment of the
indemnity and recover that port. This payment was completed by the 8th
December 1826 and on the following day the army under Sir A. Campbell
evacuated Rangoon. Lt. Rawlinson was, however, stationed in Rangoon
to act as agent for the protection of British trade and for receiving the
remaining instalments of the indemnity.
One-half of the indemnity was thus paid up within less than a year,
but the other half took more than six years to liquidate. Sufficient money
could not be raised because of the poverty of the country, and a good part of
what was raised went into the pockets of the collectors. Not being able to
pay the third and fourth instalments in time, and fearing the British might
take stern measures to enforce the claim, the Burma Government was even
at one time desirous of paying interest on the debt 1 ; finally, however, it was
decided to send an embassy to Calcutta to request the Governor-General to
agree to a delay in the payment of the balance due. 5
Two Burmese envoys, Thandozen Radanatazen dau Moo Meng Hla
Raja the senior, and Yewoon Nemyo-Nanda Thoo his junior arrived in
Calcutta on the 5th April 1827. They requested that the payment of the
third instalment be delayed two or three years and the fourth instalment by
a year longer. They pleaded poverty, and that after Rangoon was evacu-
ated by Campbell the Talaings rose in rebellion to crush which the King had
to spend 25 lacs of rupees. The Supreme Government referred the Envoys
to Sir Archibald Campbell then Commissioner of Tenasserim for a final set-
tlement of the question. Lord Amherst the Governor-General at the same
time decided to grant some indulgence to the Burmese in the payment
of the indemnity. They were to be asked to pay 10 lacs of rupees of
the 3rd instalment in cash or jewelry or timber by the 1st September
1827, and the remaining 15 lacs by September 1828; the 4th instalment of
25 lacs, it was proposed to postpone till September 1829. This decision was

When Burney arrived in Ava in April 1830 about 12 lacs of rupees
still remained to be paid to complete the indemnity; 12 it became, therefore,
one of his duties to press the Ministers of the King to discharge the obliga-
tion without delay. Without the services of Burney, in this matter, it is
doubtful if the payment would have been completed.
Burney found that the respectable Burmans disapproved of the delay in
the payment of the indemnity and blamed the officers for embezzling what
they collected.13 The King had given orders to pay off the 4th instalment
without further delay; but mere orders could not be effective because of the
poverty of the people and the empty Royal treasury. Only the Queen and
her brother Menthagyee were supposed to be wealthy, but the Government
had already borrowed 20 lacs from the former towards the payment of the
first instalment of the indemnity, and now she was pressing the Ministers to
repay the debt.14 Burney's task, therefore, was by no means an easy one.
On the 19th June 1830 Burney called upon the Ministers to fix the
date for the payment of the money due. They suggested February 1831
while Burney stuck to October 1830. Burney gives the following reason for
the attitude of the Ministers :-"The truth is the Burmese Government are
always obliged to watch for the proper time for squeezing the inhabitants.
The two most favourable periods are just after the paddy is sown in May or
June, and just when it is fit to cut in December and January. If contribu-
tions are demanded or exactions levied at any other season the inhabitants
escape into the woods or more into other parts of the country. It is an
established Burmese custom that a family is exempt from taxation during
the first year of their arrival at a new place."15
Burney explained quietly to the Ministers the danger of not paying the
indemnity quickly, and that the Supreme Government might take possession
of Rangoon; also that the King's claim over the Kubo Valley16 would be
inadmissible so long as the debt remained unpaid. This threat, however,
did not produce much effect, for Burney says in his Journal "After all I am
of opinion that unless our Government take some decisive measures
the 4th instalment will not be completed until February next."17

The Burma Government now made serious efforts to pay up the balance,
and by the 10th April 1832 were able to remit nearly 2 lacs of rupees to
Rangoon. According to the agreement the entire balance should have been
paid up by the 29th October 1832. This, however, they were unable to do.
The payment (a) was completed in February 1833.44 On a final calculation
made by Capt. Rawlinson, it was discovered that the Burma Government
had paid a surplus of Rs. 14,094 to the One Crore due. 44 This surplus was
ordered to be returned to the Ministers. 45 Thus finally was this long drawn
out question settled. It is doubtful if the Company would have received the
payment in full without the presence of the Resident in Ava and the ser-
vices of Henry Burney.
