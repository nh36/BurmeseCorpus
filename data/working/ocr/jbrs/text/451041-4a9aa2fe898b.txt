THE LINGUISTIC POSITION OF RONG (LEPCHA)
by
R. A. D. FORREST
STEN KONOW, in the Linguistic Survey of India assigned Rong to the " unpronomi-
nalised Himalayan group" of the Tibeto-Burman languages, though he did indeed note
certain traits (vigesimal numeration, vestigial classification of nouns into animate and
inanimate, etc.) which point to an ancient influence of languages of Munda type. He is
followed by Schmidt in die Sprachfamilien und Sprachenkreise der Erde (Heidelberg, 1926),
who, however, assigned to Rong a position apart from the other unpronominalised
languages, justified by its sharp differences from the dialects with which Konow ranged it;
and the Langues du Monde (1952) likewise follows Stonow's classification. So far as
classification goes this is probably as accurate as one may expect; but in calling Rong a
Tibeto-Burman language without qualification we are giving too little weight to its peculiar
features.
The principal source of our knowledge of Rong is Mainwaring's Dictionary (Berlin,
1898), a work generally satisfactory. In it the author has marked those words which he
recognised as being of Tibetan derivation, i.e., plainly traceable to the Tibetan written
language or to the modern Lhasa pronunciation; omissions are rare, and questionable
inclusions among the marked words are uncommon. As examples of this stratum we may
quote the following:

It is to be noted i) that among these asterisked forms there are none with initial dentals
(including./) + y; ii) that asterisked forms constitute a much larger proportion among
words with initial aspirated consonants than elsewhere; and iii) that compound initial
consonants are very rare among the asterisked forms though common elsewhere. The
significance of these facts will appear later.
There is, however, a much older TB stratum in Rong which bears witness to a
phase of TB development anterior to that preserved in the oldest written Tibetan. Many
of the features here preserved in something like their original forms had been already
reached by comparison of the modern TB dialects; some throw light on the forms restored
for Karlgren's "Archaic "stage of Chinese. Among these are the following:

fixes as those languages have in common with each other. If there remains any doubt as

Tibeto-Burman (or Tibetan?) stratum is the more fundamental. The word order, part of
the basic vocabulary, the use of modification of finals in word-formation, and the agentive
suffix all tell in favour of Tibeto-Burman affinities; while the extensive use of syllabic
prefixes and a large part of the basic vocabulary are equally clearly Austroasiatic. There
is no trace of the -n- infix denothing the instrument in many Austroasiatic dialects, while
on the other hand the -- regularly used in Rong to form the causative of verbs has a
parallel only in Chinese, where it is employed sparingly. Possibly the phonetic tendencies
of the language may be a truer index of its fundamental type; here an antipathy to
aspirated initials (which seem to arise in Austroasiatic generally as a result of composition)
and to palatalisation (far commoner in Tibeto-Burman than in Austroasiatic, though by no
means unknown in the latter), as well as the richer vocalism and the development of labial
fricatives (Riang, Palaung, Miao, &c) all point to an Austroasiatic substratum. How-
ever the Rong language be classified (and in such a
case this must be a matter of
practical convenience rather than of objective fact), there is no doubt as to its great
importance as an aid towards recovery of the early phases of Tibetan and of the Tibeto-
Burman languages generally.
