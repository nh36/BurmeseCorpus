BURMESE ATTITUDES TOWARD CHILDREN
by
GEORGE A. THEODORSON*
Introduction
In this paper the writer will present some statistical data involving the cooperative
effort of 486 Burmese students at the University of Rangoon while the writer was a visiting
lecturer of sociology in the Department of Anthropology in 1958-59. Daw Khin Mar Mar
supervised the distribution of the questionnaires, and with U Maung Maung Sein were the
major collaborators of the Burmese study. Many Burmese students from my class at the
University gracefully offered their assistance and were very actively engaged in various
ways in making the research possible. Miss Charity Sein U and Daw Kyi Kyi (the latter
is now in the Department of Far Eastern History) were of invaluable assistance throughout
the project.
In this paper only those questions which deal with the college students' attitudes
toward children will be discussed. Other material will be published at a future date. In
all, there were eighty items to which the Burmese students were asked to indicate their
attitudes. In the data dealt with in this paper (except for one question) the students were
given a Likert-type statement and asked to indicate with a check (√) whether they “strong-
ly agree," "agree,” were “uncertain,” “disagree," or "strongly disagree." The questionnaire
administered to the Burmese students has also been used to gather the opinions of students
in the United States, India, Malaya, Puerto Rico, and currently in Austria where the
author is for the year 1962-63 a Visiting Professor of Sociology at the University of Vienna.
Hence that small portion of the Burmese material presented in this paper is a part of a
world wide study of family structure, and therefore represents a segment of a larger study.
In this report we shall be interested in the attitudes of university students (249 men and
237 women) to statements relating to children.
Results
Liking children. In order to give us our initial reference point in examining the
Burmese university students' attitudes toward children, let us look at their reaction to the
statement "I like children." The results of this very general item show that 92.4% of the
men agreed with this statement, and only 2.8% indicated disagreement. ** Three percent
(3.6%) of the men were uncertain and 1.2% did not respond to the question. Ninetythree

The role of children in stabilizing marriage. The respondents not only indicated
that they liked children, but they also indicated that a stable marriage involved having
children. Ninety-two percent of the Burmese women and 83.2% of the men agreed that
"Children make a marriage more stable."
It is certainly clear that the Burmese students interviewed do regard children as a
stabilizing force in marriage. However, one may wonder why there are fewer Burmese
men agreeing than women. That there is a greater tendency for the men to be uncertain
or to disagree that children make marriage more stable is indicated in the fact that 7.6% of
the men studied were "uncertain" compared to only 2.9% of the women, and that 7.6% of
the men expressed disagreement that children bring stability to marriage, as compared to
only 3.4% of the women. From this preliminary analysis of the data we are not yet in a
position to explain this difference.
The person I marry must be interested in having children. To what extent do the
Burmese college students interviewed continue to maintain the traditional view of marriage
and reject the idea of a "childless marriage?” The results show a relatively traditional
response in that 88.4% of the men and 91.2% of the women agree that "The person I
marry must be interested in having children." There is no greater tendency for the men
or the women to more "strongly agree" with the statement. About 50% of both the men
and women “strongly agree" that the person they marry must be interested in having
children. These Burmese university students quite definitely view their own marriage as
including children. Only a few do not demand that their mate must be interested in
having children. The 4.0% of the men and the 2.5% of the women who disagree with the
statement represent but a very small proportion of the students. These students who
disagree with the statement do not however necessarily favor a "childless marriage.”
They may simply reflect a flexible attitude toward the personality, special situation, and
outlook of the kind of individual they might conceivably marry.
Are children the main purpose of the husband-wife relationship? While the respon-
dents quite definitely feel that children are necessary in marriage, it is interesting to inquire
a little further to see if the same proportion of the students agree that “Children are the
main purpose of the husband-wife relationship,” as agree with the statement that "The
person I marry must be interested in having children.”
The data generally indicate that the majority of the students agree that children are
the main purpose of the husband-wife relationship, which is indeed in line with a generally
traditional view of the family. On the other hand we also find that fewer of the students
tend to agree that children are the main purpose of marriage than agree with the statement
that the person they marry must be interested in having children. Only 66.6% of the men
agree that children are the main purpose of the husband-wife relationship and
67.1% of the women. (The small difference between the men and women here is not, of
course, significant.) The interpretation of why fewer students agree with this question

However, the fact that 19% of the Burmese men and women actually disagree that children
are the main purpose of the husband-wife relationship (19.7% of the men and 19.4% of the

It should also be noted in passing that in reference to this particular statement
there were a relatively large number of students who neither agreed nor disagreed but
checked the "uncertain" category. Twelve percent of both the men and the women (12.9%
of the men and 12.7% of the women) indicated uncertainty. This relatively high degree
of uncertainty, at the very least, does not indicate a strongly held traditional point of view,
and therefore differentiates these respondents from those students who agree that children
are the main purpose of the husband-wife relationship.
The belief in planned parenthood through the use of modern methods. In response to
the statement "I believe in planned parenthood through the use of modern methods,"
sixty-one percent of the men agreed, 15.7% were “uncertain," and 21.3% disagreed, with
2% not answering the question. Fewer of the women agreed with planned parenthood of
this type. Forty-eight percent of the women agreed, 24.9% were uncertain, and 24.9%
disagreed. Depending on one's frame of reference one might say that the Burmese
university student is much more, or much less in favor of planned parenthood than one
expected, or than one thinks is proper. At any rate, it is quite clear that there is a greater
division of the students on this subject than on some others. This very short report does
not deal with the characteristics of the students who agree with this statement in compa-
rison to those who disagree. Future reports will have to deal with more detailed type of
analysis.
The ideal number of children desired. The respondents were asked "Ideally, how
many children would you like to have?" The average number of children desired "ideally"
(arithmetic mean) is 4.3% children for the men and 3.9% for the women. The modal
number of children desired ideally by the men is five and for the women four. It is
interesting to note that while the women less favor the statement on planned parenthood
than do the men, they appear to desire somewhat fewer children than the men.
It is a mother's duty to devote herself completely to her children even if this hampers her
own interests? (hobbies, reading, etc.)” Seventy-six (76.8) percent of the men agree and 89.2%
of the women agree with the statement. This degree of agreement on the part of both the
men and the women is interesting. However, it is also interesting to note that as many as
14.9% of the men and 21.5% of the women disagree. In addition, the fact that more of
the women disagree than the men seems to indicate a non-traditional response on the part
of a surprising number of Burmese women.

Should a woman with young children go out to work? The Burmese men less favor
a woman going out to work when she has young children than do the Burmese women.
Seventy-eight (78.3%) percent of the men agree that "A woman with young children
should not go out to work," whereas only 62% of the women agree. While the
majority of the Burmese students interviewed do not approve of women with young chil-
dren going out to work, the fact that 11.7% of the men and 17.3% of the women disagree
with the statement may indicate a possible source of disharmony in some husband-wife
relationships.
Should a woman resume her career after her children are grown? This is an interes-
ting question that has bothered university women all over the world. What should they
"do" with their education? Do they work only prior to marriage? Should they maintain
an interest in going back to "their career," (for which they have been trained) after their
children are grown? Many college women have asked themselves such questions as they
do their studying.
College men may not have given as much thought to this question as the women,
but in examining their responses it is interesting to find that as many as 48.1% of the men
agreed that "A wife should resume her career after her children are grown," 15.7% were
uncertain, and 34.6% disagreed. It seems quite clear that there is little agreement and
quite a split among the men on this question. The women are also divided on their
feelings regarding this statement. Fifty-seven (56.5) percent of the women agreed that a
wife should resume her career after her children are grown, 14.8% were uncertain, and
27.0% disagreed. It is instructive to note that the women are more likely to agree than
the men. It is perhaps natural that women should be more likely to feel that they should
make use of their education, since they must put so much time, effort, and money into the
process.
While it is to be expected that the college student upon marriage will modify his
attitudes, it is nevertheless also true that the heterogeneous views held by the university
population studied, does suggest the possibility of future marital conflict in certain areas.
It is also quite possible that their differing viewpoints on some of these subjects, between
and among men and women even prior to marriage is a reflection of already existing
husband-wife role conflict in Burmese society.
Conclusion
This very brief report on Burmese university students' attitudes toward children is
presented with the knowledge of its limitations. The difficulty of interpreting the data at
this point is related to the early stage that has been reached in the processing of the data.
As more data is prepared for publication, it will be possible to control on various factors
to better analyze the material available. The purpose of this paper is to begin sharing a
few of the statistical findings I and my Burmese friends and colleagues have gathered on the
Burmese family, with those who are interested in the sociology of Burmese society.
