Prayers of Ancient Burma.*
It is an honour indeed to be invited, for a second time, to address a
meeting of the National Council of Women in Burma, which is doing so
much, amongst other things, to enlarge sympathies and understandings
amongst the peoples of Burma. I have chosen a subject which, I hoped,
would help, if ever so little, in the same direction. Years ago, when I first
began to take an interest in Burma, I set to work to study Burmese. But
though I managed to scramble through the official examinations, I soon
found myself up against an imaginary, but very real, wall. Perhaps some of
you may have had a similar experience. One learns the language well enough
perhaps to say the ordinary things and converse on unimportant topics, but
one cannot learn it well enough to express one's deeper feelings or meanings,
nor to understand more than a fraction of those of the Burmans with whom
one is conversing. One cannot even make a joke in Burmese, except.
unintentionally. At a pwè one follows the general drift and catches a stray
remark or two, but one misses most of those enormous jests that convulse
the audience. One can read, more or less, a Burmese newspaper or a modern
novel, but it is a sad struggle to make sense of famous Burmese prose
works, and as for Burmese poetry it remains almost wholly unintelligible.
Mr. J. S. Furnivall, a really good scholar of Burmese, once quoted in the
Journal of the Burma Research Society a line from a Burmese poem which,
if I remember right, ran something as follows:-
-
"Dark, sub-opaque, blue, dusk-brown, very dingy, black."
Apparently the right meaning was as follows:-
"The place is dim and grey, the darkness spreads.”
But he wondered why it might not equally well mean:-
"
"My darling is dusky and distant; more ominous night by night.'
That instance perhaps will suffice to shew how finally even good
scholars come bang against a wall in studying Burmese, and wonder if it is
worth while to persevere.
-
Personally, I cannot claim to have solved any of these difficulties, but I
have stumbled on a way round one of them, which satisfies me at any rate,
and provides a never failing stimulus to further study of the language and
the country. It consists in studying it historically, beginning with the oldest
literature the 12th-13th century inscriptions, and following up the growth,
at once of the literature and of the people. I am afraid you may think this
course either a very dry, or a very difficult one. I have found it neither. And
now that a good deal of the pioneer work is over, it is getting easier and
easier. Ideas, at the dawn of a literature, are simple: there is nothing subtle or
recondite about Old Burmese. The vocabulary is small, and as the subject of
the inscriptions is usually the dedication of a pagoda or offerings to it, one

A very little knowledge of history or Burmese suffices to launch one
comfortably on these inscriptions. The rest one discovers as one goes. The
main dynasty of Pagan begins with Anorahta, from about 1044 A.D.; but it
is only in the reign of his third successor, Alaungsithu, that Burmese inscrip-
tions begin with the Myazedi, about 1113 A.D. They do not become numerous
till near the end of that century, when king Narapatisithu first adopted
Burmese as the language of royal inscriptions. Thereafter, in the reigns of
Nadaungmya, Kyazwa, Tayokpye, etc., there are lots of them year by year,
some four hundred in all down to the fall of Pagan and the siege of Myin-
zaing by the Mongols in 1301. Burmese dates are given of course in the
thekkayit era; if one adds 638, it gives one approximately the Christian
year.
A particular charm is found in the inscriptions set up by the ladies of
Pagan. There are plenty of them; and there is ample evidence to shew that
Burmese women were then anything but depressed classes. From the
Buddhist point of view, of course, a woman's lot is definitely inferior to a
man's. A Pagan palace-lady (List 374a³) prays that she may be
reborn as a male. Nor was society monogamous; but the lesser wives (poc),
who are frequently mentioned together with their children and property,
seem to have found their lot tolerable. A joint dedication is mentioned (List
404a³) by a minister and his two wives. Widows sometimes remarried:
there is one instance where a lady mentions "my former husband" (List
9012). Love affairs were not unknown: there is mention of a son of a minister
and the great minister Sirivaḍhana's concubine who "became lovers"
(ǝg List 34920). There is plenty of evidence of married happiness,
if only in its converse-lovelorn husbands or wives who made dedications on
the occasion of their spouse's death (e.g. List 35022); at least one husband
finds it not improper to pray "May I and my wife without fail get the boon
of Buddhahood" (P.M. Stone 85 S.13).

A pleasing feature about Old Burmese inscriptions is their simplicity.
They have none of the high-flown mythological content of the normal Indian
inscription. Here is a typical specimen, an inscription set up by Kyazwa's
aunt :-
-
"In 597s., on Thursday the 4th waxing of Naṁkā, king Klacwā ascended
the throne. 'I never knew my mother. My aunt brought me up, and so I
grew. Truly my aunt has been a mother to me.' So saying, he built a house
and gave it me to live in. He gave me also household slaves and rice fields.
I live in the house. I employ the slaves. I eat the fields. But it were better,
I think, for a pious and wise monk to live in the house than me. With this
intent I built a cave-temple; and when it was finished I told the king, the
future Buddha: 'Dear my lord, the house you built and gave your servant is
very pleasant. But I thought it better that a monk should live there than we,
your servants. So I have built a cave-temple, and the house has become a
monastery. And the household slaves and fields you gave your servant, I
dedicate." When I told him this, the king commanded and said: 'Let my aunt
place there a worthy monk.' In 615s., on Friday the 6th waxing of Tabodwè,
I installed the monk in the monastery and moved out. Then I dedicated the
household slaves and fields..." Names of the slaves and details of the lands.
follow, and the inscription concludes with the usual curse: "If anyone steals

Some of the curses imprecated on those who injure the offering have a
homely touch about them: "May they be charred stumps in hell"-a metaphor
which seems to be taken from taungya cultivation; or again "May they
cleave to hell as burnt rice to the ricepot" (L/s 39322). Or again:-"May
the rice and curry that they eat, the water that they drink, the homes they
live in, turn to poison. When they mean to strike a young male elephant,
may they be struck themselves by a hundred (?). When they see a snake,
and mean to kill it, may they be killed themselves" (List 81b21). Other
curses are more august: "May they be destroyed and ground to powder, even
as the great earth and royal mountains when the world is wrecked by cosmic
hurricane. May they not full a place of abiding" (List 18521). Gangasū's
wife lets herself go:-
"Whoso among my relatives, the king's relatives, or outsiders, monks,
laymen, men or women, robs or violates one slave, one acre, among the
slaves and fields which I have offered, may his glory not be great as a man,
nor his life long. The king's wrath be upon him. The axe of lightning smite
him. Danger of fire, danger of water, dangers of elephant, horse, serpent,

Sometimes the length of days in each of the major hells is defined in
comparison with a day on this planet. A day, for instance, in Sañjiva
(Theinzo) hell is the equivalent of 9 million human years; no day in any hell
is shorter than that.
Let me now give you some specimens or extracts from prayers, each of
which seems to me to have a personal quality. Here is a minister's prayer :-
"Meantime, ere I reach Buddhahood, I want the happiness excited by
drum and trumpet, and the five instruments of music. Therefore I dedicate
the following players on big drums and small. ... Whensoever I am born, I
do not want the misery of hunger and thirst. Therefore I offer to the Lord
Buddha and the monks living in this monastery the following paddy fields....
I want to rebel against this world of slavery to appetite. Therefore I offer
also a hundred slaves to serve the needs of the Lord Buddha and the
monks" (List 123a, ll. 14-31).
Here is a king's dedication:-
"I, Nâtonmya the king, have built a splendid temple, a library and a
monastery, and offered a troupe of dancers, gold and silver popcorn, oil-
lamps and rice-alms, in order to cause my lords the monks to apply their
minds to love" (mittābhāvanā-List 273a, 1.3).
Here is the inscription of a provincial lady whose ideas tend to play
leapfrog over each other :-
"I, Gloria, have built a monastery, and on the full moon of Tabaung
560s., I dedicated it-3 monasteries, 3 tanks, altogether, monastic robes,
6 calabashes, 1 lump of lime, a wishing-tree, a cow. Wheresoever I
wander in Samsara, I want misery, I don't want hell. I want the grace of
perfection. And at the end of Samsara, I want to reach Nirvana. May all the
coolies share my merit. I have given oil-lamps. And I have given kathina
robes; 40 sets of kathina requisites; 20 kettles of food; 4 cups; 5 almsbowls;
1 bell; 20 mamooties (?). I don't want to know the slightest little bit !"
(Ye-ngwe inscription, Mahlaing).
