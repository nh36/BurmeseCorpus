BUTANOL OUT OF RICE-STARCH *
by

I. INTRODUCTION
Mc Cutchan and Hickey (1954)' stated in their review that the Butanol-
Acetone Fermentations are true fermentations in the sense that they are
anaerobic. In one case, the organism that brings about the fermentation of the
carbohydrate, starch, is Clostridium acetobutylicum; and the products of
fermentation are: (1) n-butyl alcohol (I-butanol) or n-butanol), (2) acetone
(propanone), and (3) ethyl alcohol (ethanol).
During World War 1, there was a great demand for acetone and none for
butanol and the storage of the latter was then a problem. At present, esters
of butanol like acetate are useful as lacquers for automobiles and for other
industries. One more use to which butanol can be put is in synthesizing rub-
ber by polymerization of butadiene, since the diene that is required for poly-
merization can be produced from butanol.
Historically it was Pasteur who observed butanol for the first time as a
product of microbial action. After Pasteur came such workers as Fitz, Trecul,
Gruber, Grimbert, Beijerinck, Van Tieghem, Perdrix, Bredemann, Duclaux
and many others. But, it was Prazmowski who proposed the name of
"clostridium" for these spore forming anaerobes; and, it was Schardinger
(1905) who discovered that acetone was also a fermentation product.
The polymerization of butadiene as a step to synthetic rubber was
started in Europe in 1909. Strange and Graham Ltd. was able to obtain
the able services of Perkin, Weizmann, Fernbach and Schoen. Among them,
it was Weizmann who in 1912 - 14 found an organism which fermented a
variety of starchy raw materials including corn and produced butanol and
acetone.

(b) Anaerobic jar. Anaerobic jar is one in which oxygen has been replaced
by some other gases like hydrogen or nitrogen so that organisms like Clo-
stridia that thrive only under very low pressure of oxygen or better in the
absence of it can be cultured in it. It should be big enough to hold several
test-tubes and must be fitted with the inlet and outlet for hydrogen that comes
from a generation flask through water (to keep back HC1) and Sulfuric acid
(to keep back H2 O). It should also be of such convenient size as to be kept
in a laboratory-incubator.
(c) Sterile Soil. Dry spore stock has to be raised on sterile soil. This
soil-stock method was reported by Bredemann. Thaysen (1924) applied the
principle to stock cultures of the Weizmann butyl organism, but used acid
washed sand instead of soil. However, the use of neutral garden soil has
become general with most workers in the handling of butyl anaerobes indust-
rially. Here in our laboratory, mixture was made in the ratio:- sand:loam: :
3:2. A word of caution is needed on the sterilization of the soil. Drying in an
air oven is to be avoided since it will char the organic matter of the soil.

Autoclaving is therefore almost indispensible, but dry soil is very difficult to
sterilize. There is always air pocketed in the spaces of the soil, which surface is
now wetted. It usually takes about 10 hours at 15 lbs. steam pressure to
sterilize tubes of soil. The sterilization can be speeded up by moistening
(but not water-logging) the soil with a few mls. of water, added after the soil is
placed in tubes. Steam from the interstitial water of the soil drives out the
pocketed air from the soil and in this way hastens the sterilization. Here, in the
laboratory, sterilizing the soil was done successively for 3 days, each time at
15 lbs pressure/1 hr. After this sterilizing treatment, sterility test was made.
Only when no growth was observed in the nutrient agar and peptone water
that was inoculated with soil, the latter was accepted as sterile. It has to be
emphasized here that sterile soil was prepared so as to enable the culture
merely to be carried on it in dormant form. In other words, what we had
received from India in the form of one tube, we wanted to multiply into
several stock-soil tubes. Each tube out of several others, then, serve as carrier
of spores to be transferred when so desired to cultural broths, mashes,
starters or the main batch media.
(d) Agar Slopes, etc. For sterility test to be made on stock-soil, agar
slopes as well as peptone-water are needed. It is very important that random
samples be taken out of stock - soil tubes and that the samples so drawn be
subjected to sterility test before any batch of soil is accepted for stock culture
use. Nutrient Agar was made as usual. 1 lb. minced and trimmed veal was
steeped in I liter of cold water,0° C,overnight and boiled next morning for 15

0.5% Na Cl; and the whole medium was now adjusted to pH 7.6, raised to
temperature 85°C and filtered. To this filtrate was made 1.5% Agar that *
was dissolved by boiling. Medium was distributed into tubes and auto-
claved (15 lbs. /40 min.). The tubes were left lying slantly on the floor. The
Agar slopes thus formed were used for sterility test.
(e) Vacuum desiccators.Vacuum in desiccators can be created by pump
as well as by sprouted rice plants that use up oxygen on growing.
(f) Aerobic agar plates. Melted nutrient agar from the tubes was poured
into petri dishes and allowed to form a plate, neither too moist nor too dry.
(g) Saline. Sterilized physiological (0.9%) was used.
(h) Glucose solutions. They were prepared with known percentages and
sterilized in the flowing steam for 1 hr. successively for 3 days.

III. BUTANOL PRODUCTION
It may not be out of place to record here that the butanol production was
carried out at the first suggestion of U Thein Nyun, my immediate senior
in research at the Applied Chemistry Research Department UBARI. If it be
possible to manufacture starch³ from cheap grade Burma rice, will it still be
possible to produce butanol out of that starch ? This paper is an attempt to
answer that question.
The production of butanol was fraught with difficulties. Even on opening
the NCL tube containing organism on sterile soil, due precautions had to be
taken. A scratch with file was made on the NCL ampoule and this scratch was
touched with the red-hot tip of Pasteur pipette. A crack was thus formed along
the scratch and the ampoule with a scratch thus formed was left for at least
6 hours during which atmospheric air filled in the void or vacuum in the
ampoule. Then only, the neck of the ampoule was broken,
The transfer of the organism-bearing soil into the medium filling 2/3 of the
beverage bottle was the next step. This step of transfer is called, in this paper,
"recovering the organism."
After opening the NCL ampoule containing organism, water must not be
added to the soil in the ampoule since the water, it being hypotonic, will
quickly plasmolyse the spores. 0.9% saline solution was therefore used to
"recover the organism." A few mls. of this physiological saline was pipetted
into the ampoule and the organism-bearing soil was stirred and transferred
by the same pipette into the beverage bottle containing the corn mash medi-
um. Incubation was done at 37°C for 48 hours, at the end of which, the bottle
was heat shocked at 100° C for 2 minutes; and a few mls. of the medium
(now subsiding after active fermentation) was transferred to another beverage
bottle containing similar medium and incubation followed similarly for 48
hours. Thus, heat shocking was done just before each transfer, followed with
incubation and testing under microscope for stray outside organisms (micros-
copically); and, altogether nearly 10 transfers were completed before the

IV.
RESULTS
The medium, now containing butanol as well as living dormant organism
should, strictly working, be filtered through special filters and then only
should be distilled. In our case, since we had no Seitz filters with EK special
pads, that can retain small bacteria on it, we simply distilled the medium in
the usual fashion. This simply means, we assume Cl. acetobutylicum to be
fairly non-pathogenic. Our observation on the distillation was semi-
quantitative. With an idea to get a comparative result we carefully and
separately collected the distillates from two sources (rice and corn) and
to 100 ml. of each distillate, we added (NH 4) 2 SO 4 enough to salt out
the butanol that, on standing, floated to the top. (NH4)2 SO 4 was
dispensed with when we found that fractional distillation was better method
to isolate butanol.
Our comparative observation pointed out that rice starch acted upon
with the strain from India and assisted with biotin produced larger amount of
butanol than corn starch did under similar conditions.
