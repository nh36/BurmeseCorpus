BOOK REVIEW
Theravada Buddhism in Burma
by Nihar-ranjan Ray.
၂၆၃
This is another book by Dr. Ray of Calcutta
University on Indo-Burmese historical and cultural
relations from the earliest times to the British conquest.
From the research point of view, chapter one, which deals
with the earliest times is the most interesting. Here the
author shows himself to be a diligent seeker after truth
and displays considerable resourcefulness in reconstructing
the early history of the religion. For instance, although
he does not accept it as a fact of history, he does not brush
aside, as do most European scholars, the Sinhalese story
of the Asoka mission of Sona and Uttara to Suvanna-
bhúmi as a myth, simply because it is not mentioned in
Asoka's Edicts. When the story of the mission to Kasmira
and the Himalayan countries, though not supported by
Asoka's edicts, is upheld by other independent sources,
should we not look for other sources for the introduction
of Buddhism by Asoka into Burma also?
In looking for such sources, Dr. Ray finds one
of the inscriptions at Nagarjunikonda in the Andhra
country of the Deccan, dated 3rd century A. D. and dedi-
cated for the benefit of the fraternities (of monks) of
Tambapamna who had converted Kasmira, Gandhara,
Cina, Cilata, Tosali, Avaramta, Vanga, Vanavàsi, Yavana,
Danila, Palura and the Isle of Tambapamni.' Dr. Ray
takes the Cilàtas to be the Kiratas of Sanskrit literature
and identifies their country with Arakan and Lower
Burma (i.e. Suvannabhumi). He arrives at this conclusion,
assuming that Kirata be identified with Suvannabhumi:-
'We have then in the Nagarjunikonda record a definite
reference to the prevalence of Buddhism in Burma some-
time before the third century A. D.' (p. 18).
Dr. Ray next discusses Chinese references to the
existence of Buddhism in the 3rd century A. D. in a
country called Lin-yang, which scholars think was situated
in the north or centre of Burma. Dr. Ray suggests that
it refers to Hmawza, the site of the capital of old Prome.

Then Dr. Ray mentions Buddhaghosa or one of
his celebrated contemporaries as infusing new life into
the religion in Lower Burma in the 5th century. He says
"There is nothing antecedently improbable in the Buddha-
ghosa tradition' (p. 84).
In connection with the earliest times the account
of Tapussa and Bhallika and the founding of the Shweda-
gon Pagoda at Rangoon should have been discussed. We
would have liked to know why Dr. Ray omitted to do so
and what he would have made of this account, which is
also interesting from the literary point of view.
Dr. Ray gives an admirable resume of the avai
lable evidences-epigraphic, Chinese, archaeological-about
Buddhism in old Prome. We wish that 'the record in
beautiful Sanskrit verse....the first of its kind in Burma....
set up by King Jayacandravarman .... with the express
purpose of establishing and enhancing peace, amity and
good will' between the king and his younger brother at
Prome, a summary of which is given by the author, would
soon be published, so that we may compare it with Anan-
dacandra's Sanskrit inscription of Arakan, dated by the
late Prof. E. H. Johnston at about 700 A.D. (Bulletin
of the School of Oriental and African Studies, London,
Vol. XI, part 2).
From the introduction of Theravada to Pagan
in 1057 onwards, the author is more happy about his
materials and tells his story with more confidence, though
in the latter part a certain amount of monotony is inevi-
table. This monotony might have been relieved if the
author had taken the trouble to assess the contents of the
Pali works, which he mentions in such profusion. Had
he done so, he might perhaps also have seen fit to modify this
statement that 'Burma has hardly any creative contribut-
ion to Buddhist logic and metaphysics to her credit'
(p.262).
It is difficult to avoid inaccuracies and hasty
remarks in a book of this kind, which is based on so many
references. We might mention a few instances.
We would like to know the authority for the
statement that the famous Shwemawdaw Pagoda at Pegu
was one of the Pagodas built by Razadirit. (p. 176).

It is, therefore, not at all unlikely that the
Burmese and their early champions, the Pagan Kings,
deliberately ignored or suppressed whatever the Talaings
had contributed towards the introduction and propagation
of the faith in Pagan and Upper Burma'-says the author
on p. 156. How would he reconcile this with the fact that
Kyanzittha used the Talaing language in his inscriptions
not only at Pagan but also in Lower Burma?
But these are small blemishes in an otherwise
creditable performance. Dr. Ray is to be congratulated
on writing a more or less connected narrative of Theravada
in Burma based on a huge mass of evidence which he has
taken so much pains to collect and study. We in Burma
who lost our libraries during the last war appreciate
the many references to and quotations from books and
periodicals given in the book.
P. M. T.
