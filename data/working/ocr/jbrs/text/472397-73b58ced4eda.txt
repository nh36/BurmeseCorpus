A GEOGRAPHICAL PROFILE OF THE ANDAMAN AND
NICOBAR ISLANDS
Lying between Cape Negrais of Burma
and Sumatra Island of Indonesia, the
Andaman and Nicobar Islands form an
island arc. The two groups of islands
are 90 miles apart from each other. The
area marks the continuation between the
Arakan Yoma of Burma and the mountain
back-bone of Sumatra. The islands used
to form one of the minor provinces of
British India, with the Chief Commissioner
residing at Port Blair. The two archipelagoes
were administratively united by the British
in 1872. They are now regarded as an
external territory of India. The Chief
Commissioner is nowadays advised by
five local councillors. During the Second
World War, the Japanese occupied the
islands to use them as advanced air-bases.
The Andamans differ from the Nicobars
both physically and culturally. Racially
and culturally, the Nicobarese are not as
primitive as the Andamanese. Many of
the animal species of the Andamans differ
from those of the neighbouring Nicobars.
Both the Andamans and Nicobars have
deep narrow inlets, pointing to a sub-
mergence of the above islands in the
recent geological past.
1. The Andamans
a. Location. The islands lie 120 miles
from Cape Negrais, the nearest land from
them, and 240 miles from the Burmese
harbour town of Bassein. However, they
are located 780 miles from Calcutta and
740 miles from Madras. Latitudinally,
they are found between 10° N and 14° N
and longitudinally between 92° E and 94° E.
They separate the Bay of Bengal in
the west from the Andaman Sea in the
east. They have a north-north-east to
south-south-west alignment.
The Indian government think they
are strategically important for the defence
of the eastern seaboard of India. Indeed,
because of their commanding position
over and proximity to the Burmese coast,
they are even more important for the
coastal defence of Burma.
b. Size. They extend 219 miles from
north-north-east to south-south-east and
32 miles from east to west. The total
area of the Andamans amounts to 2500
square miles. The three main islands,
namely North, Middle and South Andaman
and nearby smaller ones are collectively
known as Great Andaman.1
They lie
close to each other. The Great Andaman
contains 5 of the largest islands of the
Andaman region. Little Andaman, forming
the southern extremity of the Andaman
group, is 26 miles long and 16 miles
wide and is about 35 miles away from
Great Andaman. Duncan Passage lies
between Great Andaman and Little Anda-
man.

The northern part of the Andaman

conducted after 1920-21. The southern

Geology. There are no fossils to help
the dating of rocks (largely sandstones,
limestones, clays and some serpentine).
But, it is believed rocks of this region
are mainly Eocene in age. There are
also small areas of older sedimentary
rocks. These contain large masses of
serpentine and areas of limestone, pro-
bably Miocene in age. Newer rocks are
mostly confined to Ritchie's Archipelago
and they contain radiolarians and fora-
minifera. Narcodam and Barren Islands
to the east of the main chain have a
volcanic origin.
In parts of the Andamans (and also
in Nicobar Islands), shallow wells, with
a depth of 10 to 15 feet, sunk in recent
coral rock, often only a short distance
from the sea-coast, give fresh water. It
seems the flow of under ground water
towards the sea is held back by denser

Andaman Sea, phosphate deposits, still
in the process of formation, are present.
d. Physical Features. The Andaman Is-
lands have been much dissected and thus
are generally hilly. However, there are few
perennial streams. Great Andaman has a
mass of hills with narrow valleys between
them. The highest peak, namely Saddle
Peak (2400') is found on North Andaman.
Little Andaman is almost flat and is prac-
tically devoid of perennial streams.

Its climate is tropical. The tempera-
ture remains at 85°F or thereabout. It is
lowest in February and June and highest in
March, April and May. The average mini-
mum temperature is 66.7°F and the average
maximun temperature 96°F. The tropical
warmth is here tempered by seabreezes.
Rainfall is rather irregular. It occurs much
more during the Southwest monsoon (i.e.
from the middle of May to the middle of
November) than during the Northeast mon-
soon. The annual rainfall amounts to
about 100 inches.
The islands experience monsoonal
effects. But, there is no real dry season,
their location being close to the Equator or
Equatorial Doldrum Belt.

The British came to show their interests
in the Andamans by 1784, i.e. after the
conclusion of the Peace of Versailles, as a
potential naval base for curbing the Dutch
power in the Malay region. In 1789, Capt.
Archibald Blair, under instruction from the
Government of Bengal, established a penal
colony at the Port Blair area, then known
as Port Cornwallis. Two years later, the
colony was transferred to the north eastern
part of Great Andaman and the new colony
was given the name Port Cornwallis. A
naval arsenal was to be established there.
But, the scheme fell through in 1796.
After March, 1824, the British troops
secretly assembled at a rendezvous in the
Andaman Islands and were able to navigate
up the Rangoon River and seize the Bur-
mese city without a fight in a suprise move
in May, the same year.
In 1844, the British troop ships
Briton and
Runnymede were driven
ashore in the Andamans and their crew
members were massacred by the Andama-
nese. The year 1855 saw the formation of
a project to establish a settlement and penal
station in the region.
As a result of the 1857 Indian Munity,
the British came to feel the need for a penal
settlement for the Indian prisoners of the
mutiny. In 1858, a penal settlement was
once again established at Port Blair. In the
beginning, the sickness and mortality rates
among the settlers were high. But, ultimate-
ly as a result of the reclamation of swamps
and clearance of jungles, the rates declined.

gabbro and serpentine, are also present.
These masses probably are of Cretaceous

Earth-quakes of great violence accompanied
by tidal waves took place in 1847 and 1881.
d. Topography. Generally speaking, the
islands are lowlying. Indeed, some of them
are just flat coral-covered surfaces. But,
there arc hills also. Car Nicobar, Chowra,
Camorta, Trinkat, Teressa and Bompoka
Islands rise to about 500 feet and Thillan-
chong to nearly 1000 feet. Katchall, Nan-
cowry, Little Nicobar and Great Britain
attain greater heights. The first and second
rise to above 1000 feet, and the third and
the fourth to about 1500 feet and 2000 feet
respectively. The highest part of the Nico-
bars is found at Mt. Thullier (2100 feet) in
the northern part of Great Nicobar.
c.
Climate. The climate of the Nicobars
is similar to that of the Andamans. But,
because of the more southerly location the
annual ranges are comparatively less-in
other words, the equatorial characteristics
become more discernable. Calm weather
conditions prevail only from February to
April and in October. It rains throughout
the year, for the islands are exposed to both
southwest and northeast monsoons. Preci-
pitation ranges from 90 inches to 170 inches.

Rainfall is heaviest on Great Nicobar

f. Flora and Fauna. Forest resources in
the Nicobars are not as great as in the
Andamans. There is only one first class
timber tree (Myristica irya). Fruit trees
of the islands include coconut, betel nut
and Nicobar breadfruit. Coconut palms
are present abundantly. There are also
thatching palms (Nipa fruiticans). Betel
nut trees are cultivated in some places.
The islands are covered with lalang grass.
A small variety of monkey, the
crab-eating macaque, tree shrew, rat mice,
bat and Nicobar flying fox are found.
Wild boar also is present, but it
might not be indigenous. Some cattle,
introduced into the islands, have become
wild.
The reptiles, found, include snakes,
lizards, chameleons, turtles and crocodiles.
Many kinds of Indian crab are also
present. The Sumatran type of fresh water
fishes are also found.
Some of the birds of the region
show Indian affinities while others show
Australian affinities-e.g. the megapode.
Birds, present, include the edible-nest-
building swift, pigeon, parakeet and oriole.
g. History. As the Nicobars lie athwart
an important ancient commercial route,
they have been mentioned in writings
since ancient times. The Chinese monk
I-tsing¹4 sighted the Nicobars in the
year 671 on his journey from China to
India on a Persian or Arab ship. He
described the Nicobars as the Land of
the Naked Men and said "if the ship's
people refused to barter with them, the
islanders immediately opened up with
poisoned arrows."
When the Europeans first moved into
the Bay of Bengal, the Nicobarese were
fierce pirates. The Portuguese, Dutch,
Danes and British in turn tried to deal
with the piracy. Missionaries began
showing their interests in the islands in
the 17th century. Beginning with the
18th century, the Danes, Austrians, Au-
stralians, British and French briefly occupied
the Nicobars and laid claims to them
at one time or another.
Even before the Danish East India
Company took on the task of sending
civilizing missions in 1756, Dutch pirates
and French Jesuits had contacts in the
area. During the Napoleonic War, the
British occupied the Nicobars, but the
Danish attempts at colonization and
evangelisation continued until 1848. In
1867 and 1876, Prussians and Austrians
respectively showed interests in develop-
ment of the island. Sometimes the islands
were used by Malay pirates led by a
deserter from the Royal Navy. In 1869,
the British formally annexed the islands.
Only then did the Danes officially give
up their claims to the islands. The British
suppressed the Nicobars-based piracies
and established a penal colony. They
tried to colonize the area with the help
of imported Chinese but failed. They
withdrew from the penal colony in 1888.
The Japanese held the islands during
the Second World War, i.e. from 1942 to
1953. When the Japanese were occupying
the Nicobars, they used Car Nicobar as

h. People and Settlement. In 1951, there
were 12,009 people (6321 males and 5688
females) on the islands. There were 12,452
people in 1941 and furthur back in 1931,
there were 20,240 persons. Officials and
traders together add up to about 500.
Almost the whole population is found on
Car Nicobar. Forefathers of the Nicobarese
got here from Indo-China before the
Christian era. Their men generally measure
63 3/4 inches and their women 60 inches
in height.
By race the Nicobarese are probably
a mixture of the Malay and Burmese
(Mon) strains.15 Some refer to them as
Mon-Khmers. The Nicobarese has a sturdy
build and is usually brachycephalic. His
hair is sometimes wavy or curly. A more
primitive element is found in the Shom-
pen, who live in the interior of Great
Nicobar. In 1941, they accounted for
2% of the population. They are a pre-
Dravidian people.
Twelve islands are inhabited and 9
ars uninhabited. The administrative seat
is located on Car Nicobar, the main
island of the northern group.
In 1931, Great Nicobar had 300 people,
Car Nicobar in the north had 7492 people
and the island of Chowra had 615 people.
Chowra had the greatest population density.
On Great Nicobar, there were probably
more people before the abondonment of
coconut groves as a result of conflicts
between the Shom-pen and Mon-Khmers.

i. Culture and Administrative Set-up. The
Nicobarese have Mon-Khmer and Malay
cultural affinites. 16 The Nicobarese language
belongs to the Mon-Khmer of languages.
The status of woman is high. They enjoy
rights of inheritance and ownership of
properties. As regards the choice of a
husband, girls enjoy considerable freedom.
Polygamy is permitted but only the rich
can afford it. The marriage tie is supposed
to be loose and divorces occur frequently.
People wear scantily. Articles of
European clothing are sometimes worn.
They live in permanent homes and practise
cultivation. Their houses stand on piles
and have thatched roofs. Cooking is
sometimes done in separate cook-houses.
For feasts and meetings, special houses
are built by communal labour. The food
sources of the Nicobarese include rice,
pandanus pulp, fish, turtles, pigs, fowls
and birds. Coconut constitutes a very
important item in the Nicobarese diet.
They chew betel nuts.
People believe in spirits. Grotesque
carvings are kept at houses to keep evil
spirits away. Christian missionaries have
not met with much success in their attempt
to propagate Christianity among the
natives.
The village forms the political unit.
Hereditary headmen govern with a council
of elders. Land is held by the community.
But, in relation to fruit and coconut
trees, private ownership prevails. An
Assistant Commissioner operates at Car
Nicobar.
