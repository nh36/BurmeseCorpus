PROBLEMS OF HIGH ACID RICE BRAN OILS IN BURMA
AND THEIR SOLUTIONS*
DR. FREDDY Ba Hli, U Thein Nyun AND U Kyaw Thaung**
During the past few years a number of rice bran oil extraction plants were set up
and operated. Many of these plants extract oil from rancid rice bran and so the rice bran
oil produced has a high Free Fatty Acid (FFA) content. This is because the rice bran
contains an enzyme which starts to hydrolyse the oil when the bran is removed from the
grain after the rice polishing process in the rice mill and this enzyme converts the glyce-
rides in the rice bran oil into free fatty acids at the rate of about half per cent FFA per
hour so that rice bran which is even a few days old is rancid and produces oil with high
FFA content.
These high acid rice bran oils are used by the soap industries to a limited extent.
These oils cannot be used te a large extent since the soap then becomes soft and unsaleable.
The limited amount of high acid rice bran oils taken by the soap manufacturers of Burma
is less than the output of the present rice bran oil extraction plants. Thus high acid rice
bran oil remained unsold and a large problem arose of utilising them.
A study of the local situation revealed that there are many problems associated
with high acid rice bran oils. First and foremost is the difficulty of refining these high
acid oils into edible oils (with FFA less than half per cent.) It is found that when these
high acid oils are put through a refining process, the refining loss is so high that it is
uneconomical. In fact the refining loss approaches 100 per cent and the yield of edible oil
is negligible so that it does not pay to refine them.
Another problem is encountered in trying to utilise these high acid bran oils in the
production of alkyd resins, epoxy fatty acid plasticisers and derivatives such as adhesives
which are manufactured in Europe (according to the Technical Division of Unilever Ltd.,
London.) This problem is that of transporting the high acid oil to the manufacturing
countries since the price of containers and freight for these oils is too high it is not
profitable to export the oil.
A survey of the literature by McPhie of Port Sunlight revealed papers by G.B.
Martinenghi on "Utilisation of Rice Bran Oil of High Acidity", by M. Miyazaki on
"Problems of Rice Bran Oil", by S. Ueno on "Hydrogenation of fatty acids with recycling
of used catalyst”, by H. Tsurata and T. Tamura on “Refining of rice oil by use of Urea”
and by R. Kovama on "Refining of rice oil having high Acid Numbers".
In order to solve the problem of the lack of sale of high acid rice bran oil in Burma
in a practical way, the Applied Chemistry Research Department of the Union of Burma
Applied Research Institute (UBARI) carried out experiments and three successful formulas

were obtained to utilise immediately the high acid rice bran oils. These formulas have
been sold to many interested industrialists for use with the high acid rice bran oils available
in the country.
The first formula is a method for making hair shampoo out of high acid rice bran
oil. This hair shampoo gives a good lather, satisfactorily cleanses the hair and is not
injurious having been tested by many people. It is cheaper than other hair shampoos to
make. The fact that it is made from high acid oil is not a source of danger to the user
because the acids in the oil are converted by the caustic potash (one of the ingredients in
the formula) into neutral soluble soaps which are non-injurious.
Another formula is for making liquid soap from high acid rice bran oil. Liquid
soap is being used more and more in advanced countries because of its hygienic and
economic advantages for public hand washing basins, factories, universities and school
wash rooms. The UBARI formula for liquid soap uses 3 times more high acid rice bran
oil than coconut oil and so is cheap to make.
The third formula is for making soap powder which is very popular with house-
wives because of its convenience in use for laundry purposes. A very high proportion of
the acidic rice bran oil is used in the preparation. The powder has the great advantage
over the soap cake in that the required amount is simply added to water and stirred. In
the case of the cake it remains in contact with water after use and so it softens and turns
pasty giving a bad appearance and making it difficult to handle. The high acid oil can
also be used as an illuminant for oil lamps and in this respect it saves the user a large
percentage of the cost as this oil is much cheaper than other oils on the market. The
elegant way to use the high acid rice bran oil is to hydrogenate it into stearic acid or
hardened fatty acids. The process consists in (1) splitting the acidic rice bran oil into
free fatty acids and glycerin by heating under pressure in the presence of a catalyst (2)
refining the fatty acids obtained by vacuum distillation and (3) hydrogenating the distilled
fatty acids in the usual way. In this way over 12,000 tons of acidic rice bran oil can be
annually consumed in the production of a tallow substitute by a factory with a capacity of
40 tons high acid oil per day. This will save foreign exchange spent on an annual import
of 8,000 to 10,000 tons of tallow for making good, hard soap. Besides, the hydrogenated
product can be used in the cosmetic, rubber, candle and other industries.
But it must not be overlooked that the main purpose of setting up rice bran oil
plants is to supplement the supply of edible oil from local sources in order to attain self-
sufficiency in this commodity. The problems encountered with the high capacity plants,
particularly that relating to the non-availability of fresh bran, have already been mentioned.
And no satisfactory method has yet been found to keep the bran fresh on long storage.
Where it has been possible to get bran which has not been stored for too long a time, the
extracted oil contains 20 to 30 per cent of free fatty acids. Although this can be econo-
mically refined to edible oil using modern refining methods, the refining losses are high
and it could never be sold as cheaply as that refined from very low acid bran oil. There-
fore, considering this industry from all aspects, it is quite clear that the best course to
adopt is to use fresh bran for oil extraction rather than allow it to go rancid. This will
make it simple and straightforward and there will be no problems to follow in its wake.
Thus the proverb "Prevention is better than cure" particularly applies to this industry.

From the very beginning when the UBARI took up this project the problem of
rancidity of the bran was clearly foreseen and so it did not recommend the setting up of
large commercial plants for rice bran oil extraction. Instead, research was directed
towards the construction of a simple extraction unit which could be locally fabricated and
easily and economically operated. The unit was to be attached to a rice mill and it would
be of such capacity that it could consume the daily production of bran from the mill. By
this means fresh bran would always be used so that oil with low free fatty acids would
invariably be obtained and easily refined to edible oil. After laboratory research and
pilot plant studies had been completed, the UBARI rice bran oil extraction unit was
erected and successfully operated.
A review of the present situation in the country regarding the Rice Bran Oil Industry
may now be made. It includes details of production costs based on actual data gathered
from commercial operations of the UBARI Rice Bran Oil Extraction unit.
The annual production of rice bran in the Union of Burma is about 440,000 tons
per year. Deducting the bran tonnage to feed the large (25 ton and 50 ton) foreign made
Rice Bran Oil Extraction Plants for 300 working days per year at 735 tons per day (which
works out to 220, 500 tons per year) there is still available about 220,000 tons of bran per
year. This bran could be extracted for rice bran oil very profitably by the small scale,
UBARI Rice Bran Oil Extraction (RBOE) units as will be shown by the cost analysis
presented below.
The rice bran needed for cattle feeding, etc. could be provided by the extracted rice
bran from the RBOE units as this extracted bran is full of proteins, vitamins and other
food materials required by the cattle and also will not turn rancid or be injurious to the
cattle or human beings.
As a single UBARI RBOE unit has a capacity of 5.23 metric tons (5232 kilograms)
per 24 hour day the amount of rice bran excess in the country can easily feed about 140
UBARI RBOE units.
From the sales market point of view, edible rice bran oil is now well accepted in the
local market and the supply cannot meet the demand. Moreover, as the country is
importing many thousand tons of edible oil every year worth about 3 crore kyats and there
is good possibility for export also, there is ample sales market for the product.
Having shown above that there is sufficient raw material (rice bran) and sales mar-
ket for the rice bran oil it will now be shown that the production cost is also competitive,
in fact it will be seen that the production cost is very low giving a good profit to the pro-
ducer of edible oil from a UBARI RBOE unit.
The designed capacity of a UBARI RBOE unit is 720 lbs (327 kilograms) of rice
bran per batch extraction. 10 batch extractions can be completed in 16 hours and 16
batch extractions in 24 hours. These have been proved and even exceeded in practice by
actual commercial producers and can be guaranteed after about one month of training of
the workmen. The full designed capacity rating of one UBARI RBOE unit is thus 5.23
metric tons (5232 kg 16 × 327 kg) per 24 hour day. In practice more tonnage of bran
could be processed if desired as this figure of 5.23 tons per day is a conservative one.

The fresh bran from the rice mill (giving oil with less than 2 per cent free fatty acid
content) can be processed within 4 hours of dehusking with a UBARI ROBE unit installed
at the rice mill (in fact the UBARI ROBE units are specially designed to be attached next
to an average sized Burmese rice mill).
The oil from this UBARI ROBE unit could be produced for less than K 2/22 per
gallon as shown above. This crude oil can be refined for less than K 2/- per gallon
(including the refining loss of 10 per cent for this type of crude oil). As this refined oil
is edible and is an excellent cooking oil it fetches a market price of not less than K 7/- per
gallon at present, giving a good profit. This is very different from the situation for high
acid rice bran oil (which comes out from old rice bran usually processed in large foreign
made RBOE plants). This high acid rice bran oil cannot be refined economically as its
refining loss approaches 100 per cent and since the demand for it in the local market is
small it does not fetch more than K 2 per gallon. In the above cost calculation the cost
of bran is not included. This is because the cost of 12 per cent loss in weight of rice bran
(due to oil being extracted) is more than made up by the higher selling price of extracted
rice bran.
From the foregoing review it would appear that the extraction of edible rice bran
oil by the UBARI RBOE unit is a small scale chemical industry with a good chance of
success and would help to solve the problems of high acid rice bran oil to a large degree.
