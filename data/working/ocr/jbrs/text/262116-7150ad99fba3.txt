Herbert Hoover and his Connection with Burma
Herbert Hoover, thirty-first President of the United States of America,
was born of Quaker parentage at West Branch, Iowa, on August 10, 1874.
Left fatherless at the age of six, he was only ten years of age when his
mother died. One year later he started West where he spent the next six
years in Oregon securing a secondary education and his first acquaintance
with business.
When Stanford University, established and richly endowed by the
fortune of Governor Stanford, opened its doors to receive its first students
in 1891, Mr. Hoover, then seventeen years of age, entered with the freshman
class. He graduated four years later having done distinguished work in
geology and mining. Meantime field experience during the vacation months
with a geological survey party under the leadership of Dr. Branner, later
President of Stanford University, increased his knowledge of his chosen
profession. Following graduation he plunged immediately into his work, and
within two years had established a reputation in the mines of California,
Nevada, New Mexico, Arizona, and in the offices of San Francisco mining
engineers. In 1897 he entered the employ of Bewick, Moreing and Com-
pany, a London firm heavily interested in Western Australia gold mining
development.
He passed through London on his way to Perth where he landed on
May 13, 1897, not yet twenty-three years of age. Here his genius for
organization and business was reflected by increased production and earnings
for the properties with which he was associated. Following a second visit to
London, he was appointed in 1899 advisor to the Chinese Imperial Director-
General of Mines. He made the journey to China by way of America where
he was married to Miss Lou Henry at Monterey, California, on February 10,
1899. They sailed the following day for China.
During the next two years, while engaged in a survey and study of
China's mineral resources, Mr. Hoover learned from old mining records that
for centuries the Chinese had secured large imports of silver from the south
by way of Yunnan province. His next intimation of the existence of these
silver workings came in conversation with Mr. A. C. Martin who was a
fellow passenger on a steamer between Penang and Colombo in 1903.¹ He

site; following a favorable report by a second engineer, Mr. Hoover, late in

The subsequent development of the mining enterprises in Bawdwin and
Namtu, with their important contributions to the prosperity of the Shan
States and Burma as a whole, is largely the result of his efforts as Managing
Director and Chairman of the Burma Mines, registered in 1906 as the
Burma Mines, Railway and Smelting Company, Limited, to acquire the
undertakings of R. Tilden Smith and his associates.1 This work is being
continued by notable successors in the Burma Corporation.
Mr. Hoover made a total of five visits to Burma, at no time staying
for longer than three months. During the first ten years of operations a
branch railways was built, the old Chinese surface deposits were worked, a
smelter was established in Mandalay, and about $5,000,000, in capital was
invested before a profit was realized. During these years the Company
worked about 800,000 tons of litharge from which the Chinese had extracted
some 64,000,000 ounces of silver. During the early visits to Burma Mr.
Hoover was accompanied by Mrs. Hoover and their eldest son.
making visits to Bawdwin they would, on occasion, be guests of the Sawbwa
of Hsipaw.

of his earlier labors. There are in the library of Stanford University 231
volumes relating to the history of India and Burma, many of them of special
value to research workers, which have been presented by Mr. Hoover.
These are quite apart from Stanford's famed Hoover War Library, one of the

national reputation. Notable is his translation, with Mrs. Hoover, of
Agricola's De re Metallica from the first Latin edition of 1556. This classic

total of 617 pages including numerous annotations and appendices dealing
with the development of mining methods, geology, mineralogy and mining
law of the Middle Ages which were added by Mr. Hoover. The book was
printed in a limited edition in London during 1912. He was appointed
