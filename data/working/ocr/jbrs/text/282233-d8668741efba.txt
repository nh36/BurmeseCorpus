BAGYIDAW AS EX-KING 1837-1846
Bagyidaw, the sixth king of the Konbaung Dynasty of Burma,
reigned from 1819 to 1837. In 1837, his younger brother Tharrawaddy,
who was also his full brother, deposed him and reigned in his stead. It
is proposed, in this paper, to construct the history of Bagyidaw after
his deposition. There is very little or scarcely any information
concerning him in books on Burmese history. He is summarily
dismissed with the statement that Tharrawaddy kept him in honourable
captivity till he (the ex-King) died a natural death in 1845 or thereabouts.
The information that I have collected concerning him, although scanty
and chronologically broken, provides a new and somewhat interesting
chapter in the life of Bagyidaw.
The sources are almost entirely the Company's Records in the India
Office. The Konbaungset Mahayazawin supplies very little information.
on the subject: relevant portions have been very kindy translated for me
by U San Lin, my old student. All available printed sources and books
in English have been consulted for the purpose in hand.
Yule has in his Narrative correctly estimated the character of
Bagyidaw as King. "The then King of Burma," he says, "Phagyi-dau,
or Noungdaugyi (Royal Elder Brother) as he is now most commonly
called, a man of about forty-seven or forty-eight years of age, was
popular among his subjects at the capital, on account of his partiality
for the public amusements, the shows, boat-races, and fêtes, in which
they so much delight, and he had the reputation of good nature,
accessibility, and unwillingness to shed blood. But he was without
ability or strength of character, restless, childless, 2 arrogant, and
violent. The Ministers never dared to bring an unpleasant subject
plainly before him, and he often vented his displeasure on even the chief
among them by flinging his spear, Saul-like, in open court, or by
inflicting on them the most degrading punishments. He felt bitterly the
loss of his provinces, never could bear to confess even the equality of
the British Government to his own, and viewed the presence of the
Resident with jealousy and aversion, as that of a dictator and spy.”³

Bagyidaw's chief Queen, Mae Nu, exercised an extraordinary
influence over him. Her brother, once a fishmonger, was promoted to
be the highest officer of the realm, and enjoyed the title of Minthagyi-
i.e. Great Prince. Bagyidaw began to suffer from intermittent fits of
melancholia from 1831 onwards. By 1834, he ceased to take part in
the affairs of government, and all power fell into the hands of
Minthagyi and his sister the Queen. They appointed many of their
relations to high positions, and did nothing to allay suspicion and
distrust. The Princes of the blood disliked Minthagyi and his govern-
ment, and feared he would usurp the throne. Ultimately in February,
1837, the Prince of Tharrawaddy broke out in rebellion. Bagyidaw was
kept ignorant of the seriousness of the situation; but when Minthagyi
failed to crush the rebellion, and Tharrawaddy threatened to attack the
Royal Capital, the King was informed of the impending danger and the
inability of the Government to protect Ava and the Palace. Bagyidaw
immediately recovered from his malady, and he, Minthagyi, and the
Wungyis, now threw aside all repugnance for the British Residency
and appealed to the Resident, Lt. Col. Henry Burney, for counsel
and assistance. On 21 March, 1837, Burney had two consultations
with the King and his Ministers, and the Resident agreed to pro-
ceed to Moksobo, where Tharrawaddy had stationed himself, and
attempt to bring about a reconciliation. Burney had two interviews
with the Prince, but the latter was in no mood for reconciliation: he was
bent upon taking the crown. He, however, made a formal, solemn, and
oft repeated promise to Burney, "that if the Government of Ava would
not resist him but would open the gates of the City, he would not injure
his Brother the King in the smallest degree, molest any of his officers,
put a single soul to death or allow the city to be destroyed and plunder-
ed" 2
Bagyidaw, however, did not trust his brother; and by the end of
March, the situation became so hopeless, that the King and the Queen
expressed a desire to make an attempt, with the help of the British
Resident, to escape to Rangoon. On 1 April, Bagyidaw enquired of
Burney if there was any chance of the British Government assisting him
against his rebellious brother. Burney had strict orders from Calcutta
to remain neutral, and to recognize the government that would establish
itself in the country, Burney, therefore, informed Dagyidaw that he poss-
essed power only to assure His Majesty protection and asylum in British
territory, but that the question of rendering him military aid was one

Pagan Min refrained from assuming the royal title till after the
death of both the dethroned monarchs. The two ex-Kings died within
one month of each other. The question naturally arises-Were they
poisoned? The Yazawin says that Tharrawaddy died of wind trouble,
but it is silent concerning Bagyidaw.
Thus passed Bagyidaw out of the scene. He had started his reign
in great splendour, having succeeded to the large empire of his
grandfather Bodawpaya when the might of Burma was considered by
Burmans to be invincible. The defeat in the war of 1824-1826,
however, gave him a rude awakening. He was dreaming of recovering
the lost territory by peaceful means; but he failed to maintain himself
over his shrunken empire, and died in captivity. With all his faults,
Bagyidaw was a kindly monarch, and was perhaps the best loved of all
the rulers of the Konbaung Dynasty. Let us hope that he died in
honourable confinement, having in his solitude arrived at some realiza-
tion of the truth that earthly pomp and splendour are, at their best and
in the end, an empty bauble.
