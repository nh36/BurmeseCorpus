PROFESSOR PE MAUNG TIN
OBITUARY
In our late lamented Professor Pe
Maung Tin, the Burma Research Society
had lost the longest-living life-member,
the earliest among Burmans to become a
pioneer member and the first among
Burmans to be honoured as the honorary
member of the society, who had been,
in the beginning, the honorary secretary,
the editor of the Society's journal, the
managing editor of the Society's text
publication series and later its president
for quite a good number of years,
which many a capacity, he had served
the Society, for more than half a cen-
tury.

During my Rangoon-College-Student
days, Professor Pe Maung Tin was my
beloved teacher because I took Pāļi in
the Intermediate of Arts and Pāli honours
in the B. A. classes, before the boycott
of the Rangoon University Act took
place on the 5th day of December, 1920,
when the deceased was no more in
country, but had gone abroad for further
studies, in Exeter College of the Oxford
University, in England.

I was studying in the sixth standard
in the Anglo Vernacular High School at
Thatôn, when I, for the first time in my
life heard of the deceased, who was then
known as Maung Tin as the fist and
foremost Burman to become a professor
in the premier educational institution of
the then colonial country of ours. My
grandfather U Shwe O, the most senior
advocate of the town of the then Deputy
Commissioner's headquarters, asked me
to emulate Professor Maung Tin, wen
his second youngest son, the popular
foot-baller, cricket and hockey player as
well as an allround sportsman of the
Rangoon College, the late lamented
U Kyaw Din who had then passed his
senior I.A. taking Pāli as one of his op-
tional subjects, broke this piece of good
news to him, as one who was deeply and
keenly interested in Pāļi studies. The
grand old man of Thatôn used to read
Visuddhimagga aloud in pure Pāli, every
Saturday and Sunday, keeping me al-
ways near him, in order that I might
become familiar with the work of Bud-
dhaghosa, while I was still in my early
teens then, twelve years old to be exact.
When, eventually, I joined the then
Rangoon College of the Calcutta Univer-
sity, early in June, 1917, the deceased
became my beloved Pāļi teacher familiar-
ly fond of me, because I could answer
all his questions almost, in the course of
his lectures. My learned lecturer Profes-
sor Pe Maung Tin had by then, transla-
ted into English, Buddhaghosa's Visuddhi-
magga, which in its earliest event, won the
admiration and praise of the entire Samgha
of the then world-famous Mahāvihära
School of Theravāda Buddhism, outstand-
ing and cutshining unbeatable rival of the
Abhayagiri School of Scriptural studies,
in the then Sihala dipa, now known as
Sri Lankā. The Pāli Text Society in Lon-
don was in the course of printing and
publishing our Sayagyi's pioneer English
translation of the virtual Pāļi encyclopae-
dia of Buddha's Dhamma, when my
learned lecturer, the late professor Pe
Maung Tin was keeping himself very busy,
making his second spectacular English
translation of a very popular Abhidham-
ma atthakathā ship-shape, for the press,
at the request of the self-same society in
England. This second book of his was
his English translation of the Commentary
in Pali on the first book of the seven
sections of the Abhidhamma Pitaka. The
great Commentator Mahā Buddhaghosa

Professor Maung Tin could not qualify

Litt., but succeeded in securing the B.

On the eve of his return from London, he
rechristened himself as Pe Maung Tin,
since he was born of his father U Pe,
the renowned resident and owner of the
Nadaing Cottage at Inscin, where had
been existing, as from boyhood days of
the deceased the present institute of
veterinary science in the then rame of
Veterinary College. This single centre of
learning of animal husbandry, in the
then Colonial country of ours, drew the
attention and roused the interest of the
deceased's father, who spoke of his desire
that his son Maung in should study
veterinary science. However, that desire
of the deceased's father was not to be
materialised; for, already in his high
school days, my mentor had come under
the linguistic influence of M.Chas. Duro-
selle, who succeeded James Gray as the
Pāli teacher in the then Collegiate School,
later known as Government High School,
Rangoon, which, in those days, enterta-
ined the service of an I.E.S., one of the
Britishborn bureaucrats, as its principal,
who virtually was a super headmaster.
Not less than two to three years
before his arrival back at Burma from
his further studies, the National Educa-
tion Movement as a sequel ard comple-
mentary to the University boycott launch-
ed within just a week of the latter's
inception, came to be participated by all
the Anglo Vernacular Schools of the
country and happened to be in full swing,
with the sumptuous support, physically,
financially and morally given, accompa-
nied by the country-wide cooperation of
the entire Burmese public. Nearly a
hundred in number of high and middle
national schools had sprung up in all the
districts, sub divisional and township
headquarters of the then Colonial Burma.
At Bahan, in Rangoon, the first ever
National College had been opened, where,
for the first time, Burmese language and

To toe a line with the curricula of

courses of studies in the Burmese Lan-

versity College, whose head was still

The Senate of the National Univer-

I.A., I.Sc., B.A., B.Sc., one after another
and conferred arts and science degrees to
internal candidates of the National Col-
lege and external candidates who were
then working as National School teachers.
Already in the early months of the year
1923, the Dyarchical form of reforms, as
major measure of self-government was
thrust upon the colonial country of
Burma, which had hitherto always been,
the Cindrella among the provinces of
the then Indian Empire. Consequently,
the founder of the Rangcon University
Act, Lieute ant Governor Sir Reginald
Craddock, was replaced by His Majesty's
Government in Great Britain, with the
Governor Sir Harcourt Butler, popular
to Burma, who made Sir Maung Khin,
the first Burman Education Minister, in
the then colonial regime of our country.
As soon as he became the Education
Minister and pro-chancellor of the Ran-
goon University, Sir Maung Gyee amal-
gamated the two colleges, the National
College and the University College, which
wise work became a fait-accompli before
the expiry of the academic year 1923–24.

literature as one of his optional subjects
for his degree examination.

some students, who might desire to take
