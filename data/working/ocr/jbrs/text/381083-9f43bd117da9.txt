MICROSCOPIC STUDY OF THE BURMESE TIMBERS
BY MACERATION METHOD'
by
U Kyaw Soe, M. Sc. 2
Introduction
In a country dependent to a good extent on the natural wealth of
the forests, there is usually found an array of valuable literature
on various researches conducted on anatomical and histological struc-
tures of timber. Surprisingly enough, in Burma, there has not been any
intensive study, except for a few desultory attempts on the nature of
the gross anatomy. Furthermore the little that has been written on
any structural study is so scattered about in various books and journals
that for all practical purposes it is of no value. In so far as, it has
been ascertained by me no attempt has been made in the comparative
study of the Burmese wood elements. The need for such a study
was brought to my notice, when an anthropologist, visiting the
Department of Biology, requested us to identify for him a piece of
wood, no bigger in size than a common safety match box. A thorough
search of the literature revealed that there was practically no work
which could be used in keying-out a small sample of wood commercially
important in Burma. It became obvious that an intensive research on
microscopic identification of wood, will be useful both from practical
and academic points of views. With the encouragement received from
the senior staff members of the Biology Department and their allowing
me full laboratory facilities, I undertook the present investigation.
In planning this research it was decided to divide it into two
separate parts, each though complimentary to the other, is also complete
in itself. The first part is the study of the various individual elements
of wood macerated out and separated from one another; the second,
to study the gross anatomy as seen in tranverse, radial and tangential
sections.

The present paper covers only the first proposed plan of study,
wherein the method of maceration is used. This paper is primarily
meant for use as a guide to microscopic identification of small pieces of
wood, especially when the standard methods in common practice with
the foresters, namely the determination of tonnage, or density, or the
study of grains etc. may not be feasible. The academic side of the
work however, has not been neglected, and the phylogenetic relationships
of the various species, included in this investigation, have also been
considered.
Material and Method
The forty three common timbers of Burma supplied by the Forest
Department were used in this study and Jeffrey's Maceration
Method was employed. It is a valuable and much neglected type of
preparation, in which isolation of the complete individual cell from a
mass of wood is made. Finely divided wood shavings about the size
of tooth-picks were boiled to remove air bubbles. The well-boiled and
washed wood pieces were then put into 20 cc. of macerating fluid
consisting of equal volumes of 10% chromic acid and 10% nitric acid
kept in an oven with a temperature of about 30. C. and regularly tested
with a glass rod. When the pulp became easily seperable, macerating
fluid was removed and the pulp was washed by decantation. It was
then stored in 20% glycerine with a few drops of 1 percent saffranin
and was then ready to mount and study at convenience.

Phylogeny
I realise that trying to classify a great many species by using just
one character, namely the advancement or otherwise of vessel elemants,
is definitely insufficient for proper interpretation of phylogeny. How-
ever, the surprising part of this phylogenetic study is that despite some
exceptions the results are so similar to the phylogeny of Bentham and
Hooker as revised by Rendel, that I have followed that system in
preparing my key.
Using the characteristics of the vessel elements only, the following
five groups have been made to interpret the phylogeny of the plants
studied:
A. Most Primitive Group:
Bars present on vessel cylinder or across the perforation.
B. Primitive Group:
Linear elongate tails on both or at least one end of the vessel
elements.
C. Intermediate Group:
All vessel elements with rudimentary tails.
D. Advanced group:
Vessel elements possessing rudimentary tails but are mixed with
tail-less ones.

In the above five groups those with simple pits only are treated as
more primitive than those with both simple and bordered pits, and the
possession of only bordered pits is the most advanced condition. In
addition to these characters, fibre-tracheids are considered more
primitive than simple fibres.
These five groups have been made with the assumption that vessel
elements are derived from tracheids, and that those which resemble the
tracheids are more primitive than those which do not. Thus a vessel
element, hat-box shaped, tail-less and with large perforations of the same
diameter as the vessel cylinder itself is considered most advanced as it
least resembles a tracheid.
In certain plants both vessels and tracheids are present. Plants of
this nature are very difficult to interpret as their vessel characteristics
may be fairly advanced. Of such a nature is family Dipterocarpaceae
where, tracheids are present in all the species. The vessel elements here
are of the advanced group type, having tailed and tail-less ones inter-
mingled. Such species have been considered more advanced than those
containing purely primitive vessels, but less advanced than plants which
have similar types of vessel elements but with no tracheids. This is
interpretted on the assumption that the tracheids are of the nature of
"relics". Advancement had taken place in the vessels but the "relic"
tracheids are still retained.
Using the criteria outlined above, families may be arranged in order
of their phylogeny of vessels as shown below:
Lauraceae, Dilleniaceae, Guttiferae, Dipterocarpaceae,
Rhizophoraceae, Rubiaceae, Lythraceae,
Myrtaceae,
Sapindaceae, Meliaceae, Anarcardiaceae, Euphorbiaceae,
Combretaceae, Bignoniaceae, Verbenaceae, Leguminosae,
Bombacaceae, Sterculiaceae and Tiliaceae.

Rhizophoraceae, Combretaceae, Lythraceae and Myrtaceae do not show
strong affinities. Rhizophoraceae is very primitive and Combretaceae
