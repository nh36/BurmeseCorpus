Women in the Inscriptions of Pagan*
It may be said without fear of contradiction that the dynasty of Pagan
built more pagodas and monasteries than any other dynasty in the history of
Burma. In testimony of the zeal with which the people of Pagan embraced
Buddhism, these edifices were put up by almost everyone who could afford
to do so, from the king down to a maid of honour. They were maintained by
large bands of slaves who were dedicated along with fields and cultivated
lands. A study of our only contemporary source of information, namely the
inscriptions, which record the dedications, enables us to form a picture of
the donor and the slave. It is true that the picture of the donor may be
onesided, since we catch him in a religious mood. But since the religious
mood is so pervading, we catch him in other moods also, as when he appears
in a law suit over the ownership of some dedicated slaves. The picture we
get of the slave, though faint, is fairly representative, since we read of
dedicated slaves, who are painters, wood-carvers, turners, blacksmiths, gold
and silversmiths, masons, washermen, gardeners, cowherds, elephant-herds,
dancers, singers, musicians, scribes, weavers, cooks &c. And we shall see
that women played a most important part in the life of the people, both of
donors and of slaves alike.

To begin with slaves: I think that quite as many women as men were
dedicated as slaves. Thousands of names are recorded in the inscriptions.
The names of women are generally indicated by the prefix i or ui (or their
variants i', in, im, ui', uiw, uiw'), and those of men by the prefix na or na. It is
gratifying to note that in many cases slaves were dedicated in family groups.
Thus "The Lum Kwot Pagoda slaves are nā Kwom Mran, wife i' Tun, Son
nä Wam Phlañ', elder brother nå Tuin, younger brother na Ku... i' Swā,
younger brother nã Pha In, wife i' Lhok, daughter i Sih" (L81).† The
groups are not confined to families but comprise friends and companions

All of them together with their groups and companions (ayan a-up aphaw
khapan akun)." L276 refers to a group of slaves in employment (ciy
taw mu so kywan cut)." Sometimes a family would consist of some member

Thwan, son na Com Pa ca tat" (L289). Sometimes the family circle is

father), sa smi (son and daughter), mliy (grandchild), lan miya (husband and

is also Burmese. Thus muchuiw na Phlac San (L192) is apparently a wid-
ower, nä Muchuiw is a slave's name in L120. Muchuiw Khrum is the name of
a field in L206. Muchuiw Phuiw is the name of a circle of administration in
L154. One would therefore expect to find in the inscriptions muchuiw ma,
the Burmese word for a widow. But no. We do not find muchuiw ma. We
find instead kmay, a Mon word. In L186 it is applied to Indian widows of
the Yhanpuiw port for ships and it occurs freely in the Dhammayangyi
Inscription (L81) which is Indian in character. Now why is the Mon word
used and not the Burmese word? Does this indicate that widowhood among
Burmans was not countenanced in the days of Pagan and that there is some-
thing after all in the theory that a man especially among the upper classes
must marry his brother's widow? The mention in the inscriptions of first
wives and lesser wives (mayākri mayanay, e.g. unlisted Inscription S. W. of
Winidho) lends further support. And since there is no mention of first or
lesser husbands, it seems that a man could have more than one wife, whereas
a woman did not have more than one husband.

As regards women other than slaves: A woman headman (sükri) and a
woman in charge of granaries (ki san) are mentioned in inscription L176 as
making contributions in money towards the cost of offerings made apparently
to an arañ monk on the occasion of the initiation of a friend's son. A wo-

has been defined by Burmese scholars as the headman of one village, and a
sambyan as the headman of more than one village. The definition is borne
out by the inscriptions, which generally represent the kalan as a witness to a
dedication and the sambyan as taking part in more important functions, such
as carrying out the king's orders (thuy taw e.g. L230). Thus kalan appears
to be an equivalent of rua sukri, the modern word for village headman.

which probably means a person in charge of the royal fans. L196 records
Khyat Ma Miy' San as a woman holding such a post. In L533 Im Bhun

dedications of lands and slaves and says "I have offered so much wealth not
because I do not love it, but because I love Buddhahood more than wealth."
In L378 we meet with another pious lady, wife of Cañsankhā and grand-

her house into a monastery at Mlat Kri Tāmalin.
In an unlisted inscription at Lemyethna Pagoda, Inpu village east of

How then could it be applied to a woman? It may be suggested that sankri
is short for Pali sanghakari. Although the meaning fits in very well, there
are other difficulties. That sanghakārī should become sankri would not be in
accordance with the usual Burmese way of curtailing Pali words. It would be-
come sanghakār. But sanghakārī itself would be bad Buddhism. Even the
founder of the sangha has never styled himself nor has been styled the maker
or creator of the sangha. To make or create anything is utterly opposed to
the spirit of his teachings. It is therefore unlikely that a thera should be styled
a sanghakari. I suggest that sunkri is a Burmese (Mon-Burmese) word made
up of san, company, multitude and kri, great, elderly. The coincidence
between san and the first syllable of sangha is accidental, since Burmans
never shorten sangha to san, as the Mons do. Sankri then as a Burmese
word would have an ecclesiastical as well as the ordinary meaning. In the
former sense it means an elder of a company of monks (Pali thera) as it
does in the Myazedi inscription. In the latter sense it would mean an elder
of a company of lay people, as in such examples as Sankri Uiw Lhok San,
a woman (L194) and Sankri Nā Sam Wā San, a man (L90). Sankri na
Mhuy San is an householder (im syan san) in L363. Then we have monas-
tery sankri's and pagoda sankri's as further evidence of the ecclesiastical
application of the word. Thus in L214 Nā Cha San, a monastery sankri
(klon sankri) is one of the witnesses, who are monks in distinction to the lay
witnesses, whose names follow. And in L179 the Pancan 5 Pagoda sankri's
(purhā sankri Pancan 5 yok) are monks. In L364 the monastery sankri is
probably a monk: "May my younger brother and my son control (the dedica-
tion) in agreement with the monastery sankri." In an unlisted inscription
at Zagyanbin, Mahlaing we are told of the death of a lay woman sankri
Uiw' Si San. Women sankri's, by name Uin Cip San and Ma Rhak occur
in L203. From the frequency with which the sankri occurs along with slave-
names, it would appear that his or her function was mainly in respect of
slaves. Thus we read of "the lands, fields and slaves looked after by the
Sankri Nā Lwan San" (L196), "4 slaves bought and introduced by Sankri
Mon Khway Phlan" (L193). In L253 we meet with the honourable Sankri
Nā Pi San, who buys a piece of land and plants palm trees on it.

There is still another word connected with san and that is misan.
Since it sometimes occurs in the phrase misan monma, the explanation
generally given is that it is the Pali missam in missaṁ monma, maids of
honour. I myself held that view. But I now think that it is wrong. It would
be unusual for a Pali word like missant to change into Burmese misan.
Burmans like a vowel sound checked by a double consonant and pronounce
nibbāna as neikban, vimana as beikman. It is unlikely therefore that they
should act contrariwise in the case of missam and pronounce it as misan. Besides
missam in this particular sense is not found in the Pali canon. Now the full
form of misan is amisan. This suggests that it has nothing to do with Pali but
is a Burmese word with the a prefix as in amipurha, asikhan and so on. The
right division of the word is ami san, a multitude (san) of mothers (ami), a
most picturesque term for maids of honour. Sometimes the suffix tat is
added to the word to ensure that they are palace ladies. Thus in L266
Amisantaw Marhak Sañ means Ma Rhak Sañ of the royal company of maids
of honour. She is a woman of importance, entitled to being called Lady
(Sikhan). In the same inscription another such lady Amisantaw Ma Ut à San
i.e. Ma Ut à San of the royal company of maids of honour carries the
royal order, and the witnesses are all the royal companies of maids of
honour who are in the royal presence. We meet a lady san mi ui' Pon
San (L172) who evidently is a matron of the maids of honour.
A palace lady (mon ma nantau san) Ui Pum is mentioned in
L701. The phrase "All the women of the outer circle" (plan su miyma
khapan in L303) suggests an inner circle of palace ladies.
We actually come across the expression atwanruy which must mean
'selected for inner service'. Thus in an unlisted inscription at Lemyethna
Pagoda, Inpu village east of Myingun, a woman atwańruy Uiw' Lak Cway
San is a witness to a dedication of slaves, and a man atwańruy Nā Khyat
Thi is the recorder. In L203 a man atwanruy Na Wā is a witness. So there
was an inner circle of men as well.
Thus we have reason to believe that in the days of Pagan there were
social organisations called san (modern thin). The organizations of lay
people and of slaves were in the charge of elders called sankri and sanlyan,
some of whom were women. There were saňkri's also of monasteries and of
pagodas, and sanlyan's also of villages. Then there were san's or groups of
maids of honour in the charge of ladies of importance, assisted by matrons.
There were palace ladies and men selected for inner service.

Listen to the prayer of an unselfish lady in L311: "May the merit of
my deed go also to the King, ruler of us all and lord of land and water. By
virtue of my work may he live long, seeking the welfare and happiness of all
who live in the realm, and upholding this foundation. May the queens also,
and all the ladies-in-waiting share it. May they look at one another with eyes
of love, without one speck of anger or cloying. May all Kings, Princes,
Ministers and the people of the present and of the future share it and
support this foundation. May King Yama and all creatures also share it.
May those who desire worldly prosperity get it. May those who prefer to do
good deeds do them. For myself, I pray that I may never be covetous,
insatiate, wrathful, bullying, ignorant, dull, stingy, mean, unfaithful, frivolous,
forgetful nor inconsiderate. But I would cross samsara full of the good
graces-modest in my wants, easily satisfied, mild of temper, pitful, wise,
conscious of causes, generous, largehanded, faithful, earnest, unforgetful and
affectionate; and may I win deliverance in the very presence of the Lord
Mittaryā." I like this prayer especially for letting us have a glimpse of the
sort of life led by the queen and her ladies-in-waiting: "May they look at
one another with eyes of love, without one speck of anger or cloying" are
words that must have come straight from the heart.

We come across a second Phwa Caw who calls herself Racasu Phwā
Caw, apparently in memory of her son Racasū whom she loved dearly:
"Sakkaraj 653 Namyun 11th waxing Thursday the Lady Racasu's mother
who built the Acaw Racasu Pagoda was agitated at heart: My parents,
great grandparents and grandparents have departed abandoning their
inherited property. And my beautiful beloved son has abandoned his inherited
property and his own mother. Knowing that I too cannot take with me the
inherited property left by him, I offer it as a gift to the end that it may be
for my parents my son and all my relatives a means to the attainment of
nirvana." (L384). She gives her relationship as "My grandfather King
Klacwā, my grandmother Cawmańlha, my lord (husband) the king, my lord's
son the righteous King, my mother, my father, my 2 beloved sons, my uncle
Sinkasū, my aunt Sumlula, my elder sister Ratanāpum, my 3 younger
brothers, my 2 younger sisters" (Ibid). Her husband the king must be
Narasihapate, for she was grand mother of King Conac (L409). Earlier in
the inscription she is mentioned as King Conac's "Queen Caw who is more
powerful than over an hundred of beautiful women." Here we have an ins-
tance of a queen still continuing to be queen during the reign of her grand-
son. We read more about her in L701, where her younger sister describes
certain lands "which were given to my elder sister queen Caw. On my elder
sister's death, when I became queen they were given to me... This is the
slave of Racasu, King Cañsu's (Narasihapate's) son. When Racasu was no
more, he was given to my elder sister. When my elder sister was no
more, when I became queen, he was given to me."
Then we meet with Ari Caw i.e. Aunt of king Narasihapate whose
dedications are described in two inscriptions (L332, 354).
