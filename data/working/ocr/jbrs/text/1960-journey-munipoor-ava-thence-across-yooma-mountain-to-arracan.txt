INTRODUCTION
Section A
Captain R. Boileau Pemberton of the East India Company's 44th Native infantry, whose
Journal is presented here, was the writer of a book that researchers into the history of Burma have
always found of unique interest and value. It is entitled Report on the Eastern Frontier of British
India, and was published in Calcutta in 1835 by order of the Supreme Government of India. With it
was included a supplement by Dr. G. T. Bayfield containing a 'historical review' of the political
relation between the British Government in India and the Empire of Ava up to the year 1834 and a
case of maps constructed by Pemberton himself mainly from his own surveys of the actual terrain
Pemberton compiled the work as a topographical survey intended primarily for military and
administrative use; but he was a man of scholarly and enquiring mind, and included in his book much
information for its sheer intrinsic interest. Copies of the work, it may be remarked, are extremely rare.
The present document contains his personal record of what must have been by far the most
colourful and adventurous of all Pemberton's expeditions. This one was not originally intended as a
survey expedition, though in fact it added considerably to his knowledge and yielded useful data later
to be included in his Report. He set out from Manipur in response to an urgent invitation from the
Court of Ava conveyed to him through the British Resident, Major Henry Burney. The first part of his
journey, down to the River Chindwin through the deadly Kabaw Valley, was undertaken at the worst
possible time of the year, the height of the wet monsoon, and Pemberton's successful completion of it
was an extraordinary feat of courage and good management. The return journey from Ava across the
Arakan Yoma to Akyab and thence to Calcutta was not part of the original plan. The decision not to
return via the Chindwin was made in order that he might make a careful examination of the An route
into Arakan to find out what its value might be as a possible military route, as well as a short cut for
trade and communications between Calcutta and Upper Burma, to save the long journey involved in
sending everything via Rangoon.
The circumstances, which led to this remarkable trip, are not perhaps as well known as they
might be. The only detailed treatment of them from original sources is in W.S. Desai's History of the
British Residency in Burma, 1826-1840, published in 1939 by the University of Rangoon, and
unfortunately, as a result of the Japanese occupation of Burma during World War II, practically
unobtainable now. Before Mr. Desai the only writer to have used the East India Company's records to
narrate this particular episode in Anglo-Burmese relations was Bayfield in his supplement to
Pemberton's Report. Incidentally, neither author makes any mention of Pemberton's Journal.
The essential facts may be briefly told. The first Anglo-Burmese War ended in February
1826 with a treaty signed at Yandabo, not far from the Burmese capital, whereby inter alia Burma
renounced all claims to Assam, Cachar, Jaintia and Manipur and ceded to Britain the long coastal
strips of Arakan and Tenasserim.

The war had arisen mainly out of Burma's acquisition of the old kingdom of Arakan in 1785
and her subsequent military adventures in the previously independent states of Assam, Cachar, Jaintia
and Manipur, through which the security of British Bengal had been threatened. During the war the
Burmese forces had been driven out of these states, and they had come under British protection.
British India thus acquired a new north-east frontier in the long mountain barrier running from Tibet
and Bhutan in the north down to Cape Negrais at the southern tip of Arakan.
It was an immensely long frontier and the treaty establishing it had been clumsily drafted.
Hence, as soon as the Burmese had recovered from the first shock of defeat, they inevitably began to
demand a number of frontier adjustments. The most difficult and vexatious of these related to the
Kabaw Valley between the Manipur Mountains add the River Chindwin. During the war Raja
Gambhir Singh of Manipur with British help had driven the Burmese out of his country and also out
of the Kabaw Valley. He claimed that the Chindwin was the historic boundary between his country
and Burma, and his British advisers, Major F. J. Grant and Lieutenant R.B. Pemberton fixed the
boundary so as to leave him in occupation of the Valley. The Court of Ava most emphatically
repudiated this claim. According to King Bagyidaw and his ministers the Valley had been Burmese
territory before the war, and in agreeing to renounce all claims to Manipur they had certainly not
recognized it as belonging to that kingdom. The Burmese protests were so strong and insistent that in
1828 the Government of India agreed that a conference should take place on the Chindwin between
Grant and Pemberton on the one hand and two Burmese commissioners on the other.
The commission met in March of that year and the Burmese chief commissioner produced a
map showing a river called the Ningthee to the west of the Kabaw Valley which, he claimed, was the
boundary between Burma and Manipur. Pemberton, who had an excellent knowledge of the whole
area, gained not only from his topographical surveys, but also from his experience with Gambhir
Singh's force when it had expelled the Burmese from Manipur, pronounced the map a fake. He
pointed out that in the first place there was no such river as the one shown in the map, and in the
second place the Ningthee was merely another name for the Chindwin. This the Burmese delegate
firmly denied: he said that nowhere throughout its course was the Chindwin known as the Ningthee.
The only way out of the difficulty therefore would have been to tour the disputed area. The dry
season, however, was nearly over, and conditions during the wet monsoon made survey operations
impossible. These were accordingly postponed until the following January, when a further meeting
between the commissioners was fixed.
In January 1829, however, the Burmese delegation failed to turn up. Instead, the Wungyis of
the Hlutdaw wrote to Calcutta asking for a postponement of the meeting until January 1830. This, and
the firm belief of Grant and Pemberton that the Burmese map was a deliberate fake, led the
Government of India to record its decision to leave the Kabaw Valley in the possession of Manipur. A
notification of this decision was sent to the Government of Ava in April 1829 together with the
information that Grant and Pemberton had been ordered to fix the Chindwin as the dividing line
between Burma and Manipur so that in January 1830, when the meeting between the commissioners
was to take place, "nothing will remain but to visit the spot together and fix the posts on each side of

the boundary, so that in future no room for doubt or dispute may exist." And this, indeed, is what
happened when in January 1830 the Burmese delegation, headed by the Wundauk Maung Khan Ye,
met Grant and Pemberton on the Chindwin, though the horrified Wundauk protested that the Kabaw
Valley had been in Burmese possession for 2000 years. But the Government of India's orders allowed
its representatives no discretion in the matter, so that notwithstanding Maung Khan Ye's
remonstrances and threats they proceeded to plant boundary flags on the right bank of the Chindwin.
The news of these proceedings caused so much excitement and anger at Ava that it was
decided to send a mission of protest to Calcutta, to complain not only of the injustice that had been
done to Burma, but also of the conduct of the British commissioners; for Maung Khan Ye had
reported the matter to his government in such a way as to convey the impression that what Grant and
Pemberton had done had been unauthorized. Before the mission set out, however, news came that the
Government of India had appointed Major Henry Burney to reside in Ava as its representative. The
Burmese therefore came to the conclusion that the matter could better be dealt with through the
British Resident in their own capital than by a Burmese delegation at far distant Calcutta. Hence,
although up till then they had had the strongest objections to the establishment of a permanent British
Residency, they now laid these aside - for the time being - in the hope that it might be the best way of
obtaining concessions from the British; among these the recovery of the Kabaw Valley occupied a
position of priority.
-
Burney arrived at the Burmese capital towards the end of April 1830. He found that King
Bagyidaw had taken the matter much to heart: he reported that the king was as eager about it "as a
spoiled child after some toy". The king had actually issued orders for the boundary posts on the
Chindwin to be removed by force, the Wungyis told Burney, and they had had great difficulty in
persuading him to cancel them. Moreover, there was a state of tension between the frontier
authorities, and minor clashes had occurred. At the beginning of June the Wungyis reported to Burney
that Grant and Pemberton, the Joint-Commissioners in Manipur, had ordered that all Burmese subjects
residing in the Kabaw Valley must cross into Burmese territory by the 25th June, otherwise they
would be considered subjects of the Raja of Manipur; they urged him to use his influence to prevent
trouble. Burney at once wrote to Calcutta suggesting that either Grant or Pemberton should be sent to
Ava to discuss the frontier question, and to the two Joint-Commissioners advising them to do nothing
likely to cause a collision until the King of Burma had appealed to the Governor-General. At the same
time he asked the Wungyis to allow him to invite either Grant or Pemberton to join him at Ava in
order to explain Manipur's position regarding the Kabaw Valley. The Ministers promptly agreed to
this proposal, and entrusted a letter from Burney to two Burmese officers, who were to proceed direct
to Manipur to deliver it to the Joint-Commissioners, and make all necessary arrangements for
conducting one of them to Ava. The Government of India on its part accepted Burney's suggestion,
but before its orders arrived in Manipur, Grant and Pemberton had received Burney's letter, and had
decided that the matter was too urgent to warrant any delay.

So it came about that on 14th July 1830 Pemberton started upon the long and arduous
journey, which he so vividly describes in his journal.
His instructions were not to negotiate but to answer Burmese objections and explain the Raja
of Manipur's claims. He himself had no doubt that justice lay on the side of Manipur, and his belief
was strengthened by the behavior of the Burmese in producing a faked map and in failing to keep
their appointment to check up on it by going over the terrain. Moreover, they had produced no
evidence in support of their claim, but had relied entirely upon bare assertions. Burney on the other
hand was in a quandary. He was cultivating good relations with the King and Court, and, though by
no means convinced of their claims to the valley, he wanted, as he put it, to "give the King the
opportunity of adopting a civilized course", and his sympathies were certainly with the Burmese. He
had come to Burma determined to persuade the Court of Ava to accept the Government of India's
decision to leave the Raja of Manipur in possession of the valley, but his early discussions with the
Ministers had convinced him of the futility of such a resolution. Quite apart from the justice of the
case, the Burmese were humiliated beyond measure at having to lose territory to Manipur, which they
had long regarded as a tributary state, and would obviously never acquiesce in it. He suspected that
the decisive factor in the minds of Grant and Pemberton on the one hand and the Government of India
on the other was the belief that the Chindwin was 'the most eligible boundary', and not any question
of abstract justice. He was anxious therefore to go as deeply as possible into the matter, to discover
and examine the evidence upon which the Burmese based their claim; that is to say, to do for them
what they appeared unable to do for themselves. For he knew that neither the pleadings nor the
protests of the Court of Ava would avail one jot or title with the Government of India, unless
supported by convincing evidence. Hence he took advantage of the discussions, which took place
during Pemberton's visit to Ava, to persuade the Burmese ministers to produce all the documentary
evidence they possessed regarding the Manipur frontier so that he might study it and submit their case
to the Governor-General. And, to cut a long story short, after examining the material submitted to him
he became convinced of the justice of the Burmese claim, and was successful in persuading the
Government of India to reverse its decision in favour of Manipur and to restore the Kabaw Valley to
Burma.
Why were the Burmese so particularly angry at the Manipuri occupation of the Kabaw
Valley? Economically the strip of territory had very little value. The answer must be sought in the
previous history of relations between Manipur and Burma from about 1700. As far back as the middle
of the sixteenth century the great Bayinnaung, in asserting Burmese authority over the wide fringe of
Shan States extending from the Upper Mekong across northern Burma to the Valley of the Chindwin,
had exacted tribute from Manipur. And although this had meant little after Bayinnaung's death in
1581, nevertheless in 1704 the then Raja of Manipur had presented a daughter to the King of Bnrma.
Under the usurper Gharib Newaz (1714-54), however, the Manipuris had incurred the bitter hatred of
the Burmese by a long series of murderous raids which penetrated deeply into Upper Burma, caused
frightful devastation, and exposed the weakness of the monarchy in such a way that the Mons of
Lower Burma were encouraged to proclaim their independence, and ultimately in 1752 captured the
royal city of Ava and deposed the king.

Gharib Newaz himself was actually a Naga named Pemheiba who had changed his name
upon adopting Hinduism. Under his leadership the bulk of the Manipuri people had embraced
Hinduism, and their raids into Burma are said to have been instigated by fanatics who preached that
blessedness was to be achieved by bathing in the Irrawaddy at Sagaing.
When the Burmese recovered from these humiliations under the leadership of Alaungpaya
(1752-60), the founder of the last Burmese dynasty, and the country was once more united under a
Burmese king, their counter measures against Manipur were even more fearful than the raids of
Gharib Newaz. Alaungpaya himself during the cold season of 1758-9 led a merciless raid into
Manipur, sacking its capital, Imphal, and, on retiring, leaving permanent garrisons at Tamu and
Thaungdut. This was only the first of many such visitations, which continued at intervals up to 1819,
depopulating the country and stamping out its civilization, for with each raid hundreds, and even
thousands, of Manipuris were deported to new homes in or near the Burmese capital. They were
skilled craftsmen, silversmiths and silkworkers. They provided the Court with its boatmen and the
army with its elite corps of cavalry, the Cassay Horse. Their Brahmans formed the majority of the
Court astrologers, and at Court ceremonies solemnly intoned benedictions before the throne.
Thus each country had horribly violated and humiliated the other. To the Manipuri Burma
was the enemy par excellence. To the Burmese Manipur was a hated neighbour, fit only to be kept in
subjection. Hence to have to submit to an award whereby Manipur was left in possession of a strip of
territory, however insignificant in value, which the Burmese believed had never rightfully belonged to
that state, was an intolerable blow to the pride of King Bagyidaw and his Court.
Section B
I came across Pemberton's Journal when going through the India Office records relating to
Henry Burney's work as British Resident at Ava. They contain to copies of it, one in Volume 385 of
the Bengal Secret Proceedings s.v. 19 November, 1830, and the other in Volume 677 of the Home
Miscellaneous Series. Both copies are so full of copyists' errors that the task of putting together a
reasonably correct version has bristled with difficulties. Proper names often have the most fantastic
spellings, with the same word spelt in several different ways within a few lines or pages of each other.
Ignorance only partially explains the errors; the signs of crass carelessness are only too frequent, for
sentences often do not make sense through the substitution of wrong words, there is hardly a single
full-stop in the document and most sentences do not begin with a capital letter. In a good many cases
it was possible to correct one reading by reference to the other, but too often the same errors are in
both manuscripts.
A copy of the Journal entitled "Journey from Munipoor to Ava, and from thence across the
Yooma Mountains to Arracan" was submitted by Pemberton on his arrival in Calcutta to the Chief
Secretary to the Government of India with a covering letter dated 9 November, 1830. In it he writes:
"On my return [to Manipur], should it be deemed desirable, I shall be happy to submit a sketch of the
whole tract of country recently explored, having collected materials which will enable me to make
some important additions to the map, already submitted by Lieutenant Montmorency, from Ava to

So far as I am aware no one has ever made any use of the Journal, save Pemberton himself in
compiling his Report. Nor have I ever came across any reference to its existence. Even Pemberton
himself does not mention it in his book. One wonders how it has escaped attention, for one has only to
begin to read it to realise that it is a most unusual document, far removed from the jejeune official
notes normally jotted down by touring officers. The vividness of the narrative and the detailed
descriptions of incidents and people are delightful, and often exciting. One wonders how he managed
to write so much, under such difficult conditions for writing, and when he must often have been
utterly weary. To use an adjective, which he uses more often than any other, it was a truly
'remarkable' effort. The quality, which perhaps stands out most and makes the Journal so entertaining,
is Pemberton's interest in the people he meets, and his ability to record even the most trifling incident
in such a way as give it significance. The little Portuguese interpreter's scrap with the junior Burmese
officer, the loan of the elephant to the Naga unable to swim the river, the Burmese horseman who had
made too free with the brandy presented by Pemberton to his brother, the Governor of the Chindwin
province, the little ten-year-old dancing girl, the delicious account of his adventure with the junior
Burmese officer's sister-in-law, his tiff with the Spaniard Lanciago, the picnic at the Kaunghmudaw
Pagoda when the Burmese Ministers twitted him with being a bachelor, the lesser queens 'gadding
about' in a 'common wherry' at the royal regatta and greeting Burney and Pemberton, the effects of the
trifle laced with cherry brandy upon the Burmese Ministers at the Resident's dinner party, the
kindness of the old Shan headman at Ywadut; these are but a few instances of the many human, an
touches which give life to the story. To read it is to accompany Pemberton on his travels and share his
experiences. One feels, reading it, that he wrote it as a personal record which later on he would turn to
in order to revive memories he did not wish to lose. As an eye-witness view of the Burma of 1830 it is
unique.

July 14th 1830, Wednesday-THOBAL
Two Burmese officers from Ava accompanied by two chupprassies of Major Burney, the
Resident there, and a party from the Ningthee-Raja arrived at Langthabal on the 5th instant; they
brought a letter from Major Burney inviting one of us to Ava, but recommending at the same time that
we should not go until instructions has been received from the Governor General on the subject. As it
appeared evident, however, from the general tenor of his communications that a great deal of
misrepresentation had been used by the Woondouk, who formerly discussed the subject of the
boundary with us at the Ningthee in January last, Grant and I myself both thought it most advisable
that I should go at once and not lose so favourable an opportunity of carrying a desirable object into
effect, especially as the Burmese officers have expressly said that they expect to be accompanied by a
British officer. For the intervening days we have been very busily engaged taking copies of the
correspondence and writing to Government on the subject. In addition to this I have had to make
preparations for my journey, which at that season of the year will be a miserable one. The death of the
Maha Ranee, which took place the day after the arrival of the Burmese, caused some delay as the
Rajah could not be visited, though he issued orders for collecting grain and coolies. On the 8th I saw
him, and all necessary arrangements being made, we left Langthabal at 12 o'clock on the 14th. It had
rained in torrents the whole morning but became clear afterwards, and the Rajah having lent me his
large boat, Grant Gordon and myself embarked in it and went down the Hurjang Pal to the Leelong,
which was frill almost to overflowing. Here a delay of an hour occurred in crossing the servants,
baggage and sepoys, when Grant took leave of us, and Gordon and myself proceeding in smaller
boats, ascended the stream of the Eeril Nullah, and entering the mouth of a small canal, called the
Irrapattee, formed under Chorjeet's direction, arrived at a narrow pass between two hills, through
which the Irrapattee flows, and empties itself into a large jheel occupying a considerable extent of
country west of Thobal. Here it became necessary again to unload the elephants, as, though the
channel was of considerable depth, our boats were employed in transporting the baggage across; after
which we again embarked, and crossing the jheel landed about two miles from Thobal, whence we
proceeded on elephants. The Thobal River had in many places overflowed its banks and inundated the
country on the north, and had we not been provided with elephants we should have found the journey
a most unpleasant one. The sun had set when we arrived, in consequence of the great delays on the
road, and as is always the case on a first day's march, there was a great deal of bustle and confusion.
The Burmese officers had set out early and arrived in good time. Thobalool came to me in the evening
and said that the Burmese officers positively refused to divide their party (an arrangement to which
they had acceded before leaving Langthabal) as the they know well that no river would be crossed on
the Muchee route. This proposition had been made by Grant and myself to them, as they would then
be compelled to acknowledge the gross inaccuracy of their map. The party was to have been divided
at this place into two divisions, one of which, it was arranged, should proceed under Gordon's
direction by the route of Huerok, Muchee and Kampang, and the other with me via Palel, Tooeheerok,
etc.

July 15th 1830, THOBAL
The Burmese officers came this morning just before breakfast, when I repeated what I had
said yesterday through Thobalal, after which they made the following acknowledgement, which I
wrote in pencil and read to them: "We know that the Burmese map sent from Ava to Calcutta is
incorrect. We know perfectly well that no river is crossed on the route into Kubo via Muchee and
Kampang, and told Major Burney while in Ava that the map, with which you had found fault, was
wrong. We again now acknowledge it. We therefore decline going by the Northern route or dividing
our party." After this I requested them to send for the map they had shown Major Burney, which was
brought, and I perceived that they had been adding some places to it since we last saw it at
Langthabal, which were fixed north instead of west. I pointed out the inaccuracy which was
immediately followed by their obliterating the additions. The chief Burmese then said that they called
the same river Kyoung-ma-gee and Nantwee, the first being the Burmese name and the latter the
Kubo one, and that it was the very same river, which the Munipoorees call Lokchao. I then showed
him that in his map there before us he had drawn another river flowing from south to north, on which
he had also written Nantween, and told him that was incorrect, that there was a small mountain stream
uniting with the Kyoung-ma-gee, which flowed from the eastern side of the Naga village of
Muenjoorel, and that its direction was nearly from west to east, and desired him particularly to
remember what I said, as I should point out these inaccuracies to Major Burney, when we arrived in
Ava. He then left us, and soon after set out for Langatel. As the object of Gordon's coming was now
quite defeated, he will return to Langthabal, and he will have little reason to regret doing so, as
travelling at all in this season of the year in such a country as this is wretched work. I set out at 10
o'clock and found very unexpectedly that elephants were able to cross the Urong Nullah east of
Thobal without wetting their loads. Two other small but very deep ones were also crossed between
this and the villages of Wang Ching, which we found nearly quite inundated from this overflowing of
the Huerok Nullah, along whose banks we skirted for a considerable distance. On reaching the high
raised road running from this village to the Langatel Hill, we had much less difficulty, but after
travelling for about two miles we came to a tremendous gap, which the water had formed by hunting
through, and this was crossed with difficulty by the people. From hence to Langatel Hill the road was
very much intersected by streams, all of which are now very deep, and on arriving at the summit of
the small range north of the village the whole plain below was one sheet of water, caused by the
overflowing of the small nullah, which runs through this little valley or glen.

July 16th 1830, LANGATEL
A letter was received last night from the Cherap, written by the Rajah's desire and ordering
the people here to do whatever I might desire as to supplies, etc., and Suru Suba Kueryba is to be
despatched immediately to ensure the necessary arrangements. During the day I was engaged copying
some translations from Burmese letters, and in the evening went out and shot five very fine young
ducks, four of which I sent to the Burmese officers, for which they expressed themselves much
obliged. Sunu Suku Kureewoogher arrived from Thobal just as I was setting out on my shooting
excursion, and said he had been desired by the Rajah to take care that there was no deficiency of
grain. The Burmese officers sent to say that in consequence of the number of their people who were
sick, they could not carry on any more grain for themselves, and requested that I would so contrive as
to supply them daily with rice from hence to Tummoo from my own coolies. This is extremely
inconvenient, but I have consented to do so, as it is worth while to show that I am anxious to consult
their convenience in everything. I have arranged that all my own people shall start from hence with
seven days' supply, and we shall be attended by forty or fifty coolies carrying rice to supply the
Burmese party to Tummoo. Coolies are now collecting to follow us the day after tomorrow with

houses, totally inundated and deserted by their inhabitants. On entering the Myettha River I
discovered numerous Munipooree houses, and called out in Munipooree to know whether the
inhabitants were of that nation. The well-known sound of their native language, spoken by a European
officer, attracted crowds of women and children immediately, who asserted that they were
Munipoorees, and followed the boat for some distance. They were evidently under great
apprehension, as I understood they had been ordered not to hold converse with strangers, I did not like

September 30th 1830 AENG THANNA
All the Burmahs, who came with me from Maphe Myoo, amounting to 101 men, have just
taken leave preparatory to returning to their homes. I have given them all 8 days supply of provisions,
consisting of rice, gnapee, vegetables, salt and pepper, and to the two sirdars I also gave some
coloured English handkerchiefs, two white muslin turbans and two bottles of brandy. They begged for
a letter to the Mendown Woon expressive of my satisfaction with their conduct, which I gave them,
and they included by name the presents they had received, omitting only the brandy, which, they said,
it was not advisable he should hear anything of, and they were content to enjoy it secretly, without
boasting of the acquisition. They went away well pleased, requesting that if I returned by this route I
would send for them again. In the evening, the rain having cleared off, I went to look at the boats, 6 in
number, which have been brought to convey myself and followers from hence to Akyab. I have
appropriated one to the use of 13 Burmahs from Tsalain, for which I have paid 17 rupees here, and
have given them 8 days' provisions. The boats are all small but are said to be the only ones procurable
here just now. They require a good deal of repair before they can be considered seaworthy, and I am
afraid I shall be detained the whole of tomorrow.

change of masters, and are so sensible of our hatred of oppression, that they complain on the slightest
appearance of anything like imposition on the part of the native officers immediately placed over
them. I understand that the tax is 6 rupees on every house per annum, one rupee additional for a
grown-up person, 3 rupees for a shop, 4 annas for every plough and its cattle, and 2 annas for every
buffalo not employed in agricultural purposes. The zillah does not, it is said, pay its expenses. There

October 1st 1830
A boat arrived here at ½ past 10 o'clock from Sandowee with Parwunnahs for the Daroga.
They have been 8 days on the way, but have halted at different thannas on the road. The Saogure or
Zemindars, receive a commission of 8 percent on the revenue paid to Government, and another
officer, the [ ], a commission of 10 per cent. I understand that 50 or 60 Burmah families from the

territory, and those, who have been better pleased to gain a livelihood by robbery and plunder, are, I

Lanciago, Don Gonzales de: Spanish merchant long resident in Burma. He had much influence at the
Court of Ava, and was used by King Bagyidaw in negotiations with the British. In 1837 King
Tharrawaddy appointed him akaukwun (collector of customs) at Rangoon. Lanciago had
been educated in Paris. At the outbreak of the French Revolution he had gone out to the
island of Bourbon, of which his uncle was governor. He served on a privateer in raids against
English Commerce, but when his ship was driven by stress of weather into Bassein he left
her and set up as a merchant in Rangoon, marrying a duaghter of the Shabandar, Jhansey. His
wife's sister was Bagyidaw's second queen, the Danubyu Minthami. Bagyidaw took him into
royal service, but during the Anglo-Burmese war he was imprisoned and his property con-
fiscated. He was released in 1826 and ultimately restored to favour. In an undated document
written by Henry Burney after finally leaving Burma at the end of 1837 [Document B XXVII
of the Burney Collection, Royal Empire Society's Library] he advised every commander of
merchantman putting into Rangoon to go personally to call on "the King of Ava's
Akoukwoon or Collector of Customs, Don Gonzales de Lanciago, a Spanish gentleman who
has resided nearly 40 years in the country and who is a great personal friend of the reigning
Burmese monarch [Tharrawaddy]. This gentleman, although rather strict and too anxious to
please his royal mistress, the Queen, to whom he annually about the end of the year takes up
all the customs and other duties he may levy, is still a gentleman of strict veracity, an
uncommon thing in Burmah, and one who has never been tainted with personal corruption.
He speaks French like a native and is in fact more than half a Frenchman. He speaks English
sometimes not very intelligibly, but the commander will find much future comfort and
fairness in his business if he gives this gentleman a favourable impression of himself... I
recommend all commanders to take Mr. Lanciago's advice, should he be in a sufficiently
good humour to give any. He lives on the riverside to the eastward of the wharf".
