MONS OF THE PAGAN
DYNASTY
By G. H. Luce
Of the present inhabitants of Burma, the Mons
are the oldest. Their language, Austroasiatic, is related
more or less distantly to other languages reaching from
Madagascar to Easter Island, and from Central India
to New Zealand. The Mons' former centre of culture
and diffusion was Siam, which was once, not Thailand,
but Monland, neighbour to the cognate ancient culture
of the Khmers of Camboja. But the Mons must once
have spread all over Burma, where they were pioneers,
it seems, in the cultivation of rice and beans. To this
day the word for BEANS in most of the languages of
Burma is Mon. The more you study the languages of
Burma, the more widespread you will find the culture of
the Mons. In some of the North Chin languages the
word for BIRD is Mon. The word for HORSE in
Central and Southern Chin and in all Karen languages, is
Mon. The word for a HARE in Shan and even in farthest
Kachin is Mon. Mon influence is very deep in Karen. In
nearly all Karen languages the ordinary words for FRUIT,
LEAF, HOUSE, VILLAGE, TOWN, RESTHOUSE,
WIDOW, BETEL, BAMBOO SHOOTS, SUGARCANE,
HUSKED RICE, etc., are Mon; perhaps also the word for
GOLD; also many names of animals and plants. In South-
ern Karen, Pwo, Sgaw, etc., the influence is most marked.
Their words for CATTLE and FOWL are Mon, and most
words connected with the sea, fish, shipping, and Buddh-
ism. Even North Karen languages, such as Pã-o (Taung-
thu) and Padaung, sometimes have Mon words for IRON,
FLOWER, PRICE, etc. Karen belongs to the Tibeto-
Burman subfamily of languages, not widely distant from
Burmese; but many Karen languages use the Mon Cere-
bral D (§) and the Mon MB (c) sounds which do not
occur, I think, in other Tibeto-Burman languages.

of the All Ramannya Mon Association, in the chair, on the
2nd. January 1950 in the University Students Union Hall.

Mindful of their source, the Mons of Burma
have always tended to hug the east of Burma rather
than the west. Thus early in the Christian Era, their
hold of Southern Burma was impaired by the infiltr-
ation of South Indians by sea, mostly in the Delta.
In Siam, during the 7th. century A.D., the Mon king-
dom of Dvaravati was at its height. It was a Buddhist
kingdom, centred at Lopburi (Lavapura, city of the
Lawas the Wa Palaung, of course, are in language
cognate to the Mons). Lopburi is on the Lower Menam,
not far from the later Thai capital of Ayuthiya. Here
Buddhist texts in Pali have been found, and the octa-
gonal pillar of Vat San Sung now in the Vajirañāņa
Library, Bangkok, containing the oldest Mon inscript-
ions, dating perhaps from the latter half of the 8th.
century. Legend, but legend supported by facts, says
that a queen of Lopburi, Cammadevi, led a colony of
Buddhist Mons to found a new city in the hills of
Northern Siam, HARIPUNJAYA, modern Lamphun.
I shall return later to Haripuñjaya, which long out-
lived its parent capital, Lopburi.
-
Meanwhile in Burma the Tibeto-Burmans were
pressing in from the north. By the 7th. century their
leaders, the PYU, now civilized, had occupied the
Irrawaddy valley west of the Mons, and reached PROME
at the head of the Delta, where they founded the first
great capital of Burma, S'RĪ KṢETRA. The last Pyu
king of Prome whose name we know, died in 718 A.D.
Some time after this, the Pyu appear to have been
split (perhaps by the Karens) and pushed back from
the coast on Central Burma, where their later capital-
probably Halin, south of Shwebo is described in
Chinese texts about 800 A.D. Meantime a powerful
Tai or Shan kingdom, NAN-CHAO, had arisen in
Western Yunnan, threatening not only Burma, but
sometimes China and Tibet. In 832 Nanchao sacked
the Pyu capital, and in 835 the two chief cities, very
likely Mon, of Lower Burma.

In Kyaukse, the first home of the Burmans in
the plains, the victors sat at the feet of the vanquished.
The Burmans learnt letters and Buddhism from the
Mons. Though close kin to the Pyu, they adopted,
not Pyu writing, but Mon. As for religion, linguistic
evidence suggests that the Burmans, before they con-
quered the coast two centuries later and thus established
direct contact with India, learnt Indian cults-Brahma-
nism as well as Buddhism from the Mons, probably
the Mons of Kyaukse. The thinbaing (E), lower garment

From "the Eleven Kharuin" of Kyaukse district,
the Mranma spread fanwise across Central Burma,
occupying the other granary of the Dry Zone, "the Six
Kharuin" of Minbu, which they conquered from the
Palaungs and Karens, and founded PAGAN at the bend
of the Irrawaddy as an advanced base for expansion
north up the Mu and the Irrawaddy towards Tagaung
(Pyu and Thet areas), west up the Yaw valley and the
Chindwin and ultimately Arakan, south towards Prome,
where Prañtawsā klwan (Kyundaw island below Mi-
gyaungye) was their front line. Kyaukse and Minbu
barely a thousand square miles in area, their two
meagre nurseries in the desert of Central Burma were
soon inadequate to support them. Until Pagan could
conquer the ricelands of Lower Burma, it could not be
great.
Meantime what was happening in Lower Burma?
It is a difficult question to answer, but there is a
certain amount of evidence. Burmese Chronicles are
probably wrong in regarding Thaton as the home of
pure Theravada Buddhism. In the early centuries of
the Christian Era, when South Indian traders flooded
by sea into the Lands of Gold (Suvannabhūmi, Suvaṇ-
nadipa) of Further India, they brought at first a little
Buddhism, but soon Brahmanic cults take precedence
-at first Shaivism and later Vaishnavism. The Shiva
cult dominated in Champa (Annam), Borneo, Camboja
and Java. Pali Buddhism won eminence in Burma,
particularly after its adoption by the Pyu of Prome, and
the Mons of Dvaravati in Siam. The Mons of Lower
Burma were divided. The oldest statue yet found at
Thaton is Buddhist-a standing bronze Dipankara, late
Gupta in style. After that, there is little that can be dated
much earlier than 1000 A.D. At this time, on the eve
of its conquest by Aniruddha, there are plenty of signs
of Brahmanism. The three magnificent sculptures in
hard red sandstone, that were once the pride of our
University Library until the Japanese destroyed it,

Crossing west of the mouth of the Sittang (Middle
Mon Saton), one reaches Pegu (Old Burm. Paykü, M.
Mon Pago) and the vast swampy coastal plains of Han-
thawaddy. This region was called Ussi, Ussa, Ussala
proving its early colonization from Orissa on the east
coast of India. The Sittang mouth has pushed eastwards
in modern times. The modern moated city of Pegu, now
far from the Sittang, faces west and lies along the short
and shallow Pegu River. But the adjacent old moated
city and port, with long narrow walls, faced east: show-
ing that once, despite the dangerous tidal bore, ships
reached it by the Sittang. Some Mon Chronicles date
its founding from 825 A.D. The late Dr. J.A. Stewart
spent a month excavating here, discovering two vaulted
shrines, small Buddhist images and possibly Shaivite
symbols. Phayre says that "coins or medals bearing
Hindu symbols" were probably struck here. Beautiful
deep square wells of laterite are a feature of this site.
The radiating arch was known, it seems, by the Mons
of Pegu as well by the Pyu of Prome, but the Mons
rather distrusted it, for at Pagan they usually add a
carved wooden lintel at the arches.
The land hereabouts has gained greatly on the sea.
Iron buoys and ship's tackle, deeply sunk, have been
dug up as high as Kawliya, Taikkala and Payagyi. The
finest relics of the past are found at Kyôntu, east of
Waw, some 20 miles N.E. of Pegu. I am anxious to
know whether they have survived the heavy fighting in
this neighbourhood during the past 8 years. Here along
a huge platform, 80 yards square and 5 ft. high, were
rows of large glazed terracotta plaques, with characters
or masons' marks in Telugu-Kadamba script on the top
rim. The face shows floral medallions carved with a
great variety of scenes, all secular and mostly violent:
bullfights, battles, boxing, fights on horseback, or
elephantback, lion-hunts (there were never lions native
in Burma), scenes of dancing and drinking, musicians
playing drums, conches, buffalo-horns, flutes and cym-
bals, and dancers posing, leaping or reeling-not at all
crude, but shewing in their carving and disposition high
artistic skill. The human figures have huge carplugs,
armlets, wristlets and necklets, a band across the chest,

The 11th. century was disastrous for the Mons:-
in Siam trouble at Haripuñjaya and the fall of Lopburi,
in Burma the fall of Thaton. Thaton was conquered
by Aniruddha about 1054-7 A. D. The only original in-
scriptions of his reign, the so-called "Scals of Anorata",
are brief Buddhist prayers or statements stamped or
written on terracotta votive tablets. They reflect the
inchoate and floating culture of the time-in language
Pali or Sanskrit, not Burmese, Pyu nor Mon (the king's
name is always in Sanskrit), the script North Indian
Nagari or South Indian Mon-Burmese. They are found
at scattered places from Katha and Tagaung in the north
as far (I think) as Tavoy in the south, around Pagan,
near Salin, near Thazi, at Old Prome, and between
Twante and Rangoon. All this helps to confirm the

At the height of the crisis, when the rebel was
at the gates of Pagan, what did the Mon royalty do? -
Makuta himself and also his son were now dead. From
certain late inscriptions it appears that Makuta's grand-

The keynote of Kyanzittha's reign was clearly his
love of the Mons whom he had conquered, and his
statesmanlike resolve, not so much to conquer Burma
as Aniruddha had done, as to unite it. All his in-
scriptions (and here he is unique among Pagan kings)
are in Mon. His temples, too, are of distinctive type,
Pyu perhaps in plan and structure, Mon in decoration
and stone sculpture. His religion, too, was of the
broadest type, calculated to appeal not only to the simple
Burman, worshipper of spirits and Nāgas, but also to
the more sophisticated peoples of Lower Burma, Mons,
Indian and Pyu. One needs to remember that the Mons
have been exposed to Indian influence a thousand years

Cañsu I (Rhuykudayakā), the Alaungsithu of the
Chronicles, was born in 1089 and succeeded his grand-
father in 1113. He had a long reign. The dynasty
divides itself at this point. The first part is the MON
PERIOD, with the Mons leading their victors at every
point. The last part, from Cansu II (Narapatisithu)
onwards, is the BURMESE PERIOD, with the Mons
no longer conspicuous. In between from 1113 to
1174 is the TRANSITION. Cañsū I has left four
lines in Mon at the foot of the Shwezigôn inscription
of his grandfather. He has left nothing in Burmese.
Indeed there are hardly more than half a dozen Bur-
mese inscriptions original to his long reign.
His one
famous inscription, that of the Shwegu at Pagan (1131
