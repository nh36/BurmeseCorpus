A RETROSPECT AND PROSPECT
As you all know, we are celebrating this year the Silver Jubilee of His
Majesty, the King-Emperor; and as you all should know, we are also
celebrating the Silver Jubilee of this Society. Perhaps on this occasion you
will allow me to indulge in the vanity of reminiscences. But I hope they
may not be wholly vain, for I want to take them as a text for some remarks
on our object, scope and methods.
Although it is now twenty five years since we entered on the world,
there was a rather long period of gestation and the actual conception of the
Society dates from 1909 or 1908, when the late U Tun Nyein, then Govern-
ment Translator, showed me an article by a Burman in the Journal of the
then recently founded Siam Society. I still remember how, as I was walking
home, it suddenly occurred to me, "What we need in Burma is a Burma
Society".
And we did need it. Not many of you can recall the state of affairs in
Burma then. We were living on the aftermath of Curzonism-a harvest of
chaff, and the supreme object of administration was mechanical efficiency-
on paper, obtaining a show of uniformity by a reckless disregard of human
factors and of facts. Let me give you just a few examples. For the sake
of uniformity in village administration, the Myothugyi, the only living
institution in Burma, the last surviving relic of the Burmese social order, was
being threatened with active measures for his extinction. For the sake of
uniformity in revenue administration much labour was being devoted to the
unification of the revenue codes of Upper and Lower Burma; I remember
Mr. W.J. Keith later Sir William-, who had escaped for a time from the
Secretariat to the comparatively real atmosphere of Settlement, wondering
* why uniformity was not carried a stage further by requiring cultivators in
Upper and Lower Burma to grow the same crops. For the sake of
uniformity in central administration-or it might seem from sheer cussed-
ness-two Natives of India, despite their protests, were posted to the Civil
Service in Burma. The Head of the Province. who, throughout a career of
thirty years in Burma had never split an infinitive, knew so little of the facts
of life in Burma, that he copied from the Punjab a Tenancy Bill for protect-
ing the hereditary tenants of the Delta, where hardly a single tenant had
cultivated the same land for more than three successive years. On maps in
school the British Empire was still coloured red, but we were doing our best
to paint this little corner of it, "this land of colour, light and laughter",
grey. Such was the time that, according to our temperament we look back
on as the good old days or the bad old days. Some few of us were beginning
to think that they were not good enough; that for governing a country in
the twentieth century the first essential was a knowledge and understanding
of facts, and that if we whose lot, by birth or vocation, was cast in Burma
were to fulfil our obligations, we should devise some machinery for organiz

ing knowledge and thought in relation to life in Burma. I was young then
and hoped that possibly a Burma Society, a Society which would encourage
a living interest in Burma and would enable all those interested in any
aspect of life in Burma to pool their experiences, might contribute to this
end.
But it is one thing to have a bright idea and another thing to clothe it
with substance. However, I was fortunate in the possession of two friends,
with whom I could discuss the project. On my first arrival in Burma I had
become acquainted with a young advocate in Moulmein. But we had been
parted for some years. He had gone to England to study for the Bar, and I
moved about the country in the ordinary course of official service. Just then,
however, we were both in Rangoon, and I happened to notice that he was
giving a public lecture one Sunday afternoon to the Buddhist Association of
Rangoon College. The lecture was, for those days, a notable expression of
nationalist aspirations and at the end of it I took the opportunity of renewing
my acquaintance with the lecturer, U May Oung. I discussed the project
with him, and also with M. Duroiselle, then Lecturer in Pali in Rangoon
College, who was not only my friend but my saya in the study of early
Burmese history of which, at that time, he knew more than any one.
Together we drafted a prospectus and distributed copies among a few
friends.
We made it clear that we aimed at covering the whole range of life in
Burma and, either in the draft or in the covering letter, explained that we
hoped to obtain contributions for the Journal on matters of economic
interest. On the whole the criticisms were encouraging; but there was one
warning note. "You cant touch economics" said one of the very few people
who knew anything about the economy of Burma," or the Society will be
declared a political association, and all government servants will be called
on to resign".
However we went forward. In those days, even more then now, nothing
could be attempted in Burma without the approval of the Governor; in him
we lived, and moved and had our being. The next step therefore was to
approach him. He professed sympathy and took no exception to the
comprehensive scope of the proposed Society; there was only one passage
that he deleted. We had expressed regret that the activities of Government
in respect of archæology had been limited by lack of funds. "It would never
do" he explained, "for government servants to put their names to a document
containing such a reflection on Government" Fortified by his assent, we
addressed some of the leading officials and non-officials in Rangoon,
including the one or two Burmans who were regarded as the official representa-
tives of Burmese opinion, and obtained their signatures to a circular letter
for general distribution explaining the project and appealing for support.
At the last moment there was nearly a hitch. The Financial
Commissioner, warned, I believe, by his Secretary that the proposal was a
deep-laid scheme to encourage Nationalism and such-like subversive tend-
encies, wrote to withdraw his signature. The movement was, he feared,
" political". In this, of course, he was right. For we were attempting some-
thing new. It was the first attempt to provide Europeans and Burmans with

where they met to make money out of one another; it was the first time
that Europeans had been encouraged to express a sympathy with Burmese
culture or that Burmans had been encouraged to express an interest in
Burmese life; and it was the first attempt, except by Government, to
promote cultural interests in Burma. It was, on a very modest scale, an event
of political significance; morally, and potentially, of great significance.
The Financial Commissioner was right in giving this as his reason for
withdrawing his signature; he was wrong only in not recognising that this
was the chief reason for his signing the Circular. But, as Count Smorltork
said, "the word politic surprises by himself", and to call a movement political
was to damn it. However, I had just been posted to Myingyan and when I
suggested that at that distance from Rangoon I could not be very
mischievous, his fears were allayed; he permitted us to use his name and the
Circular was published.
We were very doubtful what would come of it. We were appealing to
intelligent Burmans and sympathetic Europeans. And in those days very
few Burmans had come into contact with the modern world-there were only
about a hundred graduates-and for a European to admit sympathy in
Burma was a sign of weakness if of nothing worse, and certainly bad form.
For example, not infrequently I used to visit the Shwe Dagon, where
footwearing was still allowed. On one occasion I was told that the Deputy
Commissioner of Rangoon Town had just left the Pagoda, and was surprised
to learn that he visited it regularly to talk with the lugyis. I referred to
this when I next met him, but he asked me not to mention it to any one. It
would have imperilled his reputation for sound common sense. Again, with
reference to the proposal that life members could compound for their
subscription, Ananda Metteya, who in becoming a Buddhist monk had not
lost the canny shrewdness of a Scotchman, suggested that most people
would hope to live rather longer then the Society. But some people, even
some Scotchmen, were sufficiently interested and optimistic to join as life
members, and now, alas! after twenty five years, not many of the original
life members survive. For in this, as in other respects, the event proved
that our fears had been groundless, and there was a gratifying response, both
among Europeans and Burmans.
Then, while still in the cradle, the infant Society attempted suicide;
unless, possibly, its foster-parents deliberately tried to stifle it.
I have never
found out exactly what happened. From the beginning some people, like
the Financial Commissioner, had regarded it as dangerous, and it had been
suggested that we might be content with forming a Burma Branch of the
Royal Asiatic Society. But that would not have attracted Burmans, nor, I
think, many Europeans, and we proposed to call it the Burma Society as
indicating its comprehensive scope. Unfortunately, just about that time,
this title was adopted by another Society, and we had to find an alternative.
The responsibility for choosing a name rested with the Provisional Com-
mittee of which the members, as so often happens, had been elected rather
for their position than for any active interest in the objects of the Society.

Our present title was, I understand, suggested from influential quarters and
the late Mr. A. D. Keith told me that only one other member besides himself
protested. In the Address which U May Oung delivered at the Inaugural
Meeting our original purpose was re-affirmed and emphasised; but we have
never been able to overcome the limitations implied by the title which the
Provisional Committee adopted.
The result has, I think, been unfortunate for the Society and for Burma.
In any country the proportion of people with living interest outside their
personal concerns is very small; and Burma is a small country with a very
small population. The proportion to which a Society like this can appeal is
smaller still for it is limited to those who know Burma and are in contact
with the modern world. Reckoning both Europeans and Burmans our
potential membership is not more than a few thousand; perhaps, seeing
how few Europeans do know anything of Burma, it is not more than a
few hundred. Of this few hundred most, as in other countries, are
wary and respective people who wish the world may go well, so it be
not along of them". Only by extending the scope of our activities as widely
as possible can we draw on a field large enough to provide the Society
with a secure foundation. The limitation of our scope has led to a
constant struggle to obtain adequate support. Again, by reducing the
number of potential contributors to our Journal it has involved the Editors in
a constant struggle to obtain sufficient material of a respectable standard.
If our original plan had been adopted we should have had a larger
membership, and a larger, better and more interesting Journal. Still for
twenty five years we have managed to keep alive and to publish our Journal
with very fair punctuality. I remember that when the name of the Society
was first announced some people thought we were as good as dead, and
perhaps better dead. Mr. Tuck, for example, then Deputy Commissioner of
Myingyan, said that all I had done was to make a grey world greyer. "Would
you have taken so much trouble" he asked, "if you had foreseen the result ?"
Well, I thought then that, despite the name conferred on us by some
malicious fairy godmother, we might do something to make life in Burma
not greyer, but richer. And now, twenty five years later, we can claim, I
think, to have done so. When Sir John Marshall, the later Director of
Archæology retired, the French savants entertained him to dinner on his
way through Paris and congratulated him on leaving India 2000 years older
than when he first went there. We have not done as much as that for
Burma but we have contributed something at least to building up a richer,
fuller picture of its past, its history, language and literature. I am not
claiming too much, I think, when I say that every student of the history,
language and literature of Burma must at least consult the Index of our
Journal to find out what has appeared in it during the last twenty five years
on the subject of his study. I do not say that he will always find what he is
looking for, but on certain matters he will find valuable information which is
available nowhere else and—this is the important point-which would not
have been available at all if our Society had not given a stimulus to research
and an opportunity for placing such material on record. But the material

part, of the work in which we have had a share. There are, for example, the
publications of the Text Publication Committee and the English translation
of the Glass Palace Chronicle; without our Society it is doubtful if Mr.

Burmese Dictionary would have been attempted. I could enlarge the list, but
these examples will suffice to illustrate my argument.
What is it exactly that we have done? We have done three things.
First, and most important, we have organised the demand for a knowledge
of the past of Burma. That is a matter which does not interest very many
people very much. But by bringing together those whom it does interest we
have given them an opportunity to satisfy their interest; we have fostered
an interest that otherwise might have perished of starvation. Without our
Society the interest, if it survived at all, would have been merely an
ineffective inclination, an idle wish; we have enabled it to find expression
as an economic demand which can be satisfied by the payment of an annual
subscription. But the organisation of demand implies more than that.
Take my own case, for example. When I came to this country I wanted
to find
out something about Burma and its people. But I did not
know where to begin. There was no way of making a beginning. A
solitary individual cannot undertake such studies by himself; they need co-
peration, organisation, Now, any new-comer to Burma may join the
Society and, if he wants information on any point within the sphere of our
activities, he can address a letter to the Journal asking for the information.
that he requires. (I wish more people would do it !). But we have done more
than merely organise the demand for a knowledge of certain aspects of ilfe
in Burma. We have done something to satisfy it. We have collected facts
with reference to these matters; we have organised knowledge in respect of
them. And, thirdly, we have provided opportunities for the discussion and
better understanding of those facts; we have contributed to the organisation.
of thought in relation to these matters. In respect of certain aspects of life in
Burma we have done what we hoped originally to do for all aspects of life
in Burma; we have organised knowledge and we have organised thought.
And what is the result? The result is that within the sphere of our activi-
ties some progress has been made; not very much, perhaps, but some. And
outside the range of our activities practically no more is known of Burma
than was know twenty five years ago. If during these twenty five years we
had been publishing notes and discussions on modern Burma as well as on
the past of Burma, it is not unreasonable to suppose that our knowledge of
modern Burma would have made at least as much progress as our knowledge
of the past of Burma. Probably, since many more people are interested, and
more keenly interested, in the present and in the future than in the past,
much greater progress would have been made. But because this demand has
remained unorganised it has been ineffective. That is why I say that the
limitations of our scope has been as unfortunate for Burma as for our Society.

But if a Society such as ours but embracing the whole range of life in
Burma was needed twenty five years ago, the need is now far greater. At
that time Government worked behind a smokescreen; it was rather like one
of those small cuttlefish which, on the approach of an inquisitive stranger,
discharge a cloud of ink. We were perhaps young and optimistic in thinking
that it might, or at least ought, to welcome a closer contact with reality. But
during the past twenty five years there have been changes, changes which in
some measure we foresaw and for which we hoped to make provision. One
change is that Government has been brought a stage nearer to reality; it
must face criticism in the Council. One may feel sometimes that it has merely
brought its weapons up to date and, instead of hiding behind a cloud of ink,
takes refuge in a fog of words. And one must admit that it is easier to
answer ill-informed or unintelligent criticism, than sound criticism. But once
criticism is permitted, it must in the long run come to be understood that
sound criticism is helpful. Therefore, I say, it is more than ever necessary
to organise knowledge and thought in relation to life in Burma; and more
urgent, by twenty five years, to do so than it was twenty five years ago.
This can be done only by the publication and discussion of facts in books
or periodicals of serious interest. But in
But in Burma there are, practically
speaking, no such books or periodicals. The public, European and Burmese,
needs educating before there can be a demand for such books and periodicals.
And such demand as exists needs to be organised if it is to become effective,
and, as things are, it is unlikely to be organised except by or through some
Society. In Europe, of course, provision is made for the organisation of all
such interests separately through a number of Societies, each with its special
object. But that is quite a modern feature. Formerly there was only one
learned Society in England, the Royal Society, which took all learning for
its province. In Netherlands India at the present day there is one
comprehensive society, the Royal Batavian Society, which covers the whole
range of social interests; I myself have had the honour of addressing the
Economic Section. It is a Society such as twenty five years ago we intended
our Burma Society to be, and such as this Society might still become. If
we do not rise to this opportunity it is improbable that any other Society
will fill the gap; either we must extend the range of our activities or things
in Burma will go on in the bad old way; only from bad to worse.
It is, then, to the interest of Burma that we should extend our scope.
Is it in the interest of the Society?. It is certainly to the interest of our
Society that we should extend the field of membership and have a larger
selection of material for publication in our Journal. But some may be
apprehensive that an attempt to extend our range of interests might lead to
the break up of our Society and that if, for example, we touched on
economics Government might still think proper to call on official members to
resign. Now I am not suggesting that we should enter the political field as
partisans, that we should support this, that or the other party, or be either
for or against the Government. I am merely suggesting that we should
deliberately step outside the narrow groove in which we have been walking
and, of set purpose, aim at encouraging the study of all aspects of life in

Burma by opening the columns of our Journal to all facts relative to life in
Burma and to the discussion and understanding of such facts; that we
should do something towards organising the knowledge and thought without
which modern constitutional forms are mere sham.
But if we are to attempt this, how are we to set about it? We must
recognize that it will need an effort. Unfortunately we have got into a
groove, and one cannot get out of a groove without an effort. But I think
we can learn something from our past experience. For we have already
tried to get out of the groove more than once. In very early days we
published an artical by Mr. Aubert on millet in upper Burma which I hoped
would lead to further contributions on agriculture and economics. But nothing
came of it. Later there was a more sustained effort to encourage contribu-
tions on Natural Science; but some of these did not bear very closely on
Burma and some were of such special interest that they did not interest the
ordinary reader. Mainly for these reasons, the experiment proved
unsuccessful. But it has always seemed to me that we ought not to have
dropped it for fear of boring the ordinary reader. The ordinary reader has
his rights, but he should not be allowed to fix the standard of our Journal or
it must, necessarily, be a low standard, and cannot rise above the highest
common factor of people with a diversity of special interests. In this matter
our position is much like that of the selection committee of a Club Library.
Usually they are afraid to buy a good book because it will appeal only to a
few and the result is that most Club Libraries contain nothing but trash.
We should aim rather at gratifying, each in turn, a variety of special interests.
It has always seemed to me that in our Journal we should aim at providing
one or two articles of special interest and, preferably in a distinct section,
others of a more popular character. But now that we have got into a groove,
it might perhaps be better to publish articles, which deal with matters out-
side our usual range, either as separate pamphlets in a distinct series or in
an occasional, perhaps annual, special number. This would increase our
expenditure on publication. But if we publish such articles separately, at as
low a price as possible, and place them on sale in the book shops, we might
hope to recoup some of the cost and, by thereby attracting new members, to
increase our revenue from subscriptions.
But here the Editor may object that he would be only to pleased to
publish such articles, in the Journal or separately, if people would submit
them; but they don't. That is one result of being in a groove. No one is
likely to submit articles which seem to lie outside our special line unless we
make an effort to obtain them. I would suggest that it should be possible to
obtain them by approaching the Departmental Heads of the Colleges in the
University, and of the Agricultural College and perhaps the Forest School.
These are all interested in the organisation of knowledge and thought in
Burma in relation to their special subjects. Then, again there is the Burma
Economic Society. There are at present various Societies, mostly struggling
to keep alive, before which papers are read which they cannot afford to
publish. It should not be difficult to come to some arrangement by which
these papers should be published in our Journal; at present they are very

There is still one other matter which seems on such an occasion as the
present to deserve attention. The object of our Society is to promote
science, art and literature in relation to Burma and the surrounding countries.
But we have mostly been content to run along our groove in blinkers, without
looking outside Burma. Burma links up India with China but it belongs
more especially to the Tropical Far East. The Hindu colonies which laid
the foundations of Burmese civilisation established outposts also over the
whole of the Tropical Far East and ever since then, and possible earlier, all
these countries have had many common links. Here then is another wide
field which we should do well to cultivate. For many hundred years Burma
was practically cut off from the world, with disastrous results. Now that it
has been brought into contact with the outside world in the sphere of
economics, the results will be even more disastrous if nothing is done to
foster cultural contact with the outside world. To do this is one of the
professed objects and might be the privilege of our Society.
It is, I think, no small achievement that, without any help from Govern-
ment, we have managed to keep alive for twenty five years, through the
years of war, the recent years of depression, and through many great
changes; and our gratitude is due to all those who have helped us, especi-
ally as editor, secretary or treasurer (particularly, one may say, to the
present incumbents for at times we have kept alive in spite of our secretary
and treasurer). But it is not difficult to foresee that, if we confine ourselves
within our present range of interests, we shall have in future a greater
struggle to survive than ever in the past. Twenty five years ago some of us
had a vision of a Society which would attract all interested in any way in
the cultural welfare of Burma by dealing with all aspects of life here, and
which would branch out, as it fostered special interests, into sections that
might in time grow into daughter societies, standing independently. Now,
after twenty five years, such a Society is even more needed than it was
then. I still believe that if we go forward according to our original concep-
tion, we may not merely manage to survive but go from strength to strength.
I would ask a younger generation whether this is only an empty dream.
J. S. FURNIVALL
