THE ROLE OF THE MASSES IN RAISING
THE STANDARD OF WELFARE
by
ANG DVAN NGU
Mr. Chairman, Esteemed Delegates, Ladies and Gentlemen,
I am very happy to present to you to-day some reflections about "the role of the
masses in raising the standard of welfare”.
Dear friends,
The different branches of natural science have discovered many basic laws of the universe,
compel it to serve more effectively to the benefit of mankind. The medical branch, a branch
of science which put to use the achievements of others, has aiso made big progress,
particularly since the end of the 19th century. Everybody knows that the learning on the
activties of the hormones, of the vitamines and of the higher nervous system, the discoveries
of the antibiotique, the biostimuline, the vaccin and serum constitute big achievements and
bring about a great lessening of sufferings and losses to mankind.
But on the other side, we have to recognize that diseases which affect most humanity are
not diseases of individual but diseases of society 1 mean the social diseases. About these,
medical science has to confess that it has not yet fulfiled its mission. The social diseases
since thousands of years have burdened the fate of mankind, and actually are still rampant
on most countries over the world. Hundreds of millions of people are permanently being
threatened by malaria. Trachoma, syphillis lapra etc. are still spreading in many coun-
tries. Very few places are free from helminthiasis, the percentage of which nearly reaches
100% in many tropical countries. From generation to generation, social diseases have indeed
receded. But this recession is mostly due to the improvement of the living standard of the
people, rather than to the direct action of the medical activities.
Is it this unhappy situation due to the lack of knowledge of mankind? No, in the
contrary, mankind had long age discovered the principles to prevent and to combat these
social diseases. Since the antiquity, it has already known that such diseases like tuberculosis
leprosy, syphillis, trachoma are contagious one, spreading from one to another through
contact with the patients. More than 100 years ago, it has been already known with certu-
tude that malaria, filariasis are diseases transmitted through the bitings of mosquitos, and
that helminthiasis are mostly communicated through contaminated food.
At present, many men of science are toiling ardently on clinics and pathogenesis of
different diseases or on the life cycle and biology of the parasite and microbe determining
these diseases. Such undertakings are to be encouraged, because very new acquirements
are always useful to mankind in the present as well in the future. But in the other side to
be able in the least time to bring about more benefits to most of the people, the great

principles to combat and to prevent these social diseases. We do not wait for more know.

knowledge about them, because of lack of application of our acquirements for their eradica-

against scoial diseases which affect the health of hundreds and thousands of people, diseases

The forces that combat and prevent the social diseases should be the forces of the whole
people, under the leadership of the specialists, of the medical men. Penetrated with the
two principles: 1st prevent rather than treat and 2nd, mostly rely on the forces of the people,
the Vietnamese medical workers have scored big achievements.
Allow me in this connection to mention some few instances taken from the records of
activities of the health Department of my country which bear testimony to the incalculable
potentialities of the masses.
During the War of Resistance in Vietnam, in areas occupied by the french colonialists,
in 1951, there were 2,640 cases of small-pox of which 1,129 died. In 1962, in Hanoi only,
there were 3,280 cases and in 1053, also in Hanoi only, 565 cases causing death to 244 persons.
But throughout the 9 years of war, in all the liberated areas which were twice as large as the
areas occupied by the french, no epidemy of small-pox was recorded. In 1954, when our
Government victoriously came to take over Hanoi, there still were 1,000 cases of small-pox
and 700 persons died. But these were the last victims left by the colonialists. Since 1953,
in all the North of Vietnam, not a single case of that disease was recorded. What forces had
enabled such a wonderful achievement in the domain of preventive hygiene? That was the
forces for the masses. If the population did not cooperate, and if the health workers were lelt
alone with their endeavours, it was practically impossible to vaccinate 19,744,488 in 2 years,
(1955-1956 and 1957).

The records regarding the fight against malaria showed more vividly the role played by
the masses in preventive hygiene. During the 9 years of the war of Resistance, millions of
people left the delta regions where malaria was practically non-existant, but which were
submitted to French occupation and terror, to come and live with our Government in the
heavily malaria infested jungles. The "transfer” of millions of persons not yet malaria
immune to malaria-infested regions could provide ample opportunities for the break out of

large scale epidemics. Nevertheless, throughout the 8-9 years of war, no malaria epidemics
was recorded. Moreover, areas previously known as the most heavily infested by malaria
had become, after having been inhabited for some time by our Government organs, relatively
healthy regions. In some place, the percentage of persons who caught the discase fell from
50% to 5%.
Mention should also be made of the fact that during the first years of war, we experienc
ed a dire shortage of medecines against malaria. The progressive receding the disease and
the successful prevention against malaria epidemics were due for the most part to the coopera-
tion of the poulation. In liberated areas, they regularly organised hygiene campaigns
destroying mosquitos, cleaning up sewers and killing the larvoe. The practices of sleeping in
mosquito-nets and chasing away mosquitoes by smoke were regular and wide-spread habits
of peple in our liberated areas.
At present, we are planning to completely eradicate malaria in North Vietnam by the
end of 1963. And we conceive that, in order to carry out a simultaneous spraying campaing
in all areas and every hope before the malaria season starts, we cannot succeed without the
cooperation of the whole population.
After malaria has been basically eradicated, once more the forces of the people have to be
relied on to detect early indivdual cases in order to prevent the diseases from raising and
spreading again.
It is not to be understood that, by reason of all the people contribute to the medical
work, the number of medical works should be lowered. On the contrary, as the understand-
ing of the people about disease increases, people want more and more advices from the
medical staff.
It was in the light of such experiences that our Government has put special emphasis on
the problem of training cadres. In 1954, when peace was restored, in the whole of North
Vietnam, there were 69 doctors 287 medical assistants, and 121 nurses. We have now more
than 399 doctors, 1155 medical assistants and 1,115 nurses. If when we took over from the
French, there was one medical worker for 220,000 inhabitants. We have now one medical
worker for 10,000 inhabitants.
That rate however is still low. And in order to achieve the above-mentioned successes,
our medical workers have to rely upon the organisation of village health workers and preventive
hygiene activists who had been trained in Health Seminars lasting from one to three months.
There are now more than 20,900 village health workers and more than 100,000 preventive
hygiene activists. Each village possesses a health section manned by one health worker and
3 to 5 hygiene activists (all part time volunteers) who look after the health of the people and
help them carry out preventive hygiene.
I only have above-mentioned some datas about the number of the medical worker But
at the same time, the quality is also needed. The medical college which has the duty to form
the leading medical cadres for the whole country, follows the principles that medical students,
from the beginning of their curriculum, has to be impregnated with the principles of preventing
diseases. In order to be able to give good advises to the people, the future medical workers

have to be acquainted from the first year with the way of life of most of the people they have
to serve in their medical life, that are the peasants in the countryside and the worker in
mines and factories. In the programme of medical study, the medical students have every year
one month in which they go to the countryside to make acquaintance with the life of the
peasants. They profit also of this occasion to organize exhibitions of parasites and microbes,
exposing their pernicious actions on human welfare and explaining how to prevent from
contracting them.
In the programme of industrialisation of our country the number of workers in mines
and factories in growing speedily. That is why, the medical student have also to go to the
mines and factories to get acquainted with the workers' life. These are reserved to the stu-
dents of the last year of their curriculum after having learned the theory about the professional
diseases. Such life outside of the 4 walls of the Faculty, quite different from their life in
town is highly appreciated by the students who are all longing for that period. During their
stay in the countryside or in factories, the students have the opportunity to put into practice
what they have learnt at the college. They also constitute a great and available force for
studying the distribution of endemic diseases throughout the country. The different Depart-
ments of the Faculty, especially the Department of Parasitology and that of Physiology, have
rely upon them to proceed research works and survey on a larges cale.
The above-mentioned experiences allow us to consider that the eridication of social
diseases aimed at raising the welfare of the people, is not a difficult matter. It requires only
the using of the force of the people to put into action the very simple principle of medical
knowledge in the field of social diseases. But in order to be able to use the force of the
people two conditions are required:
the first is that men of science and medical students have to use their mother language
as vehicule for scientific learning and propagation
and the second condition, the most important one, is that the people is confident in the
Government and fully realized that everything the Government plan and ask them to
do have no other aim than their own well-being and happiness.
