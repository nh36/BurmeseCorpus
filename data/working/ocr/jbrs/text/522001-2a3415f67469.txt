The Origin and Nature of the Provincial
Contract System:-
The Provincial Contract was the
financial arrangements made between the
Government of British India and its
Provincial Governments. Under the Pro-
vincial Contract System, the Provincial
Governments had some power of financial
control over certain heads of revenue
and expenditure of their own provinces.
One of the main objectives of the in-
stitution of the Provincial Contract
System was the "decentralisation" in the
financial relations between the Central
and Provincial Governments. This is
intended to give the Provincial Govern-
ments greater incentives to increase their
revenue collections and to economise
their expenditure.
Before the introduction of the Pro-
vincial Contract System, a highly centra-
lized system of financial system was adop-
ted by the Government of British India.
Under this system, the Provincial
Governments had very little power
of control over their own finances.
The revenues and expenditures of the
Provincial Governments were controlled
directly by the Central Government. The
Provincial Government had no power to
spend without the sanction of the Cen-
tral Government, and they had to make
annual demands for further assignment
of revenue from the Central Government
so as to meet the expenditure for every
kind of improvements in the administra-
tion of their respective provinces.
The revenue receipts of the provinces
went into the Imperial Treasury and an-
nual "scramble" had to be made to get
as large a grant as possible from the
Central Government for the provincial
expenses. Under this system the Go-
vernment of India was unable to
make equitable distribution of its availa-
Provincial Contract System
ble resources among the Provincial Go-
vernments. The Provincial Governments
also had little incentive either to econo-
mise their expenditure or to increase
their revenue collection. "The distribu-
tion of public income,” observed Sir J.
Strachey, Financial Member of the Go-
vernment of India, "degenerates into
something like a scramble, in which the
most violent has the advantage, with very
little attention to reason; as local econo-
my leads to no local advantage, the
stimulus to avoid waste is reduced to
minimum; as no local growth of the in-
come leads to an increase of the local
means of improvement, the interest [of the
Provincial Governments] in developing
the public revenues is also brought down
to the lowest level”i
These defects led to the beginning
of decentralisation of the Government
of India in 1870-71, whereby only certain
heads of revenue such as revenue from
Post Office, Railways, Salt, Opium and
Customs were retained wholly by the
Central Government. The revenues from
Land revenue, Excise, Stamps, Forests
and Registration were divided between
the Central and Provincial Governments;
the share of each head of revenue was

History of Provincial Contract System in
Burma.
Burma, under British rule, was go-
verned as a province of British India
until 1937, when it was politically sepa-
rated from India. Contemporary opinions
alleged that, as a province of British
India, Burma, being the furthest from
the centre, with poor representation to
voice its interests in the Viceroy's Council,
used to get the least annual grant for
its expenses.³
Until 1870, Burma, like other pro-
vinces of British India, had no control
over its own finances; its budget was
framed in India; it collected revenues
as the agent of the Central Government;
and it received annual grants for the
provincial expenditure, which was to be
spent strictly in the manner prescribed
by the Central Government. When the
new “decentralised” system of financial
arrangement was introduced in 1870-71,
the Central Government made over to
Burma certain branches of the administra-
tion, and a fixed annual assignment of
revenues was granted from the Imperial
revenue.
The transferred departments
were Jail, Registration, Police, Educa-
tion, Medical service, Printing, Roads
and Civil Buildings and miscellaneous
Public improvements. The principal heads
of revenue which were directly controlled
by the Government of India were Land
revenue, Forest, Excise and Marine: But
Burma derived little advantage from this
new financial system; during the five years
from 1873-74 to 1877-78, the provincial
share of the receipts averaged only

The result of the Provincial Contract
