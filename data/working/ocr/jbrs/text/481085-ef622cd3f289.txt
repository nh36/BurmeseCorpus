INDUSTRIAL AGRICULTURE
Introductory note by U Aye Hlaing, Rector, Institute of Economics.
The late Mr. Furnivall was a close
observer of the economic development of
Burma under the British rule. In the pam-
phlet Industrial Agriculture he has brought
out vividly one of the interesting features
of the agricultural development in Burma
viz. a high degree of "division of function"
which is an essential feature of Industry
rather than Agriculture. Specialisation of
function can be found at three different
levels. Firstly, specialisation in terms of
the economy as a whole, which became a
mono-crop economy depending on imports
for almost all other commodities than rice.
Secondly, there is the sectoral specialisation
in the sense that the functions of agricul-
tural production, financing and marketing
were, to a large extent, assumed by different
social groups. Finally, there is the division
of labour within production itself i.e.
different agricultural operations such
ploughing, transplanting, reaping and
threshing were usually performed by
different people. Writing in 1920 and
enthralled by the rapid agricultural expan-
sion, which was made possible by the high
degree of "division of function", he argued
as
that "it is hardly necessary to insist on the
‘advantages' accruing from an economy of
this type." He also looked with keen
interest on the course of development of
"agriculture, and of the people under
these conditions." But what had followed
was a great disappointment to him for he
noted in the Colonial Policy and Practice
(1948) * that the growing imports due to
the "concentration on a few export crops"
competed “more or less directly with local
manufactures, and created unemployment
among the artisians and craftsmen", and
that the development of agriculture along
"industrial lines" with enlarged supply of
cheap labour impoverished the agricultural
labourer and led to the growth of "a mass
of part-time labour, unable to find employ-
ment for about half of the year and with no
other source of livelihood but crime.”
Instead of growing into available economy,
Burma lapsed into stagnation and remained
an underdeveloped country. Therefore,
Industrial Agriculture is reprinted in this
issue not only as a scarce publication but
also as a record of the changing views of
an eminent authority on Burma.

INDUSTRIAL AGRICULTURE
THE essential difference between In-
dustry and Agriculture has been insisted
on by every writer since the birth of
modern economics. "The spinner is almost
always a distinct person from the weaver,
but the ploughman, the harrower, the
sower of the seed, and the reaper of the corn
are often the same. The occasions for
these different sorts of labour returning
with the different seasons of the year it
is impossible that one man should be
constantly in any one of them." Modern
industrialism is characterised by produc-
tion on a large scale, involving localisa-
tion of industry, organisation of the labour
and a specialisation of function: the
industrial unit is the mill. But the typical
agriculturist is the peasant proprietor,
performing with his family all the labours
of the field. Even where production is
on a larger scale and outside labours is
engaged, it long continues, as in Dorset
so late as 1892, to be annually hired.
This is, as it were, an artificial extension
of the family which is still the agricultural
unit, as it has always been.
However there are possible in agri-
culture advantages resulting from produc-
tion on a large scale which at times have
minimised the difference between industry
and agriculture, or even threatened to
extinguish the typical agricultural economy.
In the purchase of raw material the sui-
table disposition of his labour, in mar-
keting the crop, and, above all in obtaining
credit, the larger producer is more favoura-
bly situated. "Thus in England the capi-
talist organisation of agriculture pioneered

the way for that of manufacture. But
the introduction of machinery which can
be shared in turn by neighbours, just as
formerly they used to owe a hand in
harvest," and the many inventions relative
to the preparation of the soil have favoured
the small owner. Thus in Europe, as a
final result of the agricultural revolution,
the position of the small farmer seems
to be assured.
But in Europe the adjustment of
agricultural organisation to a world market
found the land already parcelled out in
comparatively small allotments. It had to
be superimposed upon an organisation
where home consumption was the main
object of production, merely the surplus
being marketed. In large areas of the
modern world agriculture has grown up
in direct relation to the export market.
With a single crop over a wide area and
no great range of climatic variation
agricultural organisation has assumed many
of the characters of industry. But the
fundamental distinction has remained. In
agriculture specialisation of function would
involve the maintenance of an impossibly
large industrial reseve. Labour difficulties
have therefore been surmounted either by
slavery, or by the introduction of machinery.
In both cases, in ultimate analysis, it is
agriculture true to type. The owner of a
bonanza grain farm is but a peasant
proprietor on a super-human scale, and
the implements of production, animate
and inanimate, represent a more than
patriarchal family. There is no essential

Industrial Agriculture
The distinction between industrial agri-
culture and ordinary husbandry is the
division of function between the labourers
employed. These labourers are of two
races, the mongoloid indigenes, and people
from India proper both of these may
be either local residents or temporary
immigrants. These various races and
classes, with different standards of living,
are in the labour market at different
times and under different circumstances,
and are therefore available for different
operations; and between them, as is noticed
in the report, "division of function among
the labourers is probably carried out to
the extreme limits possible in agriculture.”
The physical conditions may be recalled.
The whole area is devoted to a single
crop; on each farm, as over the whole
area, the whole of any single operation
takes place simultaneously and has to be
completed as speedily as possible: all
the fields must be ploughed within a few
days, later on they all ripen simulta-
neously. As is almost inevitable under
such conditions, with holdings of such
size, the direction and supervision of the
labour is an occupation in itself, "the
tenant or agent will usually supervise the
operations without personally taking part."
The preparation of the land is un-
skilled labour. For the most part it
consists merely of repairing the ridges
of earth between adjacent fields. This
has to be carried out between January
and May, from the reaping until the
rains put a stop to further work. This
preparatory earthwork is mostly carried
out by immigrant labourers from India.
Little Burman Labour is available; during
January they are still engaged on threshing
the last year's crop, by the beginning of
April they have been re-engaged as
ploughmen. So far as Burman labour is
95
available it is discouraged, partly by the
organisation of the Indian labour, partly
by the rate of wages paid. The Indian
labour is organised in gangs imported
from India by some labour master in
that country, and contracts can be booked
in advance. The farmer has therefore a
certainty of performance by a fixed
date and is also relieved of the difficulty
of himself organising casual labour. The
Burman moreover has a higher standard
of living than the Indian cooly, and
demands a higher wage. The rates for
earthwork have been adjusted to the
Indian scale.
The next process, ploughing, calls
for the longest continuous engagement.
This is also the operation which requires
most skill. The labourer is hired for a
period of four months. He may be
either Indian or Burman, ordinarily the
latter, a local resident or an immigrant
from Upper Burma who has come to the
delta for the ploughing season.
Even during the course of this engage-
ment outside assistance has to be invoked.
The transplantation of seedlings from the
nursery all takes place very much at the
same time during the height of the rains
when the country is one continuous stretch
of water. By this time the stream of
immigrant labour from India has ebbed,
the Indians have all gone back to their
own country, and the transplanting there-
fore has to be done by casual Burman
labour, Pulling out the seedlings from
the nursery and dibbling them in the
fields are two separate processes for which
labour is separately engaged; the latter
process is often carried out by women.
But there is no customary wage. Payment
is made eilher by time or piece-work,
and wages vary greatly with the difficulty

threshing physical conditions prevent the
cattle going very far away and there is
little grazing close at hand. One herd
therefore has to be employed for every
yoke of cattle. After the threshing the
country is open and there is a longer
interval. At this period therefore one man
or boy will superintend a herd of cattle
collected from several estates.
The general economy is thus summar-
ised in the report. "The most noticeable
feature of the system is that no labourer
performs all the operations of agriculture;
each engagement is a separate transaction,
the employer naturally seeking for the
cheapest labourer and the labourer for the
employer who will offer most. Thus it
often happens that a man will not find
employment in more than two of the annual
operations, ploughing being one and thresh-
ing the other, while even for these his
engagements are often on different estates."
With supervision, the preparation of the
land, ploughing, the two stages of transplant
ing, reaping, threshing, and the two periods
of cattle-herding carried on by different men
often of different races, and with the crop
marketed on the field where it was grown,
and credit supplied mostly by European or
Indian money-lenders, there is clearly
justification for the comment made in the
report that division of function is probably
carried out to the extreme limits possible in
agriculture.
Perhaps it is hardly necessary to insist
on the advantages accruing from an econo-
my of this type. The farmer is no longer
compelled, as in the old days, to support
his hands during those periods of the year

during the season of employment, while the
enlarged supply of floating labour and the

reduce the wages to a minimum. So far
as the organisation of labour can reduce
cost it would be difficult to produce rice
more cheaply, and under these conditions
Burma with a comparatively small gross
out-put has come to dominate the Indian
export trade in rice. It does not follow of
course that rice is proportionally cheapened
to the consumer, indeed, as has been
noticed there are conditions militating
against this. The good farmer is at a
disavantage compared with the cheap farm-
er when taking land on rent, and it is not

always possible to pay the expenses of
transplanting with its consequent increase
of yield. Nor do there appear prospects of
any immediate reductions of the cost. With
increasing specialisation the labourers tend
to be less able to develope into good all-
round farmers while their opportunities for
doing so become increasingly restricted;
they have less chance of putting together
the necessary initial capital and are increas-
ingly dependent on the money-lender.
Thus industrial agriculture, like other forms
of modern industry tends to assume a posi-
tion of unstable equilibrium between capital
and unskilled labour. The future of agri-
culture, and of the people, under these
conditions should provide interesting
material for study.
