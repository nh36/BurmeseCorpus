EARLY DAYS IN THE POLICE IN ARAKAN 1825-1828.
One of the problems confronting the Directors of the East India
Company when arranging for the invasion of the Burmese Province of
Arakan in 1824 and a problem the solution of which would not brook of
any delay, was the introduction into the invaded territory of some
system of organised administration and the return of life throughout
the Province to normal as soon as possible after the end of the war.
The Directors realised that a successful administration could be
introduced only by gaining the confidence of the inhabitants and by
assuring them of the new government's benign intentions, and for this
purpose they selected one Mr. C. T. Robertson of the Bengal Civil
Service to accompany General Morrison's column.
Mr. Robertson was at the time Collector of Chittagong and was
well known to the Arakanese refugees in that District, in addition to
which he had for some time previous to the outbreak of war been in
close touch with the Burmese officials across the frontier.
He was
therefore the ideal choice and he fully justified his selection as after
events were to prove. He was at first merely the Political Agent to
the column but, after the annexation of Arakan, he became Civil.
Commissioner for the whole of that Province, in which capacity he
comes into the story. So well did Mr. Robertson succeed in gaining
the goodwill of the people and such a profound impression did he make
on them that the hill on which he built himself a house at Myohaung
is known locally to this day as "Robertson Taung".
As General Morrison's troops advanced so it became the duty of
the Political Agent to arrange for the immediate pacification of the
countryside and, so far as he was able to organise it at short notice, its
settlement. This article, however, is concerned only with the arrange-
ments made by Mr. Robertson and his successors for the policing of the
newly acquired territory.
The early records do not indicate what civilian personnel accom-
panied the column from Chittagong but that there must have been a
certain number is evidenced by the fact that Mr. Robertson opened a
Civil Police Station-the first British Police Station in Burma-as
soon as the troops crossed the frontier in January, 1825. The frontier
between Arakan and Bengal was then, as it still is, the course of the
River Naaf.

the Naaf where the Burmese government had formerly maintained a

This brings us to the end of what might be termed the first phase
of the Police organisation in Arakan. We see a newly acquired
country with Police posts located at strategical points, responsibility for
law and order mainly in the hands of the Headmen and the "Kyouks”,
an elementary system of village police, and the regular police responsi-
ble only for those rare cases of a "heinous nature" brought to them by
the Headmen. We see also Police Station staffs composed of non-
Burmese-speaking Indians relying on their interpreter for what small
contact with the local inhabitants they may have had forced upon them,
and never leaving the precincts of their headquarter Station. All of
this was, possibly, very satisfactory in theory. As time went
however, the situation gradually changed for the worse and towards the
end of 1826 the happy-go-lucky system broke down in face of the many
difficulties which about that time it started to come up against. Ever
since the invasion of Arakan by the Burmese King in 1784 there had
been a continual migration of the local inhabitants across the border
into the British Province of Bengal. Because of the oppressive methods
of the Burmese administration and the proscribing of all those who had
anything to do with any of the many rebellions against them, this exodus
had attained tremendous proportions, so much so that Arakan is
described in 1824 as resembling a depopulated desert. At the conclu-
sion of the war, when the exiles saw that the Burmese had gone for
good and that it was safe for them to return to their homes, or to the
homes of their ancestors as the case may have been, they commenced

And so to the third stage. Their Lordships at Fort William had
remained all this time in close touch with the situation in Arakan and
they eventually decided that the whole administration there needed a
through overhaul.
A third, and Special, Commissioner, one Mr.W.
Blunt of the Bengal Civil Service, was therefore despached to Arakan
early in 1828 with instructions to overhaul and reorganise the entire
system of administration in that Province. He was armed with full
powers and was given a free hand to institute whatever reforms he
considered necessary as the Bengal system of Govenment was obviously
not suited for Burma. These duties he proceeded to take in hand
without any loss of time with the result that each department was
completely reorganised, the Police in particular coming in for their
share. It must not be forgotten that, at this state of the development
of British Rule in India, there was no such thing as a hard and fast
Police system with its detailed and centralised organisation as we
understand it today. Local authorities each had their own ideas on the
subject and these they proceeded to put into practice within their re-
spective charges with, naturally, varying success .It was not until after
the annexation of Scinde in 1845 that Captain W. Browne of the 12th
Bombay N.I. gave his attention to the problem and evolved the simple
and efficient system, which eventually became the pattern for all Police
organisations in India and the model of the India Police of today.
Mr. Blunt's subsequent report to the Board of Directors was a most
comprehensive document and dealt inter alia with every aspect of

The Police received a completely new set of instructions* as regards
their duties in general in supersession of those originally issued by the
Joint Commissioners. The references in these instructions to Headmen
are explained by the fact that Headmen still continued to be held
responsible for the policing and general welfare of their charges and
still maintained, presumably at their own expense, a number of village
police. Each Headman was therefore regarded as corresponding in
rank to an officer in charge of a Police Station, the station being, in his
case, his particular village.
In addition to these general instructions another and very compre.
hensive set was issued** which make very interesting reading and which
give in detail the duties and responsibilities of Durogahs (Officers in
charge of Police Stations), thus supplying what was probably a long felt
want. The general diary, crime reports, warrants and summonses also
make their appearance for the first time, the latter in place of the
"Purwana", or orders, which until then had been deemed sufficient.
Specimens of these are reproduced in Appendix D.
The establishments of the Police Office and of all Police Stations
and outposts were gone into and resulted in the first of that succession
of revisions of strength which have continued to be issued from time to
time ever since. Certain outposts and Police Stations were abandoned,
others were amalgamated, enlarged or reduced, and the personnel of the
surviving outposts was ordered to be found from the strength of the
parent Police Station-"to be relieved as often as may be expedient".
instead of their existing as separate entities as before.
The pay of a Burkundauze had been until then Rs. 5/- a month and
was the cause of a great deal of dissatisfaction. As Mr. Blunt points
out in his report, the pay of an office chaprassie was Rs. 8/- and that of

anything but the ne'er-do-wells and the down-and-outs, least of all a
decent type of Burkundauze. He therefore raised their pay to Rs. 5/-
a month.
