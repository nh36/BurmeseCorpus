INTEREST RATES IN ASIA AND THE FAR EAST
by
U Tun Wai M.A. Ph D. (Yale) 2
INTRODUCTION
Although it is generally admitted that economic forces operating
in the more developed countries are different from those of undeveloped
ones; many do not realize that there are differences between groups of
underdeveloped countries. Thus South American and Latin American
countries have certain forces which are non existent in Asian countries
and vice versa.
Recent economic studies of Asia and the Far East
which comprises of British Borneo, Burma, Ceylon, China, Hong Kong,
India, Indo-China i.e. Cambodia, Laos and Viet-Nam, Indonesia,
Japan, Korea, Malaya, Nepal, Pakistan, Philippines and Thailand have
revealed that even between countries within this region, some
differences are to be found not only in the pattern of economic
development but also in economic problems confronting them.
Although such differences are of special interest, one finds that a
study of the common problems facing the countries yields more fruitful
results for the formulator of policy, who can learn and act from the
experience of others. An important economic problem of the countries
of this region is that of high interest rates which has been acting as an
obstacle to economic development.
Interest rates in this region are high not only in relation to those
prevailing in the more developed countries but also with reference to the
marginal efficiency of capital in the region. It might be felt by some
that in view of the large nationalization programmes and also the
increasing amout of government participation in industry and trade,
the problem of high interest rates is no longer important on the
grounds that governments will either be able to borrow at reasonable
rates or raise the necessary finance through taxation. But the fact of
the matter is that even though governments are taking a greater share
than before in industry and trade, there are still large sectors of the
economy in private hands. Furthermore, a number of countries
continue to rely on private enterprises for bringing about economic
development.

In a study of the nature and structure of interest rates in this
region, one comes across many interesting features, some of which are
probably found not only in other undeveloped regions, but
amongst the more developed countries. We mention them here as they
form a background information to the problem of high interest rates.
Multiplicity of Interest Rates
It is generally agreed among economists that there is no single rate
of interest even though in many text books and theoretical papers the
subject is treated as if there was such a thing as a single rate. This
truism of a multiplicity of interest rates is to be found within this region
as well. Probably the spread and diversity of the interest rates here is
larger than in any part of the world. Rates vary considerably between
the organized and unorganized money and capital markets, and also
within each market. The rates in the unorganized market are very
much higher than in the organized markets.
In the organized markets, although the rates do vary somewhat
with the purpose of each kind of loan, the variations depend primarily
on the credit-standing of the borrower and secondarily on the
collateral offered. The collateral offered partially determines the rate
and also the margins. Such variations in interest rates, though comp-
letely justified from the purely economic point of view, has worked
to the detriment of the indigenous people as in comparison with
foreigners, they could offer mostly such less desirable collateral as
landed property and stacks of goods instead of better collateral of
government and gilt edged securities.
Furthermore, credit worthiness is an elusive and intangible item,
which although partly based on the net worth of the potential borrower,
yet depends mainly on the subjective judgment of the lender. As in
most countries a very substantial portion of banking was in the hands
of foreigners until recently, it was quite natural for the indigenous
businessmen to have to pay higher rates than their foreign competitors.
In Burma, out of about 21 banking offices only 2 are Burmese, in Ceylon
there is only one Ceylonese bank out of 10 banks. In Indonesia there
are 4 Indonesian banks as compared with 8 foreign banks. In the
Philippines about half of the 12 commercial banks are foreign owned.
In India in 1949, 18 per cent of total deposits and 25 per cent of total
advances were those granted by foreign exchange banks while in
Thailand in June 1949, 33 per cent of total deposits were held by
foreign banks.

In the unorganized markets, which cater mainly for the rural
population although there are variations in rates depending on the
credit worthiness of the borrower, we find that variations in rates
depend primarily on the type of collateral offered. This difference
between the unorganized and organized markets is due to many factors.
Whereas the commercial banks are normally very keen on seeing that
the loans do not go bad so as to save the cost of unnecessary overheads
arising from the legal process of foreclosing on loans and selling
collateral; the moneylenders do not care very much for what purposes
the loans are made nor whether the loans go bad. Since they have at
least another occupation namely that of a landlord, agriculturist or
trader, they do not mind acquiring the collateral and therefore charge
interest rates mainly on the type of collateral offered rather than on the
credit standing of the borrower. A commercial bank on the other hand
does not wish to become a landlord or a merchant.
Another interesting feature about interest rates in the unorganized
markets is that a substantial portion of these rates are expressed not in
monetary terms but in kind. The loan may be taken in money but
repayment may be in either kind and/or money. Sometimes the loan
itself may have been taken in kind. In Malaya the Muslim population
are against taking money interest on loans. They do, however, accept
gifts or return help in form of labour etc. when repayment is made
or even during the course of the loan.
Ineffectiveness of the Bank Rate
In almost all countries of this region we find that the bank rate is
ineffective as an instrument for implementing monetary policy. The
reasons for this are many, but among the important ones mention
might be made of the recent establishment of central banks and their
relative weakness. The Union Bank of Burma was established in.
1948, the Central Bank of Ceylon in 1950, the Reserve Bank of India
1935, the State Bank of Pakistan in 1948, the Central Bank of the
Phillippines in 1949, and the Bank of Thailand in 1942. Exceptions to
recent establishment are the Bank of Korea in 1909 and the Central
Bank of China in 1928-the Peoples Bank of China however was
established very recently. It is to be noted also that as yet no central
banks exist in British Borneo, Hong Kong, Indo-China, Malaya
and Nepal.

Lack of properly organized money and capital markets within the
region and also the probable greater importance of the unorganized
money markets plus the existence of a large non-money sector have all
tended to make the bank rate ineffective even in those countries where
central banks have been established. for a relatively long period or
armed with wide powers of control.
The fact that foreign commercial banks have been operating
on a large scale has also meant that the bank rate could not be effective
because these foreign banks do not rely upon the central bank as a
lender of last resort, but instead rely upon their own head offices in
foreign countries. It is true of course that there are other weapons at
hand to control the commercial banks in such circumstances for
example as done recently in Ceylon where the Central Bank has laid
down that foreign banks must not bring into Ceylon banking funds from
other centres.
Seasonal and Cyclical Variation in Rates
Information on interest rates in this region is not available over a
period of years. But judging from the available information on Burma
it seems that there are cyclical variations in interest rates in the
organized money market, more or less in keeping with changes in the
interest rates in the world markets. As for the cyclical rates prevailing
in the unorganized market, the variations through the business cycle
were very slight. In prewar years the seasonal variations in rates in
most countries of the region were very great both in the unorganized
and organized markets. The seasonal variations in the organized
markets however have been reduced in postwar years owing mainly to
the creation of central banks. Thus for example in India whereas in
1935/36 the call-money rate in Bombay fluctuated between per cent
and 3 per cent, during 1949/50 it fluctuated only between per cent
and 1 per cent. In most countries, the seasonal demands for credit
is met by the commercial banks running down their cash balances in
the busy seasons and/or selling their investments, especially of
government securities. In certain countries, particularly in Burma,
Malaya, British Borneo and Hong Kong the demand has also been
met by a seasonal inflow of capital.

East, in contrast with the state of affairs existing in the developed
countries where short term rates are lower than the long term ones.
There is no doubt that given the same credit worthiness of the
borrower and the same collateral offered, the short term rates in the
organized markets of Asia and the Far East are lower than the long

and per cent and the long term government bounds which yield

It is in the unorganized markets that doubt arises. Proponents of
a higher short term rate have supported their case on such grounds as
a) it costs more to handle a series of small loans with renewals than
on a long term loan, b) on a short term loan the borrower can afford
to pay more as the absolute amount which the borrower has to pay is
less, c) short term loans are taken for personal purposes as well, while
long term ones are usually for productive purpose, and thus the risk of
a short term loan is greater and therefore the interest is higher. What
all these reasons really prove is not that the pure short term rate is
higher than the pure long term rate of interest; but merely that taking
into consideration other factors which determine interest rates, the
short term rates in the unorganized markets are usually higher than the
long term ones.
Pitch of Rates.
Although there are slight variations in interest rates between some
countries within this region; generally one can speak of the pitch of
interest rates. What one finds is that the rates prevailing in the
organized markets are comparable with the rates prevailing in the
developed countries.
Thus the bank rate of the existing central banks within this region
are in the neighbourhood of 3 to 4 per cent. With reference to rate
of interest paid by governments on their borrowings we find that the
treasury bills on 3 to 9 months vary between to 3 per cent, while on
medium term and long term loans around 2 to 4 per cent depending
on the length of the loan, the credit standing of the government, the
monetary policy followed by it and also on the general conditions in the
money market. The rates paid by government owned commercial and
industrial enterprises are slightly higher e.g. the government owned

National Development Company in the Philippines has been paying

With reference to cooperative credit, the rates charged by the
apex institution (known by various names e.g. in Ceylon as the Ceylon

Provincial Cooperative Bank) to the intermediary banks vary generally
between 2 to 5 per cent. The intermediary banks lend to primary
societies at rates varying between 5 to about 7 or 8 per cent; and the
primaries lend to their members at rates varying between 7 to 15 per
cent in Burma, 9 per cent in Ceylon, between 6 and 12 per cent in
India and between 8 and 10 per cent in Thailand.
From the above it will be seen that with the possible exception of
the cooperative movement the rates of interest in the organized markets
of this region are not too high. It is only when we consider the rates
prevailing in the unorganized market that we come across really high
rates. The unorganized market caters mainly for the rural popula-
tion, and within that area mainly for the purpose of financing
agriculture. Loans are also made for petty business such as retail
trade, cottage industries, personal purposes of consumption etc. It is
quite common for moneylenders to charge generally about 18-24
per cent per annum on loans against the best collateral and generally
about 24-36 per cent on loans without collateral. On very risky loans
for very short periods the rates have been as high as 100 per cent per
annum. In India, for example, although interest rates in the unorgani-
zed markets are reported to vary between 12 and 50 per cent, the
loans by the pathans are said to be as high as 300 to 400 per cent.
Causes of the High Interest Rates
Of the various causes of high interest rates, mention might be made
of the following important ones :—
(1) Both the money interest rates and the real interest rates are
high because of a shortage of capital. This shortage of capital is due
to the relatively undeveloped nature of the region and the low incomes

existing which makes it difficult for any significant amount of capital
formation to take place. Matters have been aggravated, especially in the
field of money interest rates by the existence of outdated monetary
systems and also lack of banking facilities.
Capital is relatively more abundant in the more developed coun-
tries and thus although one might have imagined that foreign investment
in this region would reduce interest rates, what one finds is that interest
rates have not been generally lowered because (a) the amount of
foreign investment has not been of sufficient magnitude, (b) foreign
investment works only through corporate enterprises and thus affects
mainly the rate of interest prevailing in the organized money market.
(2) The lack of communications and widespread nature of the
unorganized market has increased not only costs of lending loans and
collecting interest but also the risks attached to it, and thus the lender
naturally desires to charge a high rate.
(3) The general ignorance and lack of education of the borrower
who has been accustomed to paying high interest in the past and who
because of lack of accounting knowledge, does not at times even know
that the rates are uneconomic.
(4) The large percentage of defaults causing rates to be high.
The Problems Arising from High Interest Rates
The foremost problem arising out of high interest rates is that of
slow economic development. It might be argued by some, that even
though interest rates are high, economic develepment will come about
quickly because, in an underdeveloped country, the marginal efficiency
of capital is high. Even if we concede the logic of this argument one
should bear in mind that the marginal efficiency of capital is not a
constant figure but a declining one; and therefore, from a purely profit-
ability point of view economic development is bound to be rather slow.
Furthermore, the above mentioned argument ignores one very
pertinent fact i.e. that when foreign investment takes place within this
region from capital borrowed at low rates or if domestic
investment takes place with funds borrowed at relatively low rates
from the organized markets; they enter fields where the returns are
highest. This leaves the less productive fields to be financed by the
high rates of interest prevailing in the non-organized money markets

and it is this, which causes economic development to take place at a
slow pace. Furthermore, in a poor region where those having some
funds find that they can get high rates of interest from usury with
immediate return they would naturally not be interested in making
investments which will give return only in the long run as is the case
with all industrial enterprises.
-
Another problem arising as much out of high interest rates as the
disparity of interest rates between the organized and unorganized
markets is that, in the past it tended to increase foreign economic
domination in the key fields of the economy. The foreigners were and
are able to get finance more cheaply than the domestic businessmen - and
thus there is unfair competition between the two.
One of the main causes of the modern agrarian problems of the
landless cultivator may be traced to that of high interest rates. The
cultivator was forced to pay uneconomic rates of interest for his finance
thus leaving him with no margin of profit.
In the past, in as much as the cottage industry worker had to pay
high interest rates while industrialists in foreign countries obtained
finance at much lower rates has been a factor which weakened the
competitive power of the cottage industry worker; and this helped to
bring about the downfall of cottage industries in this region.
Evaluation of Efforts Made to Solve High Interest Rate
Different countries in the region have taken various measures to
bring down interest rates. Some of these measures have been directed
towards the organized money and capital markets while others have
been directed at the unorganized markets. In the main it is true that
efforts directed only at the organized markets do not tend to bring down
the general level of interest rates by any great extent, as the rates in
this sector is not too high. But efforts to increase the size of the
organized markets and thus their relative importance vis-a-vis the
unorganized markets do tend to bring down the general level of interest
rates.
Many countries especially Ceylon, India, Pakistan, Philippines, and
Korea have empowered their central banks to control the terms of
lending of commercial banks including in some cases the interest rates
to be charged. But in actual practice practically none of them have
made use of such powers-probably realizing that this weapon is meant
to fight cyclical fluctuation of interest rates rather than to lower the
secular level of rates.

Another measure taken by governments has been to try and
promote credit aspects of the co-operative movement and also to
encourage the establishment of land mortgage banks. In spite of these
efforts, the importance of co-operative credit in the organized money
and capital markets continue to be negligible. Thus in Ceylon and
Philippines during 1949 and 1950 the short term credit supplied by
co-operative societies was just around 1 per cent of total short term
credit supplied in the organized markets; while in Pakistan and Thailand
it was no more than 3 per cent. In Burma, Japan and Malaya it was a
little higher, being between 5-8 per cent and in India it was about
14 per cent of total short term credit supplied in the organized market.
In long term credit also, available information indicates that the position
is very much the same.
The credit aspects of the cooperative movement has not developed
for probably the same reasons as for the slow development of the
whole movement. Special reference may be made to lack of organisa-
tional skill and knowledge of banking. Were this still to be present the
organised markets could very well have been adequately served with
cooperative societies or other organizations such as joint stock
commercial banks.
Some goverments have brought about the creation of a few
specialized institutions to provide long term finance at reasonable
interest rates. In India and Pakistan the governments have been
instrumental in setting up Industrial Finance Cooperations with part
ownership by the government. The one in India was established in
1948 with an authorized capital of Rs. 100 million while the one in
Pakistan was started in 1949 with Rs. 30 million. The Indian Industrial
Finance Corporation charges interest at the rate of 5 per cent per
annum and on 30 June 1950 the amount of loans outstanding was
Rs. 38 million. The amount of loans given by the Pakistan Indurstial
Finance Corporation up to 30 June 1950 was Rs. 7 million.
In Ceylon
there is the State Mortgage Bank and the Agricultural and Industrials
Credit Corporation. The former was established in 1932 and lends
primarily for agricultural purposes at rates varying from 4 to 5 per
The latter was established in 1943. It lend for both agricultural
and industrial purposes at rates varying between 5 to 8 per cent.
In the case of these institutions in Ceylon and also of those in India,
Pakistan and other countries, it is to be noted that although this
particular approach to the problem of bringing down high interest
rates has its merits, nevertheless the amount of loans made available in
relation to the needs of the economy has been negligible and thus has
not been a useful or effective weapon.

Many countries of this region have tried to bring down interest
rates by fixing maximum rates of interest which may be charged
especially by money lenders who operate mainly in the non-organized
markets, e.g. in Burma by the moneylenders act of 1945, the maximum
rates which may be charged on loans with collateral is 12 per cent and
without collateral 18 per cent per annum. These laws, as with most laws
pertaining to social control of business, are of no use unless they can
be implemented, and in order to bring this about there needs to be not
only effective executive machinery but also a relatively high standard of
moral integrity among the lenders and the borrowers. Unfortunately,
within the region the laws controlling interest rates have not been
successful as the conditions necessary for their success have been
woefully absent.
Another method of tackling these high interest rates which has been
carried out by many countries including Burma, Ceylon, Malaya,
Pakistan and the Philippines is for government to either do the lending
directly or through government owned special institutions at low rates.
of interest; or to relieve the demand for funds in a particular sector of
the economy by the government investing and itself going into the
business of manufacturing or trading etc. In Burma the government has
been giving liberal amounts of loans to the agriculturists and also going
into business itself through such government institutions as the State
Agricultural Marketing Board and the State Spinning and Weaving
Factory. In Malaya the government has been granting loans to tin mine
owners for rehabilitating purposes at very low rates of 3 per cent per
annum. In Pakistan mention might be made of the Pakistan Jute
Marketing Board. In the Philippines there are also many trading con-
cerns such as the PRATA and in Thailand the Government Rice
Purchasing Agency and Government Purchasing Bureau etc. In addition
to the above weapons, the use of which might be increased, others
which might be used to bring down interest rates are :-
1) Reduce risks of lending by governments encouraging the build-
ing of proper warehouses for storage of goods, controlling floods and
rivers for agriculture, and disseminating knowledge for the proper
and efficient operation of businesses to reduce business failures etc.
2) Increase consumer resistance to paying high interest rates.
In as much as in the past one of the causes of high interest rates espe-
cially in rural areas is the apparent foolish willingress on the part of the

In the last analysis although we have evaluated the efforts so far
made to bring down interest rates and also given a few suggestions,
when one reflects over the problem, it will be realized that all these
efforts are not so effective as compared with that of increasing the
capital stock of the economy by raising the national income. It is one
of the vicious circles that economic development if financed out of
domestic resources will not take place at a rapid pace unless interest
rates are lower and that interest rates cannot be lowered to any signifi-
cant extent without an appreciable increase in the national income and
capital. It is at this juncture that the necessity for inflow of foreign.
capital arises.
