CHAPTER IX

SAN BAW U.
The story of Sha Shujaa (Thet-thuza of the Arakanese palm-leaf
MS. history), the Sultan of Bengal, forms one of the most interesting
and romantic chapters in the history of Arakan.
In order to have a clearer perspective the reader has to be carried to
a different period and locality, where events took place that shook the
very foundations of the Moghul Empire. They were enacted in the
17th century at Delhi, the capital of Moghul Emperors. The Emperor
that ruled India then was Shah Jehan. He had four sons-Dara, Sha
Shujaa, Aurungzebe and Murad. When the Emperor felt that he was
advanced in years, he partitioned India into four divisions. He gave
Hindoostan to Dara the eldest, Deccan to Aurungzebe, Bengal to Sha
Shujaa and Gujarat to Murad the youngest. They were accordingly
proclaimed Sultans over their respective territories, the Emperor himself
remaining at Delhi recognised by his sons as the paramount sovereign.
Sometime after the sons had taken up their respective reins of govern-
ment, the emperor suddenly fell ill. When they learnt the news of their
father's illness, there was a scramble for the throne. Each with his own
army marched towards Delhi. They fought one another. In the sangui-
nary battles that followed Aurungzebe overcame his brothers one after
another. The last encounter of Aurungzebe was with Sha Shujaa, who
was defeated at the battle fought on the 15th of January 1659 A.D. at
Kidwa near Allahabad. Sha Shujaa with his family and many followers
fled to Dacca. For fear of being pursued by Meer Jumla, the Com-
mander-in-Chief of Aurungzebe, they riding upon an elephant moved to
Chittagong; and after a wearisome journey reached that city. As there
was no ship in the Port, Sha Shujaa had no alternative but to ask the
King of Arakan, who was then Sanda-thudama, to allow him and his
family to reside temporarily in Arakan and furnish him with a sailing
ship later for the purpose of proceeding to Arabia, where he intended to
settle down. King Sanda-thudama replied acceding to Sha Shujaa's
request. They therefore continued their journey by foot along the coast
of Arakan, until they reached the river Naaf. There they were received

cruelty, an enemy to oppression; in the execution of justice he had
no respect of persons, except when the natural tenderness of his

never told a falsehood; but he did not always tell the whole of the
truth. He was more tranquill, more close and reserved, than
Dara; and he was more fitted for the intrigues of party; and that
management which is necessary to direct the various passions of
men to one point. He was generous to his friends and did not disdain

Graceful and active in his own person, he loved in women that
complete symetry of limbs which rendered him the favourite of the
sex; and he spared no expense in filling his seraglio with ladies
remarkable for their beauty and accomplishments. In their society
he spent too much of his time; but the warmth of his constitution
did not make him neglect the necessary affairs of his life. During
his long government of Bengal, he won the affections of the people
by the softness of his manners, and his exact and rigorous execu-
tion of justice; and the country flourished in commerce and agri-
culture under the protection which he invariably gave to industry.

Soon after his arrival in Arakan, Sha Shujaa asked King Sanda-thudama's
help to recover the throne for him by sending out an expedition to
Delhi. Sha Shujaa presented his sister to the King to keep her in the
palace as one of the ladies-in-waiting and promised to return the twelve
states of Bengal that formerly belonged to the forefathers of King Sanda-
thudama and recognise his suzereignty. Sanda-thudama agreed to the
terms and ordered a fleet of over one thousand war-boats (graubs or
krwet-hles) to be fitted out, over which Letwe-win-mhoo was appointed
admiral-in-chief. The expedition started and when it reached the place
where there were whirpools, the Moghuls surprised them by delivering a
night-attack. In the fight that ensued Letwe-win-mhoo, the admiral-
in-chief lost his life. Being greatly handicapped the Arakanese fleet
retired and returned home without its object being fulfilled.
In the month of Tabodwe 1023 B.E. (February 1662 A.D.) Sha
Shujaa, with the object of seizing the throne of Arakan, rebelled against
Sanda-thudama. Being defeated he escaped to the source of Gachapa
nadi (Kalladan river), leaving his family and followers to their fate. The
elder son (Mohamad Bank) was wounded, and some of the followers
were killed by the infuriated troops of King Sanda-thudama. When
the King knew that the rebellion had failed, he at once issued strict
orders that the followers that remained alive should be spared. Being
renowned archers and having made them swear the oath of allegiance
by holding their arrows, the King appointed them his body-guards and
the palace had to be guarded by them in turn. The place where they
lived was therefore known as Kaman-thè-taung; the Indian word Kaman
if rendered into English means “archer". Later, in the year 1025 B.E.
(A.D. 1664) this body-guard threw off their fealty and rebelled against the
king the second time. They set fire to the palace and killed Manawtheeri,
the Myoza or governor of Myauk-u. Apparently their rebellion finally
ended in failure. It is perhaps not known to many that the race who
are now found dwelling at the villages of Thinganet and Tharagon at
the town of Akyab, are the descendants of Sha Shujaa's remnant followers
known as Kamans."
