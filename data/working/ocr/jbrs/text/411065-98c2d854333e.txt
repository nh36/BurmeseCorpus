Mon Migration
In Burma, there are many different peoples, namely, Mon,
Burmese, Shan, Karen, Chin, Kachin, Kaya, and Arakanese. They are
all native peoples of Burma and are called Burmans. Out of all the
present inhabitants of Burma, the Mons are the oldest. Their language,
Austro-Asiatic, is related more or less distantly to other languages
reaching from Madagascar to Easter Island and from Central India to
New Zealand.
In very ancient times, many peoples have passed through Burma,
mainly from north to south. The Negritoes, people with black skin
and woolly hair were the earliest inhabitants who passed through
Burma. The Andamanese and also the Smeng of Malay Peninsula are
of the Negritoestype. Various types of people came after the Negritoes
into Burma, but their languages, belonged to a common family which
Pater Schmidt of Vienna first called the Austric or Southern-family.
The Austric-family has two main sub-divisions. The first is called
Austro-Nesian or Languages of the Southern Islands. The second
sub-family is called Austro-Asiatic or the Languages of the Southern
Mainland of Asia.
The Austro-Nesian, languages of the first group are spoken in the
Malay Peninsula and in the southern islands as far as New Zealand.
The Austro-Asiatic, languages of the second sub-division extend
from India to China Sea coast. More narrowly, they comprise the Mons
of Burma and Thailand, Khmer of Cambodia, Khasi of Assam and they
have connection with the old Munda languages of Central India.

Austric-speaking peoples inhabited the whole of South East Asia during

Mon Legend
According to a Mon legendary chronical, two young princes, sons
of the king of Telengena in the Madras coast, south east of India, came
and lived as hermits on Thaton Hill, Lower Burma. Under their
separate roofs, two boys of a dragon-mother were brought up. Their
father was a super-natural-being (Magician) who returned to the
Himalayan forest after the birth of the two boys. Their Mother,
the dragon-lady left them earlier. One boy became the first King of
the Mons at Thaton (Lower Burma) which was known as Suvannbhumi
or the Goldenland. The other boy who died at the age of seven years
was reborn in India as Gavampati and became a famous disciple of
Lord Buddha. The Shwedagon Mon Inscriptions of the fifteenth
century A. D. mentions that two Mon merchants landed in India by
cargo boat and then continued their trading journey on five hundred
bullock carts. While they were wandering about in India, they were
said to be guided by an unseen spirit, and fortunately met with Lord
Buddha on the very day of his attainment of Buddhahood. After
worshipping Lord Buddha, the two Mon brothers, named Tapussa and
Bhallika brought back to their native home eight hair relics which were
enshrined at the Great Shwedagon Pagoda, in Rangoon. At first the
stupa was only 27 feet high. It was enlarged to the present size with
the height of 326 feet and gold plated in the fifteenth century by a Mon
Queen known as Banye Htow in Mon and Shin Saw Bu in Burmese;
together with her son-in-law King Ramadipati or Dhammaciti. This
shining golden Pagoda which stood on a hill of 400 feet high is the
largest Buddhist monument of its kind in the world.

The earliest Mon inscription was discovered at Phra Pathom, 30
miles due west of Bangkok, Thailand, which was edited by George
Coedes in the 50th. Anniversary of the French School of the Far East
in 1952. He considered it to be inscribed about 600 A. D.
There has also been found an octagonal pillar with an ancient Mon
inscription of about 800 A.D. at Lopburi about 100 miles north of
Bangkok and it is not far from the later Thai Capital of Ayuthiya.
In Northern Thailand, at Haripunjaya or Lamphun, about 300
miles north of Bangkok, there are seven pillars of Mon inscriptions at
different temples which are dated from eleventh to thirteenth centuries
A.D.
All these Mon inscriptions found in Thailand were edited both by
George Coedes and Rev. Robert Halliday (A. B. M.) which appeared
in BEFEO in 1929 and 1930.

some 50 miles south-west of Bangkok. The ancient objects discovered

From the architectural and sculptural remains and the ancient Mon
inscriptions that have been brought to light in Thailand, it can be said
with confidence that during the first millenium A.D.,, the dominating
inhabitants of Thailand were the Buddhist Mons.
The script of the early Mon inscriptions resembles the Pallava
script of Southern India. Therefore, research scholars consider that
the Mon derived their alphabet from Southern India. Lower Burma
is in a direct sea route with Southern India and Ceylon from which the
Mons received their literature and Buddhist religion and a slight of
Hinduism.
All the Mon inscriptions found in Thailand dercribed about the
good works done by the kings and the priests to the temples and
Pagodas. Among them, Lopburi Mon inscription is the most beautiful.
It is an octagonal pillar with a fine carving at the top of the stone
pillar. The style of writing the script is in a very archaic type. It is
now preserved at the National Museum, Bangkok.

In 1926, a relic-chamber was unearthed at Hmawza together with

Not long after 800 A. D. the Pyu kingdom at Prome began to break up
owing to internal dissensions. The last Pyu king was called Sihavikrama.
According to urn inscription in Pyu language, Sihavikrama died in 718

the south. The last Pyu Capital was at Halingyi near the town of
Shwebo in Northern Burma in which they disappeared from the scene.
From 800 A. D. onwards the Burmese people, belonging to the
Tibeto-Burman sub-family, began to appear at Pagan in Central Burma
just above the old Pyu kingdom. The Burmese people were akin to the
Pyu people.
In 1044, A. D. Anawrahta became king of Upper Burma centred
at Pagan. Pagan was at first formed by 18 villages. Sometime after
his accession, the Burmese king Anawrahta came into contact with a
young Buddhist Mon priest, namely Shin Arahan who was visiting
Upper Burma as a Buddhist missionary. After receiving Buddhism
from the Mon priest, Anawratha drove out all the Aris whom he and
his people formerly worshipped. Aris were a kind of priests who wore
black robes and practised against the Buddhist laws.
According to Mon and Burmese chronicles, Anawrahta sent a
courteous request to the Mon king Makuta of Thaton, Lower Burma
to supply him with Buddhist priests and scriptures to teach his people
the Mon form of Buddhism. It was in 1056 A. D.
The request
was refused by the Mon King of Thaton and therefore, in the following
year, the Burmese King gathered an army and attacked the Mon
kingdom. When the Mon fell under the Burmese, Mon king Makuta
was carried off to Upper Burma together with all his learned priests,
Buddhist scriptures and Buddha's relics which were put on 32 white
elephants. All Mon architects and learned men were also taken to
Pagan. The result of the capture of the Mon kingdom of Thaton was

Old Mon is so different from modern Mon that even scholars
could make no headway with Old Mon until the late Dr. Charles Otto
Blagden, Dean of London School of Oriental and African Studies dis-
covered the rules of translation. Dr. C. O. Blagden was struck by the
great difference between Old Mon and Modern Mon. So he had to
get in touch with the late Rev. Robert Halliday of the American
Baptist Mission in Burma and also spent a month with the latter in
Scotland, learning Modern Mon and comparing with old Rev. R.
Halliday had lived among the Mons both in Burma and Thailand for a
very long years and was well versed in Old and Modern Mon. The
friendship and long co-operation between these two scholars was a
happy thing in Mon language; for it not only led to Halliday's Mon-
English Dictionary and many other scholarey works on old Mon of
Thailand but also led to Dr. C. O. Blagden's masterly in four volumes
of Epigraphia Birmanica, of nearly all the old Mon inscriptions of
11th. to 12th. centuries and some of the Middle Mon inscriptions of

Mon Manuscripts on Palm-Leaf
At present, there is a considerable amount of Mon literature
written on palm-leaf on religious, historical and imaginative or legend-
ary folk-tales both in Burma and Thailand. In the manuscript, Thaton
was known as Suddhamawaddy and Suvannabhumi but it was also
called Raksapura in the inscription. Raksapura means city of the
demons.
A legendary story of the demons in Lower Burma is mentioned in
the 7th. century Ceylonese Chronicle, Mahavamsa and repeated in the
15th. century Mon inscription of Kalayani at Pegu. The demons used
to come out from the sea to steal the new-born babies. The damons
were said to be man-eaters. At last, the demons were driven away by
the two Buddhist missionaries known as Sona and Uttara who arrived
in Lower Burma in the 3rd, century B. C. They were said to be deputed
by the Great Emperor Asoka of India.
Some scholars consider this tale as to throw light on the existence
of a racial intermixture of an older barbarious race with the new comers
of higher culture and religion. The people who first migrated and
passed through Burma were the Negritoes, having blackskin and bushy-
hair. These people mostly lived in islands and they were supposed to
be called Raksasas by the new arrivals. The Skai people who are still
surviving 800 miles to the south of Burma may be considered to be one
of the oldest inhabitants of Lower Burma who might have lived on an
island which now known as Bilugyun, situated opposite to Martaban
and Moulmein towns. Bilugyun means the island of the Rakasas or
the sea demons.

Pillar of the Four Languages
Kyansitha's death bed scene was described in well known pillar of
the Four Languages which was inscribed by his own son Razakumar in
1113 A. D. This Pillar is now known as Myazedi and also called
Myinkaba Kubyaukgyi Inscription. It was engraved in duplicate on
two stones. Each stone has four sides containing four different
languages such as Pali, Pyu, Mon and Burmese.
The Burmese writing appears for the first time in this Pillar,
Razakumar was the only prince in the Burmese History who inscribed
four languages of the same matter in a four-face stone. The transla-
tion made from this inscription is as follows :—

"The Mons Present Status & Effect"
Out of 19 millions of Burma's population today, the number of
Mon speaking people is one million. The number of Mon in Thailand
at present is about half of that in Burma.
To make effective the revival of Mon Language and literature and
for publishing books on the subject, a Mon Cultural Research Section
has been established in 1953 under the Ministry of Union Culture,
Government of the Union of Burma. In the following year, an advisory
committee on Mon Affairs was appointed by the Office of the Prime
Minister of the Government of the Union of Burma. The Mon Affair
Committee is responsible for holding annually a Mon Affairs Conference
and Mon Cultural Exhibition.
Last April, it was held for the fifth year and the drama of "The
Struggle of Razadirit" (A historical play) written by me, was played
by the Mon Students. The music and songs performed in the play
were tape recorded for broadcasting twice a week for Mon listenners of
Burma and Thailand.
Mon language is being taught as a second language in State Schools
and offered as an optional subject in the University of Rangoon for
Mon Students.
Politically, the Mons in Burma are not treated as a minority race
but they are grouped as Burmans.
In future, the status of Mon people may be changed if they so
desired.
