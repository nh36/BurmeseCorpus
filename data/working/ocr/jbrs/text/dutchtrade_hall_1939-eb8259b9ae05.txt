TRADE WITH BURMA IN THE
SEVENTEENTH CENTURY
In January 1680 Sir Streynsham Master, Agent-Governor of
the English East India Company's factory at Fort St. George,
Madras, noted in his journal that he had seen a small Dutch ship
and sloop off Nasapore "bringing all their factory from Pegu." On
12th June of that same year there arrived at Batavia a Dutch ship the
Aerdenburg, from Bengal, which had been sent to Burma to assist in
the evacuation of the Dutch factory at Syriam. Her captain reported
that on arrival there he found that the factory had been closed some
time before, and the factors had decamped in haste with all their
effects to the Coromandel Coast. In consequence, he said, he had
had "much sport with the hair brained Peguers and had been arres-
ted with his ship and everything on board." Luckily, however,
through the good offices of a Danish private trader resident there,
and by the application of liberal palm-grease, he had managed to get
safely away.(1)
Such was the end of the Dutch factory in Burma after nearly
half-a-century of existence. Its history seems
to have been
written, though the many references to it that lie buried in the
published volumes of the Daghregister of Batavia lead one to think
that the Dutch archives probably contain more than enough material
for such a purpose. The present sketch, however, based almost
entirely upon the Daghregi ter evidence, makes no pretence to com-
pleteness. I have never had the opportunity to pursue the subject
further than the great Batavia record; and that unfortunately has so
many lacunae that it is impossible to tell a connected story. Thus,
although the order for the maintenance of daily registers by the
principal Dutch factories in the East dates from the year 1621, the
Batavia record does not begin until 1624, and the years 1630, 1638,
1639, 1646, 1649-52, 1654, 1655, 1658, 1660 and 1662 are missing.
And in quite a number of the existing volumes references to the
Burma trade are few and meagre. Nevertheless, after combing all the
volumes I could lay my hands on in the India Office Library, I find
that my translations run to some hundreds of pages of manuscript,
and provide such valuable evidence of Dutch trading operations in
Burma, that no further excuse is needed for this somewhat rash

The project of establishing regular official trade with Burma
originated with the Dutch factories founded during the first decade
of the seventeenth century on the Coromandel Coast of India. When
they settled there, at Masulipatam, Tegnapatam and Pulicat, the
Dutch discovered that for centuries there had been a close connection
between the Coast and the ports of Arakan, Pegu and Tenasserim.
The trade was in the hands of Indian merchants, who exported
piece-goods to Burma, and brought back precious stones, gold, lac
and tin. It was not long before the Dutch merchants began to
compete with the Indians in this trade.
Their earliest ventures were to Arakan. (2) But the dreadful
famine which attacked the lands round the Bay of Bengal in 1631
and lasted for some years, brought this trade to a temporary
stoppage. In 1634 as the result of an Arakanese embassy to Batavia
the Mrohaung factory was reopened. In June of the same year
there occurs the first Daghregister reference to official Dutch trade
with Burma. Martin IJsbrantsen, Governor of their Coromandel
factories, reports to Batavia that he has despatched the Vlielant to
Burma with a cargo valued at 35,988 florins, and that he intends to
send a further ship there in the following September.
The Burma venture and the re-establishment of the Arakan trade
must be viewed as by no means unconnected items in a big Dutch
movement for gaining control over the trade of the Bay of Bengal.
At the very moment when these moves were being made, the Batavia
Council instructed the Governor of Pulicat to send a merchant to
Tenasserim "to discover what profit is to be made in that quarter
for the Company." And only a few years earlier the first factory
had been opened in Bengal, also through the instrumentality of the
Coromandel factories. In fact at this time Dutch trade in Bengal
was within the jurisdiction of the Governor of Pulicat, and the
Coromandel Coast was beginning to assume a very important role
in the Company's operations. Batavia needed rice, vegetables and
slaves. Furthermore it supplied the bazaars of the Archipelago with
textiles, and the Coromandel factories kept a vast number of
weavers and dyers busy in adjoining towns and villages.

factory in Burma comes from a long report dated Syriam 27th
December 1635 and sent by Dirck Steur, the new Dutch superinten-
dent there, by way of Arakan to the Governor of Pulicat. He stated
that on arrival at Syriam in May of that year he had gone before the

The king's expressions of goodwill towards the Dutch were soon

Van der Stel obtained the king's permission to proceed to Ava,
hoping that the Dutch residents there would be able to secure his

were attached to the household of a patron, as was the custom with
royal slaves. The Dutch merchants daily importuned the king for their
release, representing that they belonged to the same nation and

King promised to write to the Governor of Pulicat about them, and
to set them and the captive lascars free when the next Dutch ships
came to Burma from the Coromandel factories.

Among the entries for the year 1640 there is a letter from the
Governor of Pulicat stating that he was collecting a cargo for despatch
to Syriam. He asked for a consignment of sulphur to be sent as a
present to the king in the hope that he might be persuaded to permit
the Dutch to manufacture gunpowder in Burma. He was indeed an
optimist. Although there was abundance of saltpetre of a sort in
Upper Burma, no foreigner was allowed to utilise it for the
manufacture of gunpowder, save in the service of the king. Any
attempt to smuggle it out of the country was punishable by death.
Every king of Burma lived under the constant threat of rebellion.
It was a cardinal point of Burmese policy to disarm all foreigners
entering the country. As the Dutch were well aware, foreign ships
on entering a Burmese port had to surrender their guns and
ammunition for the period of their stay there. The behaviour of the
Portuguese both in Burma and in other parts of the East had taught
the Burmese that while foreigners could be very useful, they could
also be very dangerous. Hence they must be afforded no
opportunities whatever of practising their undoubted skill in firearms,
except in the armies and arsenals of the king.
The years 1640 and 1641 furnish some interesting figures
showing the extent of Dutch trade in Burma. At the end of the
year 1640 the amount of capital invested in it was over 83,000 florins,
compared with 70,000 florins at Masulipatam and 26,000 florins in
Bengal. The profit yielded by the Burma factories during the year
1639 was estimated at between 80,000 and 82,000 florins. But this
was presumably a good year, since the arrival of a rather larger
number of Indian piece-goods merchants in the following year caused
the Dutch profits to drop to 30,571 florins.

In 1642 feelings of depression regarding the Burma trade were much
in evidence. The Batavia Council expresses its low opinion of a
trade the recent proceeds of which were only 3,820 pagodas' worth
of rubies "bought with little or no profit." The Governor of Pulicat
reports that the profits of the Burma factories had dropped to 24,901

much of this sum represented hopeless debts. Not only the Dutch,
but all foreign merchants, he explains, were treated there with in-

What was the outcome of all this the Daghregister fails to tell

any further information about Dutch trade in Burma. From the
English records of the period, however, we may infer that the Dutch
factories were not closed. In 1647 the English factory of Fort St.
George, Madras, sent an expedition to open up official trade with
Burma, and from the letters of the English residents in Syriam and
Ava a few facts about Dutch operations there may be gleaned.
It is interesting to note that while jealousy of Dutch success in
Burma in the sixteen-thirties had caused the English factories on
the Coromandel Coast to press for a factory to be opened there, and
the Masulipatam traders engaged in a fair amount of private trade
at Syriam, when Fort St. George did decide to go ahead with the
establishment of official trade, the Masulipatam factory was strongly
opposed to the step. The strong probability is that its closer
contact with both the Dutch trade and Burma itself made it aware
that there was little hope of success.
The reports from the English residents in Burma stress the
much stronger position of the Dutch there. Their large annual
presents to the king and high officials ensured better treatment than
the English ever obtained. They had also the advantage of employ-
ing more ships than the English. Hence on some occasions we find
English correspondence, and even a proportion of their trade, being
carried on Dutch ships, until the outbreak of the first Anglo-
Dutch war put a stop to such amicable arrangements. During the
war Dutch superior naval strength in the East caused them to bring
English trade in Burma almost to a standstill through the capture of
some of their ships and the blockade of others. And, according to a
report from Syriam in 1653, the Dutch in Burma did their best to
render the English "odious to the people". (9)

Our next glimpse of the Burma trade in the Daghregister is in
the year 1660. The Governor of Pulicat, Laurens Pit, reports that
the Pegu had returned from Syriam in December of that year with a
cargo valued at 70,284 florins. There are the usual complaints that
trade is bad, and that very few of the outstanding debts have been
collected. In that year also the Dutch suffered a bad disaster. On
30th September the yacht Coromandel, bound from Pulicat and
Masulipatam for Syriam, was wrecked off the Arakan coast, and of
the 42 Dutchmen and 200 natives on board all the latter and 14 of
the Dutchmen were drowned.
The reports of the year 1661 describe trade as "at a standstill."
This is said to be due to the invasions of the "mongrel Chinese."
The reference here is to the freebooter armies of the defeated Mings,
who raided northern Burma at this time, when Yung-li, the last of
that dynasty, was driven out of Yunnan by the Manchu armies and
sought refuge in Burma. (11) The Dutch reported that the Chinese
raiders had caused such confusion in the neighbourhood of Ava that
no trade could be carried on. In November news came from the
factory at Ayuthia that the Chinese had actually overrun the whole
of Upper Burma down to the Laos country just to the north of Siam.
The Dutch in Pulicat were exceedingly worried by the news; all the
more so because the ships they expected from Burma had failed to
turn up.
At last in December 1662 the Zuijlen arrived from Syriam with

Pindale (1648-61) had proved to be a most incompetent ruler.

The Daghregister lists with scrupulous care the articles handled
by the Dutch in their trade with Burma. In the early sixteen-sixties
gold, tin, lac, elephants-teeth, long pepper and Chinese copper pitjes
(13) were the chief commodities exported from their Burma factories.
There was also a refined lac, referred to as wax. In 1663 we have
the record of one ship bringing 30,000 viss of ganza to Pulicat. It
is listed as Pegus koper geld. At this time it was the most extensively
used native currency in Burma. An English estimate of its value in
1650 gives a viss as worth about sixteen pence.(14) In 1664 the
Dutch in Pulicat fixed its value at ten stivers the viss. Its export
was strictly forbidden. Foreigners, however, found that it yielded
splendid profits, and hence there was much smuggling. In 1653 the
English factors in Burma wrote gleefully to Madras that the Dutch
had been caught attempting to smuggle 3,000 viss out of the country.
The ganza had been confiscated and the Dutch gaoled. Through
some heavy bribery, however, they were liberated after three days.(15)
After the closing of the English factories in Burma, according
to the Dutch, English private traders made a regular practice of
smuggling ganza, and had their ships fitted with double bottoms in
order to facilitate the practice. The Dutch residents at Syriam
suggested that their own ships should be similarly equipped for this
illicit trade. But the Governor of Pulicat decided against the pro-
posal on the grounds that while it might assist smuggling it would
also afford the servants of the Company too good an opportunity to
carry on private trade on their own account, like the servants of the
English Company, which was less strict in matters of this sort.
William Jearsey played a big part in this smuggling game.

The 1664 letters contain a fair amount of political news.
The country was said to be tolerably quiet. The war in Martaban
still continued, and was a great hindrance to trade. The king had

Currency.-
The Dutch florin was worth a little less than the rupee in the 17th century.
A convenient table of values is : 2 rupees=2} florins=4s. 6d. English sterling.
The Pagoda, a gold coin, was the chief unit on the Coromandel Coast. They
were coined by the Dutch at Pulicat, by the Danes at Tranquebar and the English
at Madras. Early in the century its value may be taken as between 7s. and 7s. 6d.
Its value tended to rise, and in the quarter of the century it was worth 8s.
W. H. Moreland gives an excellent account of the currency, weights and measures
