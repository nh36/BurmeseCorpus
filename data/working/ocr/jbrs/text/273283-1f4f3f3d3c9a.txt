A Burma Diary of 1810.
(In the year 1809 the London Missionary Society despatched
two of its members to found a mission in Burma. The two missionaries,
Edward Pritchett and Jonathan Couch Brain, sailed from Gosport in
April 1309 and reached Madras early in January 1810; they had intend-
ed to go to Rangoon via Calcutta, but finding that the brig James
Balfour was shortly to sail for Rangoon they took passages in it at a
cost of one hundred pagodas, and went on board on the 19th February.
The following is their diary from the date of embarkation at Madras.
It is reproduced, by permission, from the records of the London Mission-
ary Society, Livingstone House, Westminster.)

21. WEDNESDAY.-Winds light and variable. Our crew consists
wholly (officers excepted) of people of colour, and these of various
nations. Our fellow passengers (who are numerous) consist also of
men of different nations and languages. Mr. Fore sails with us; besides
him we have as cabin passengers Mr. Mcpherson and his son, as also
two Armenian gentlemen, one of them a monk of the order of St. Bazil,
and a young Burman who belongs to an Armenian family of some
distinction at Rangoon. In the steerage are several Armenians, Moors,
and Portuguese, and in the fore part of the vessel are eighteen Burmans,
who were blown from their own shores by the north east monsoon
across the Bay to Masulipatam, a place near Madras; where they have
been some time waiting for a passage back to their own country. The
total number on board amounts to seventy-five persons.
Yesterday Mr. Pritchett learnt a few Burman words and to-day he
has spent some time in conversing with the Burman captain through
the medium of a Moorman who understands a little of English and
Burman. Our present situation is much more favourable than our last
for our private exercises, as we have the cabin parted off and one half
of it appropriated to our use.

At 5 p.m. a fresh breeze sprung up; by our reckoning we ought

16. FRIDAY.-This morning we have a fair and brisk wind and
are running six or seven knots an hour. Just before sunset the Captain
discovered Cape Negrais from the fore-top, bearing E. N. E. We have
sailed more than 100 miles since yesterday evening. We cannot but
feel thankful to him who bringeth the winds out of his treasures, for this
seasonable supply of it, as our fresh water and provisions are nearly
exhausted, and more especially because we are now released from the
pain of hearing such impious complaints against a providence which is
infinitely wise and generous.
18. SABBATH.-This morning we were awoke by an unusual
noise upon deck. We went up immediately, and to our surprise found
the binnacle all in a blaze, some oakum having been carelessly pla ced
too near the lamp. The flame raged exceedingly for a time, and all
were at their wits end, but it was happily smothered with a blanket, and
finally extinguished with water. Here we desire to adore that God
who while he has shewn in this affair how easily he could punish his
creatures for their unthankfulness and sins in general, has neverthe-
less preserved us from the fury of the all-devouring flame.
We have not seen land since Friday evening, although we have
continued in from 14 to 30 fathoms of water.
3 p.m. The wind dying away and the tide running strong against

23. FRIDAY.-7 a.m. The pilot came on board; he confirmed the
report we heard at Madras that nearly the whole of Rangoon was burnt
by its inhabitants, as an effectual method of expressing their discontent
on account of the heavy taxes imposed upon them, the forcing away of
so many men to the wars, etc. At noon we weighed anchor and sailed
up the river to this town and at 4 o'clock came to an anchor again
abreast of the town. We soon after went ashore, and to our unspeak-
able joy were met at the landing place by the Baptist Brethren, Messrs.
Chater and Carey, who very kindly invited us to take up our abode at
their house. At a little distance from the river is the Customs House,
or Godown as they term it, where we were rubbed down and our
pockets very minutely examined; here also we had to wait upon Mr.
Rogers, the Shawbundar (or intendant of the port), a man of our own
nation who has resided in this place for many years, has married a
Burman woman, and assumed the costume and adopted the manners of
the country, fills a very important office and is said to be a great favou-
rite of the King's. We found him in a temporary building constructed
of bamboos, attended by several inferior officers of the Customs, who
were giving in their respective accounts, sitting down upon their heels
at the same time; he had a few guards also attached to him. Mr.
Rogers received us with the greatest affability and expressed his regret
that he had no chairs to seat us upon, declaring that the fire had consum-
ed whatever piece of furniture he possessed, and all his clothing
except the Patzo in which he then appeared (which is a long piece of
striped silk wrapped round his middle, then passed between his legs, and
brought over his shoulders). We sat down with him upon some mats,
and after conversing a little upon general subjects, etc., we took our
leave. On our way to the Mission House we passed through the town,
or rather the remains of it, rejoicing that God had fixed the dwelling of

A Pra is a religious edifice of entire solid brick-work, having a
number of idols, altars, etc., placed around it at the distance of a few
paces, their worship being performed in the open air. A Kyowm is a
kind of monastery, where the pungees, or priests, reside. The dress
of the Burmans, and especially of the females, is remarkably indecent.
We understand that the term Pra is applied to everything that is held
sacred.
24. SATURDAY.-Went to Mr. Rogers to request him to exempt
us from paying the duty upon our Dollars when they came ashore.
This he said could not possibly be done, that the port regulations must
be submitted to in every respect, but that he should be able to do as
much and even more for us in some other way. Got some of our
luggage ashore, the examination of which at the Custom House was
very particular, a new set of officers having been appointed, the former
ones being removed on account of their carelessness. In the afternoon
we went to Captain Flemming's and delivered our letters of introduction.
He appears very much discontented with his situation at Rangoon (he
is a merchant and shipbuilder), speaks very much against the rigidness
of the Shawbundar, and the injustice of the Government; but at the
same time declares it to be his firm opinion that most of the public
grievances, the war, and burning of the town, is principally owing to the
Penang Government sending a certain person to Rangoon upon shipp-
ing business, and to missionaries being permitted to settle in the
country. He says such persons as we have no business here. We hope
this gentleman will not be able to do us any harm ; we can hardly expect
that he will do us much good. See Journal 13 and 14 Feby.

26. MONDAY.-Employed in bringing our goods on shore. The

town and its environs, saw many pagodas, or pras, and a collossal

supposed was about twenty-five feet long, and gilt all over.

Christian friends now contemplate Stonehenge.

4. WEDNESDAY.-Visited the senior pungee of a neighbouring
kyowm; with him we visited another more splendid kyowm, and after-
wards went with him to see the remains of another principal pungee
who had been dead for some time and who lay enshrined in wax spread
thinly over and coloured agreeably to the Burmah complexion; the
features were traced in so well, that Mr. Chater told us it formed no bad
resemblance of his countenance. The other pungees were busy in prepar-
ing a kind of square frame for it to rest upon, and a kind of fantastical
pidgeon-house to be placed over it. In this manner it will be preserved
till two or three others are dead, and then they will be taken to a place
appointed, where they will be blown to atoms by rockets, etc.
We also passed by the Court of Justice, which consists of a large
wooden platform, open on all sides, traversed with several rows of
wooden pillars which support the three roofs, the first the size nearly of
the platform, the second much less, and the third lesser still; it is by no
means superior to an English ox stall. This evening our Brethren
Messrs. Chater and Carey proposed to us to relinquish the idea of pro-
ceeding into the interior of the country, because they thought it would
be better on the whole for us to continue here, as it would tend to the
more full establishment of the Redeemer's cause in this place. We
replied that it was certainly most expedient for the present for us to
remain at Rangoon, and that at some future period we should be better
able to consider of their proposal. We also expressed ourselves much
pleased at their liberality in not considering our being even fixed at
Rangoon, should that be the case, as any interference with their mission,
and that should the Government not permit us to proceed further (which
Mr. Chater thinks not improbable) we might then be induced to settle
where we are. Mr. Carey, who has visited Pegue, says that that once
populace city is now reduced to a mere village, so great have been the
ravages of war; Rangoon has also been deprived of many thousands of
its inhabitants during the present war with the Siamese.
5. THURSDAY.-We went this evening to see the Great Pra,
called Shoee Dagoun. It is a solid building, of an enormous size (the
shape something like the doom and spire of St. Paul's, standing upon
several square platforms each banked out beyond the other) and will
doubtless prove a lasting monument of the idolatry of the nations in-
habiting this peninsula. The people of the surrounding countries repair
thither twice a year to worship Gaudma, eight of whose hairs, we are
informed, and vast treasure of gold and silver, are deposited under the

13. FRIDAY.—Hearing that an execution had taken place, we
went to the spot, and saw the bodies of three men who had been
beheaded lying at full length, their bellies, which had been ripped open,
being next the ground, with stakes driven into it through their left sides,
the viscera also appearing between the bodies. The heads were placed
to their respective necks, the faces being upwards as if staring at you,
having also a stake driven through the mouth and throat into the
ground. In this state they are to continue three days, exposed to the
action of the sun, which will greatly add to the horridness of their
appearance. Two of them suffered for what is considered a treasonable
act-using a shoee chatra or gilt umbrella; the third, who was an
officer and into whose custody the others were committed, for taking a
bribe and suffering them to escape as he was on the way with them to
Ava. The indifference with which the Burmans regarded this tragic
scene as well as the sight itself affected us much. N.B.-We have
heard that the above men were persons of very bad character.

themselves in the jungles.

16. MONDAY.-Went to the ground where the deceased poengees
are burned to witness the funeral solemnities, or rather diversions, of
the before mentioned person. A great number of men, women and
children drew the vehicles which contained them and all their appen-

rope fast to each end of the carriages and dividing themselves into two
parties pulled against each other for about an hour with various success;
and breaking off the rope, etc., when tired of their sport, the coffins

29. CIRCULAR LETTERS.-From January 1808 the Baptist
Mission at Serampore had issued a monthly report embodying the
letters of its members from their various stations; the Letters were
despatched to all members to keep them informed of one another's
progress.
GORDON, LEE AND HANDS.-Members of the London Missionary
Society in Madras. Gordon and Lee were later associated with
Pritchett in translating the Bible into Telugu.
AUGUST 8. PORT JACKSON.-Sydney, New South Wales.
OTAHEITEE. Tahiti. The London Missionary Society's members
in Tahiti had secured the protection of King Pomare and, on his death
in 1805, of his son and successor of the same name. In 1808, however,
Pomare II was driven from his kingdom and retired with most of the
missionaries to Port Jackson, where he remained until 1812, when he
regained his power and the mission was reestablished.
