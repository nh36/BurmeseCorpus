Dr. Johnson, the father of the English Dictionary says "Dictionaries are like
watches; the worst is better than none, and the best cannot be expected to go quite true."
And he defines a lexicographer as a harmless drudge.
We can therefore look upon a lexicographer as a drudge who needs a tireless capaci-
ty for taking pains. His problems are many. But I shall take only few matters that
confront him every day amidst the increasing complexity of human life and activity. The
first problem that challenges attention arises out of the daily addition of new words. The
impact of two world-wars, the vast movement of peoples and the undreamt of modern
scientific advance have resulted in an explosive expansion of human vocabulary. The
dictionary writer's ear must therefore be quick to hear the accent of the age expressed in
new words coined from day to day as well in new nuances of old words. He has to begin
where others before him have left. He must rise on the stepping-stones of his predecessors
to higher things. He deals in short with new words and new meanings.
Man cannot create things. But he can and does create words incessantly. The
influx of words is so large that it is quite impossible to admit every new word to the
dictionary. Some limit must therefore be fixed and some selection must needs be made.
The access of new words and new meanings can be gauged from the experience of scholars
who have endeavoured to bring the great Oxford English Dictionary up to date. After a
ten-year revision they have only reached the letter S, and they have to face new words like
scooter, spaceman, stiletto heels, strapless dress, strip-tease and smog.
Furthermore, there are words used only by specialists in connection with techno-
logical processes. And certain nonce words (like Englishness of English Architecture)
have been invented for some definite purpose. The lexicographer is charged with the
task of ascertaining if such words have been brought into literary use. Otherwise they
must be excluded.
The lexicographer has constantly to bear in mind that when all is said and done his
overriding purpose is to indicate, as best as he may, the exact signification of words.
This is no simple matter. The art of defining words bristles with acute difficulties. For
concrete objects such as a stick of furniture or an article of dress and for simple actions
such as tears and laughter a suitable equivalent can usually be found. But in many depart-

Again the problem of translating foreign words also demands solution. The diffi-
culty is accentuated by the fact that while there are 26 alphabets in the English language
the Burmese has 33. Further, the exact sound value of English letters like
F.H.L.M.N.S.V.X. cannot be reproduced in Burmese. All that can be done is to handle
the problem with the maximum of accuracy and minimum of irritation to both the reader
and the printer.
Speaking practically there are three methods of transcribing words in foreign
characters. First the pronunciation is imitated according to the orthographic conven-
tions. Secondly, a system of graphic transliteration is used. Each letter in the foreign
alphabet is rendered by a corresponding letter of the language to be transcribed. But this
is scarcely possible in Burmese owing to the difference in the number of alphabets invol-
ving the use of diacritical marks confusing to the reader and inconvenient to the printer.
Thirdly the basic letters of the second system may be followed without diacritical marks.
This will result in ambiguity.
