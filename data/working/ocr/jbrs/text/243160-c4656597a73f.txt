THE MINBYA CHIN HILLS OF THE AKYAB DISTRICT
BY
U THA TUN PRU,
Akunun of the Kyaukpyu District.*
1. The Minbya Chin Hill Tract has a total area of about 80 square
miles and is bounded on the north and north-west by the Arakan Hill
Tracts, on the south and south-west by the Myebon and An Townships of
the Kyaukpyu District, on the east and north-east by the Minbu District
and the Pakokku Hill Tracts, and on the west and north-west by the
Arakan Hill Tracts and the Myohaung and Minbya Township of the Akyab
District.
2. The tract consists of rugged hill ranges with many vertical cliffs.
In some places there are lofty trees and bamboo forests; in others the surface
is hard rock on which little grass can grow. There are two principal
streams, the Wetchaung and the Thanchaung, each having several tribu-
taries. All begin with tortuous courses between steep lofty banks and
finally feed the Lemro river; they have numerous very deep pools which are
full of fishes of numerous varieties.

3. Villages The villages are scattered and far away from
another, but they are all on high banks by the margins of the streams for
the obvious purpose of easily obtaining water. Each village is under the
direct supervision of a taungmin who, althoug the office is hereditary, is
appointed by the Deputy Commissioner and is responsible for the mainten-
ance of peace and order. The taungmin settles all manner of disputes
among his little community, and the litigants are generally satisfied with his
decisions, seldom or never attempting to carry matters any further. The
crime most common amongst these people, in fact the only sort of crime to
be met with there, is the abduction of married women. Taungmin is simply
a combination of the two Burmese words taung (a hill) and min (a chief).
4. Administration-For the purposes of administration the villages are
grouped into the four village-tracts shown below the next paragraph, each
tract being supervised by a village-headman, who is appointed by the
Deputy Commissioner of the Akyab District and is responsible for the
collection of revenue. There are two kinds of taxes: the capitation or
poll-tax and the taungya tax, the latter having been imposed only in 1919.
A taungya is a clearing in the forest, generally on a hillside, on which
primitive cultivation is practised.

they all belong to the Chin race-group. There are two sections, the Ledu

8. About 80 per cent of the Chinbon can speak Burmese fluently,
having acquired it by frequent contact with Upper Burmans with whom
trade relations have long been established.
9. Houses Residences are shifted from one hill to another every two
or three years. The people build their own houses and raise them on tall
posts (often of bamboo) as a protection against wild animals. On account
of the heavy rainfall the roof is made rather steep. As a rule a house does
not endure more than three years. There are six standard kinds of houses,
beginning with the ordinary house and proceeding by grades which are adop-
ted in turn as the owner becomes more well-to-do. The fourth grade is
seven yards long and four yards wide and has verandahs at the sides; the
next is ten yards long. The best kind has its posts made of 25 to 30
bamboos tied together. Very few can afford to build one of these better
houses because of the expensive feast which must be given in such a case;
for such a feast 10 to 15 pigs, 6 or 8 bulls and 2 or 3 buffaloes may be
killed and 10 to 15 pots of khaung (rice-beer) will be consumed with them.
10. Occupations-Bamboo is abundant all over the hills, and bamboo
cutting and selling are the chief occupations from which the people get
their greatest income. The bamboos are cut near the streams and brought
down by water to the Lemro river. A subsidiary occupation is mat-making;
the mats are sold to people in the plains, who use them for their threshing
floors and other purposes.
11. Rice is grown on the hillsides for home consumption. A few sell
their rice for miscellaneous expenses if they get more than they require for
their food. In such a case the Chinbon sell their rice in Upper Burma.
12. Weaving is carried on by the women of almost every family. The
looms are very simple, one end of the warp being tied to a post and the
other to a belt worn by the weaver. The cloth produced is coarse but service-
able; usually it takes about two months to produce a yard of it. Four
hundred and seventy-two looms were counted at the time of the census of
1991.
13. Dancing and Amusements-The chief musical instruments are small
drums, gongs and cymbals. People of the Ledu race begin a dance holding
a spear with both hands, and then continue dancing with the spear in the
right hand and the shield, which is made of buffalo skin in concave shape,
in the left hand. They sing and dance according to the sound of music. To
a stranger their singing and dancing appear to be perfectly ridiculous. The
former is in monotone and the latter is seldom varied. The pace of both is
accelerated not so much by the livelier air of music as by the deeper degree
of intoxication.
14. People of the Chinbon race strike the drum with both hands first
and dance without the spear and the shield, and then continue dancing with
the spear in the right hand and the shield in the left hand. They dance also
according to the sound of music. The people of the Ledu race tie up a paso
around the body before commencement, whereas that of the Chinbon put a
paso on the right shoulder.

15. There is a kind of dance between two rice pounders (wooden pestles)
which are clapped together by two other men to the sound of music. The
dancer puts his foot in and then withdraws it again in the proper time to
avoid being hurt severely when caught between the heavy clappers. This.
kind of dance is performed at funerals or in the jungle.
16. There is a kind of fishing resorted to in the stream which affords
the people a certain measure of amusement. Before the proper operation
begins whole bamboos are cut with leaves and branches intact. Then the
outspread branches and leaves are loosely tied round the bamboo stem so
as to form a sort of roll. When enough rolls are made so as to span the
whole breadth of the creek end to end, some 30 to 40 men according to the
necessity of the case get into the water some distance from the mouth and
drive the fish towards it by all manner of means such as shouting, striking
the water with sticks and so on. Then these bushy rolls of bamboo are
lowered by means of heavy stone weights so as to sink them across the
creek. When the drivers have approached sufficiently near the mouth, a
certain number of men get on to small rafts and cast the nets close to the
barrier. In this way large quantities of fish are caught to the no small
delight of the entire village.
17 Religion, religious rites and sacrifices-Both Chinbon and Ledu
are all animists. They firmly believe in the great potentiality of the spirits
of the hills and the rivers, and as a means of appeasing their wrath and
thereby averting evil they offer up the blood of pig, fowl and goat. The
differences in the performances of religious rites between the Ledu and the
Chinbon are mainly two in number. After the ceremony is over the women
of the former cannot eat the sacrificial food when it is performed in the
jungle. The women of the latter can do so. The former can make offerings
to the spirits without the inclusion of "Khaung" (fermented rice) whereas
the latter cannot do so.
18. Dress-Ledu men wear a thin strip of home-made cloth, 4 inches
wide and six feet long, wound round their loins and then passed between
the thighs. Unlike the Kamis and the Mros the ends of their cloth do not
hang down in front or behind. Around their heads another small strip of
cloth, 2 inches wide and 6 feet long, is tied; it consists of a mixture of red
and white cotton. They tie their hair into a knot which is brought well over
the forehead. They also wear large ear-rings made of nickel iron or silver.
19. Ledu women wear a short skirt consisting of two separate but
similar pieces of spotted blue or red cloth secured round the waist by a belt;
one piece of the skirt is worn in front and one behind leaving an opening at
each side. The back piece reaches to the knees; but to give freedom of
action to those joints the front piece is made a little shorter. Over the body
the women wear a short tight-fitting jacket which does not fall quite down
to the waist. Each ear lobe is graced with a stud of nickel, iron or silver or
with a tube of bamboo. Strings of coloured beads form their necklaces,
and the hair knot falls well over the nape of the neck.

20. If we judge by their dress the Chinbon have advanced further in
civilisation than Ledu. Chinbon men wear longyis, jackets and turbans much
like their more advanced neighbours of the plains; but they still tie their
hair into a knot and wear large ear-rings. Chinbon women hide their bodies
more successfully than do their Ledu sisters, for here, in their dress, a
superabundance of clothing is to be met. They wear a short dark blue
cloth round the loins. Then covering this again completely is another long
piece of cloth fastened over the breasts and allowed to fall down to the calf.
This serves as an additional skirt and jacket combined together. Another
large piece of blanket-cloth covers the rest of the body and partially over-
laps the other piece. The hair is knotted over the nape of the neck and the
ear-lobes and neck are ornamented in the same way as the former.
21. Tattooing-Both the Ledu and the Chinbon tattoo the faces of
their women at a comparatively early age. The former do it between the
ages of 11 to 15 and the latter between 12 to 15. Though this practice is
common to both yet there is an essential difference in the design adopted
by the two peoples. The Ledu women employ parallel and curved lines to
make what they call the flower pattern, while Chinbon are in favour of
completely blackening the face, so that to a complete stranger it would
appear as if the head had been dipped into a sufficiently large pot of blue-
black ink. When asked as to the origin of this hideous practice they said
that in the days of Burmese independence there lived a woman of their
tribe who was renowned for her beauty and attractivenesss. Hearing about
her the Burmese king came personally to the village, and, seeing that
the reality was even beyond what the report said of her, he carried her off
to his palace and kept her as a minor queen. Smarting under the insult
and disgrace of the whole thing, the tribe then took an oath that in future
all their women must tattoo their faces from the time of the commence-
ment of puberty, so as to destroy the beauty of the faces and prevent desire
arising in the hearts of powerful strangers who should behold them.
22. Marriage-The Ledu do not intermarry with any other race;
whereas the Chinbon of this tract inter-marry with the Chinbon of Minbu
and Pakokku Districts, with whom blood relation exists.
23.-There is no fixed age for marriage. It is contracted on the
consent of the woman's parents after the payment by the suitor of a dowry
fixed by bargaining. No female can receive a dowry; it must be received by
the nearest male relative. Sometimes the suitor makes an engagement for
one or two years as he is unable to pay the whole dowry at once. Sometimes
the suitor elopes with the girl before the payment of the dowry and gives
his services on hire for two or three years. If he is still unable to pay the
dowry in the proper time the parents or the relatives of the girl take her
back. If the dowry is not received by the relatives it is considered a disgrace
to the girl's family. They consider it proper to marry a girl great with child
though it be of another man.
24. If a woman dies in giving birth to a child before marriage the
reputed father must pay her value to her father or the nearest male
relatives.

25. If a husband wishes to divorce his wife he may do so and take all
the children, but will foreit his claim to the dowry.
26. It is worthy of note that a man sometimes elopes with a married
woman and hire themselves together as husband and wife for years. The
former husband is quite satisfied if his wife is either returned back to him
or the dowry he gave is received back.
27. If a man dies leaving behind wife and children, his wife will be
wed by one of his (deceased's) relatives who is entitled to take charge of
them.
28. I find these tribes marry with any of their own relatives except
mother, sister and niece.
29. Manners and customs-Though there are a few minor
differences in manners and customs of these people yet on the whole there
is great similarity. They usually take three meals in a day. When visitors
pay a visit to the village they are treated to a little food in every house at
meal time. When a person dies the dead body is laid out in the house and
a feast is prepared; apart of the food is set apart for the spirit which is
supposed to remain in the house as long as the dead body is there. At the
time of cremation rice is left for the spirit as well as for the dead body to
feed on.
If the oldest man in a village dies, the villagers have to fell a big
tree at the time of his cremation. Whenever a bull or buffalo is killed for
purposes of a common village feast, the horns are always preserved in the
house of the owner; it is considered an uncommon distinction to possess
so many tokens of wealth and generosity. If any one gets ill he is compell-
ed to sacrifice blood to the spirits of the hills and the rivers in order to
recover from illness; if he neglects this he is despised by all.
30. General remarks-The tract is infested with wild animals, espe-
cially tigers. I had to keep watch with about 35 or 30 men with bonfires
every night as the tigers are very ferocious. This year, I am informed, six
men were killed by tigers. One night at Kye Chaung Camp a tiger visited
the village which was quite close to my camp and took away one pig and
one dog before it was dark. One night at about 11. p.m. I found a tiger
hiding in my latrine which was about twenty yards from my Camp. How I
came to know was because one of my men threw a stone to the latrine and
the tiger at once jumped out of it and ran away. Almost every night I used
to fire my guns as I heard the roaring of the tigers.
31. All my followers had fever in the hills though I myself escaped it.
