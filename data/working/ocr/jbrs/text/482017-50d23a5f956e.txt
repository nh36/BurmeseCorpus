KING MINDON AND THE WORLD FAIR OF 1867 HELD IN PARIS
[ From documents in the French Foreign Office. ] *
Vivian Ba
When Burma participated in the 1964
Paris Fair, it seemed that King Mindon's
dream had come true.
Being closely connected with the remote
preparations for the Burmese Stand and
other liason work with the Fair authorities
I became interested in the History of Paris
Fairs. Since the greatest exhibitions of
French greatness took place under Napoleon
III, under whose Government Burma seemed
to have had the friendliest relations with
France, I was sure that there were some
documents in the French Archives inviting
Burma to participate in the Fairs.
The Imperial Government invited the
Kingdom of Burma to participate in the
1867 International Paris Fair. King Min-
don wanted to open the windows of Burma
to breezes of reform and industry and did
not hesitate to accept the invitation to
display Burmese products and handicrafts.
Franco Burmese Friendship
As the English power gained more and
more ground in and around Burma, King
Mindon was advised by his trusted roving
diplomat the Oblate Father Abbona to
seek the help of other sovereign states of
Europe to regain his lost territories. French-
men in his court fanned his hopes and one
of the principal architects of this great
diplomatic design was Ne-myo-thi-ri-ze-ya
Maha General d'Orgoni. This mysterious
personage was hated by the British. Many
considered him to be an unscrupulous
adventurer. Few praised him and sided
with him. But it was he who brought the
FIRST Burmese Embassy to the Court of
Napoleon III in 1856. (They were received
in solemn audience on the 16th October
1856.) Nopoleon III's Foreign Minister
Count Walewski sent Count Henri de Sercey
on a secret diplomatic mission to Burma in
1858. The Einsheman, a patron of indus-
tries and higher studies, sent three young
Burmans to study in Paris when Count
Henri de Sercey returned. One of the
students was named Mg Shwe O, and he
studied in the Central School of Arts and
Manufacturers which still exists today, and
turns out civil engineers as in the days of
Maung Shwe O. (This brilliant student of
the household of the Einshemin later
beceme the Phangyet Woon - See Guardian
Monthly 1962). The Director of the above
school was Monsieur Perdonnet who was
appointed Guardian of the Burmese Students
by the French Foreign Office after Count

The Fair took up an area of 687,000m2.
There were 11 million visitors. Total

23,440,000 francs.
The First Paris Fair was held in 1798.
It was held in the Champs-de-Mars like
several others which followed and its crea-
tor was Francois de Neufchateau. Then
there were the Fairs of 1801, 1802, 1806,
under the Empire. Then those of 1819,
1823 and 1827 under the Restoration.
Three other Fairs took place under the
Monarchy of July in 1834 and 1839, one
under the Second Republic in 1849. Under
Napoleon III, on the same Champ-de-Mars
(Field of War) two Universal Fairs were
inaugurated in 1855 and 1867. It was his
apotheosis, and to this great display was
Burma invited. Of all the Fairs, that of
1867 remains memorable for two reasons:
first for the display of magnificience which Official Invitation to King Mindon
nobody had dreamt of before and in the
second place for the frenzied gaiety of the
crowds. Everything was brilliant, high,
mammoth. The town planner of Napoleon
III, the Baron Haussman, conceived it to
be the most brilliant show of the earth.
The Champs-de-Mars, is that great
esplanade lying today between the Eiffel
Tower and the Military Academy. Besides
being a military exercise field it had been
known for the Revolutionary ceremonies
of the celebration of Fraternity. It was
there that the executioner beheaded a
Mayor of Paris, and Napoleon distributed
the eagles to his victorious legions and the
Legion of Honour to his moustached
grenadiers. There, the crowds flocked to

Alea Jacta Est
But there were troubles in the palace
and the 'Ein-she-min,' the most enthusiastic
inspirer of the Fair was assasinated, in
August 1866 and in the turmoil Burma did
not participate in the Fair. In a letter to
the French Foreign Office expressing his
sorrow at the disturbance to the studies
and activities of the Burmese State Scholars
caused by the death of their patron, the
Heir to the Throne, Mr Perdonnet wrote:
“ALEA JACTA EST!”8
He could not change the course of
events, and the Exposition for Mindon's
Burma remained a great and lovely dream,
until it was realised in 1964.
