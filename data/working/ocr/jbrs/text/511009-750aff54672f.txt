1: Life in Rural Areas
The annexation of Burma in 1886 as
a part of the British Indian Empire has
been accepted by historians as the water-
shed between Old Burma and Modern
Burma. The changes resulting from the
passage of the old order to the new in the
revenges of time range from conflicts
(internal and external) to the recovery of
independence on the 4th January 1948.
Their political aspect lies beyond the scope
of this study. We are concerned with the
interior values that lie behind the exterior
aspects of Burmese society.
Burma's shifting political fortunes have
effected vast exterior changes. But the
inner life of the people has not been altered
appreciably. Particularly in the rural areas
the hard core of the spiritual life has
defied the erosion of time. For a just
appraisal of the Burmese inward life it is
therefore necessary to bend our attention
to the life as it is lived in the rural areas.
This will be clear when we bear in mind
implements. The cartwright makes carts.
The carpenter builds houses. Spinners and
weavers turn out fabrics. The potter makes
pots.
In larger villages master-craftsmen
train their apprentices in the advanced
crafts that require technical skills such as
the manufacture of lacquer-ware and
silver-ware, wood and ivory carving,
painting, sculpture and architecture.
2: Burmese Optimism
Judged by modern standards, the
Burmese villager may appear to live a
humdrum life. Yet he is quite contented
with his lot. He is not unduly perturbed
by the jar and fret of life. Nor is he
tormented by ambitious thoughts. His wants
are few and simple, and nature is indulgent.
He need not therefore toil and moil cease-
lessly for his daily bread. His life is swayed
by his religious belief that a person's Karma
follows him relentlessly through the entire
cycle of existences. He cannot elude the
present consequences of his past actions
(Karma). What is to be must therefore be.
Why should he give way to carking care?

5: Pagoda Festivals
To all outward seeming the villager
leads a grey existence. But in truth and
in fact his life is punctuated by seasons
of merriment. In addition to the custo-
mary seasonal festivities, he has his merry-
makings which centre the pagoda

festivals. Consequently the pagoda festival
has the greatest attraction for the villager.
It is held from time to time either in
towns or in fair-sized villages. It is
mainly religious in character. But inciden-
tally it affords an occasion for reunion
with old friends when fragrant memories
of the past are revived as well as for for-
ming new friendships. Above all it is a
time of general rejoicing when the air is
vibrant with the sound of dance and song.
Numerous small booths can be seen dis-
playing for sale home-made wares and
other artistic products There are also
sheds where games of chance are played
on a small scale as a concession to the
popular love of gaming.
Villagers from the vicinity flock toge-
ther ostensibly on a pilgrimage They
travel in bullock-carts that grate and jolt
their leisurely way. The younger folks con-
sider the festival as nothing more or less
than an excursion if not a picnic. The
festival lasts for a few days and parties
break up and return home with
satisfaction that they have made the best
of both worlds. The expenses of the fes-
tival are met by public subscriptions.

and structure.

East Asia have as their original pattern
the ancient Hindu stupa or dagoba which
to begin with was a sort of mausoleum.

The stupa with its soaring tower repre-

When the Burmese pagoda-builders
came within the orbit of Indian influence,
they assimilated what appealed to them in
the Hindu shrine. But under Mon influence
their mental edge was greatly sharpened,
and they erected as a supreme act of
worship a shrine which is peculiarly their
own. With the new inspiring impulse their
mind proliferated and their creative genius
came to full flower. It is but truth to say
that the Burmese shrines show marvellous
applications of the principles of artistic
construction and arrangement, and an
exquisite blend of taste and imgination.".
The word pagoda has been traced
back to the Pali Dhatu gabbha which means
a 'relic chamber'. Its prime purpose is to
enshrine the relics of Buddha. It is plainly
impossible to have the sacred relics in
every pagoda. Pagodas therefore fall into
four divisions. First comes the dhatu zedi.
(ဓာတုစေတီ) which enshrines the sacred relics
or their models. Next we have the paribawga
zedi (60) in which the eight
(ပရိဘောဂစေတီ)
utensils of monks take the place of the
relics. Then there is the okdeiktha zedi
(2) which enshrines images. Lastly
we get the dhamma zedi. (c) in which
the sacred books are deposited.5.

The Burmese Pagoda gives us the
feeling of an attempt to stretch into
infinity. There is a continual strain heaven-
wards. Its tapering top strikes us as an
artistic striving to imitate Mount Meru
of Buddhist cosmology.
The basement or plinth is a four-sided
solid mass raised over the relic chamber
(g). The gate-ways at the four
cardinal points recall the four sides of
Mount Meru which are protected by the
guardian spirits of the world (lokapalas).
Above the basement there are generally
three receding terraces pyitsayans which
would correspond to the three-fold divi-
sion of the world of sentient beings-kama-
loka (the world of desires), rupa-loka (the
world of forms) arupa loka (the formless
world)..
Higher still is the bell-shaped dome
with the ornamental lotus flower (Kyalan)
which seems to be a development of the
original hemispherical dome of the Hindu
stupa. This is capped by what appears as
an inverted monk's alms-bowl. (သပိတ်မှောက်)

9: The Boat Race
Among the festive outbursts that ruffled
the even tenor of rural life of the past
the boat race was peculiar to the neigh-
bouring riverine areas with trained oarsmen.
It took place some time after the full moon
of the month of Thadingyut (late in
October). It was the focus of local in-
terest. The best crew of one riverine village
challenged the best crew of another riverine
village. If the challenge was accepted, the
race was fixed on the nearby river or
stream. Not only the villages concerned
but the neighbouring villages and towns
were all agog with excitament about the
coming event. On the appointed day

of the river or stream where the race was
to take place eagerly discussing the chances
of their favourites. The sun would be hot,
but its sultriness was forgotten.
The rival boats each manned by a
crew of 12 or 24 paddlers made a preli-

The glimpses we have had of the
Burmese rural life project an image of its
mental climate. It has its own sense of
good and evil enabling one to live the
good life. Every member of the community
lives on the whole a tranquil and conten-
ted life compatible with its condition and
capacity. Happily modern civilization has
not yet succeeded in making serious in-
roads on its moral standards and good
manners which constitute the very pith
and marrow of its culture.
