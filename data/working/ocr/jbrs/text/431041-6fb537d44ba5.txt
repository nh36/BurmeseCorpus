INAUGURAL ADDRESS, BY J. S. FURNIVALL
A
It is my privilege this morning to join with the President in offering a very hearty welcome
to the distinguished visitors from foreign parts who have so kindly come to help us in
celebrating this Jubilee, the fiſtieth anniversary of the foundation of our Society. We all
hope that you will enjoy your visit and will carry back to your homes pleasant memories
of new friendships made or old friendships renewed. I must admit that I would have pre-
ferred the comfortable and dignified position of emeritus, but the Committee overruled my
protests. Possibly they wanted to show their zeal for antiquarian research by exhibiting
me as a specimen of Ancient Man in Burma. But there may be another explanation.
Jubilee is a time for jubilation, for enjoyment. One may expect a learned Society to offer
a feast of learning, but I trust you will find it more than that. For us it is a festal occasion,
a fiesta, and we would like to make it a fiesta in the fullest sense. Some one it sounds
like G.K. Chesterton-has said that, without a sacrificial victim, a fiesta is a fiasco. So perhaps
it is as a victim intended to propitiate the assembled deities that I stand before you, an
antediluvian, a troglodyte dragged from the obscurity of his cave with eyes dazzled by the
stronglight of a new dawn.
Is there a new dawn? That is a question which 1 hope to answer, but first let me
accustom my eyes to the daylight by turning them towards the past. As in pious duty
bound, I must begin by asking you to remember U May Oung and Professor Duroiselle,
without whose help the Society would never have been founded. Professor Duroiselle was
still with us until a few years ago. Among other notable scholars who have passed away
since we held our Silver Jubilee in 1935, first in age and first in learning, must come Dr.
Blagden, who unveiled the secrets of the Pyu script and did so much for the study of old
Mon. Then, not unworthy to be mentioned in the same breath, Dr. J. A. Stewart, to
whom Burmese literature is so greatly indebted, and whose genial personality many here
will still remember. A more recent loss was that of Mr. Swithinbank, a former President
of our Society, than whom perhaps no Englishman has been more dearly and deservedly
loved and respected by a wide circle of Burman friends. The memory of J. H, Telford
survives in his profound monograph on Animism in Kengtung. And I must not forget to
mention R.B. Dennis, less widely known because of his modest and retiring temperament,
but whose scholarly work on Burman customs promised a flood of light on Burmese ethno-
logy had it not been darkened by his early death. In the melancholy list of those whom
we have lost two names stand out to represent local scholarship. Professor Cassim, a
former President, was a Burman though, as his name implies, not wholly of Burmese origin,
Dr. Aung Thein was the leading authority on the relations in modern history between
Burma and Thailand.
Only one name, you may notice, is Burmese. The reason may be seen in the pre-war
record of officers and contributors. Among the five Presidents between 1934 and 1941 there
were only two Burmans and, of these two, only one represented scholarship rather than
official status; there was only one Burman among the successive Editors of our Journal and
none of the Secretaries was a Burman. The tale of chief contributors to the Journal
presents a similar picture; the list comprises 33 names between 1935 and 1941, but only
6 were Burmans; their contributions were not the least valuable but very few. From its

Now, perhaps, after this backward glance into the twilight of the past, my eyes are
strong enough to look round at the present. They are certainly strong enough to recognize
a great change in the constitution and activities of our Society. After the war it did not
come to life again until 1948, but then it was re-born under new auspices. The stimulus to

unknown in 1935. Here surely there is a promise of a new dawn, with light already strong
enough to dazzle the eyes of a troglodyte like myself, dragged from my pre-historic cave.
That is not all. Not only has the constitution of our Society been transformed and
its membership so widely expanded but so also have the character and scope of our activi-
ties. The original intention was that the Society should comprehend all aspects of literature,
art and science in relation to Burma and its vicinity. At our twenty-fifth anniversary I
expressed regret that, through various circumstances, our activities had been so narrowly
restricted to literature, language and ancient history. Within thiss pecial domain we had done
useful work but, outside this narrow field, even in Burma we had done practically nothing,
and we had made little or no attempt to look outside Burma at the neighbouring countries. I
then urged the need for a special effort to get out of our groove. That effort has now been
made. Since 1952 we have held an annual Conference to which scholars, Burman and
non-Burman, have contributed papers on subjects ranging from solar cookers to the frogs
and toads on the University estate. Such contributions have not been restricted to the
annual Conference; at an ordinary meeting of the Society in 1952 our President, Dr. Chit
Thoung, tried to impress on Burmans the need for research in natural science. (Perhaps,
however, I may remind members of my former suggestion that the Journal should ordinarily
retain its former character and that contributions outside the usual line should be published
separately in one or more special numbers.)
Research-what does this mean? Some years ago I reminded members that the word.
"search" came from the Low Latin circare to go round in a circle, and suggestee that to re-
search was to go round and round in a circle. Now, if we are still going round and round
in a circle, it is a much wider circle. We cannot even go round in a circle with our eyes
shut; to search implies that we are looking for something. Most people, I suppose, have
at some time an impulse to learn something about the world, and especially about the land in
which birth or some other caprice of fate has cast their lot. We have an impulse to look
round. But it is dull work looking round alone; we tire of it and settle down to accept with-
out question the routine of daily life. People forget much that they may have noticed. Here
our Society can help them. It provides a medium for recording their observations so that they
are not forgotten and are brought to the notice of others who may be stimulated to look
round and record their own observations. It also gives facilities for discussion which may

Under its own rulers Burma was shut off from the outside world with disastrous
consequences. Under British rule its contact with the outside world was almost exclusively
economic. Now, independent Burma has to face two major problems, one is to achieve
national unity within its natural boundaries; the other is to bring the people of Burma into
effective human contact with all aspects of the larger world outside. These problems are
conditioned by the geography and history of Burma. But the forces impinging upon Burma
have no such limits; they have been operative also within the other countries of the same
region. Dr. Toynbee in his Study of history deprecates the tendency of historians to
choose the nation as the normal field of historical study, because the forces in action are
not national but proceed from wider causes, which operate on each of the parts and are not

During recent years there has been great progress in studying the internal history of
Burma: even in this we must look to Indian and, as Professor Luce has so convincingly
demonstrated, to Chinese sources. But I would insist that we shall never read the history
of Burma correctly unless we read it in the light of what was happening at the same time
in the other countries of South-east Asia. Some authorities question the unity of South-
east Asia because the several countries are so widely separated by the salt estranging sea.
Yet the sea that appears a barrier to intercourse between peoples may also be a highway
joining them, and in early days travel by sea, once out of the range of coastal pirates, was
less dangerous than travel by land among strange peoples. Long before Hippalos discovered
the secret of the monsoons, canoes from this part of the world found their way to Easter
Island and Madagascar. If, then, the forces impinging upou Burma have been active through-
out the whole region we are likely to arrive at erroneous conclusions as to their reactions on
Burma unless we examine their operation elsewhere. The study of neighbouring lands in
their relation to Burma, though greatly neglected, has always been a professed object of our
Society. The presence of our distinguished visitors suggests that in other lands also the
need for such a comprehensive study is recognized; we can learn much fron them and
possibly they can learn something from us. This conference seems a convenient occasion
to urge that the peoples of all these lands have much in common in their origin, their
history and, of more immediate practical importance, in the current problems of the present
day. That is why there is now a greater need for an organisation such as ours, with the
study of neighbouring lands as one of its professed objects. I may as well confess at once
that on none of these matters can I speak with the authority of a scholar. But I wish to
invite your attention to some points that in a rather wide range of casual reading have led
me to look on South-east Asia as a whole and have raised problems requiring a compara-

who are better informed, I may be merely emphasizing the obvious or, on the other hand,
just talking nonsense and providing enterteinment rather than edification.

Whatever question there may be as to the essential unity of the region, it is beyond
question that the whole region has had to sustain the successive impact of identical forces
impinging on it from the outer world. For a thousand years or more from the beginning
of the Christian era, intermittently if not continuously, the whole region was permeated by
merchants from China and, more intimately, by influences from India ; Hindus and Buddhists
were succeeded by Muslims coming from, through or round India, and these, from the 16th
century onwards, by adventurers from Portugal or Spain, the Netherlands, England and
France and, for a time and again recently, Japan. Trade was the magnet which attracted
these foreigners, and I would suggest that the varying course of traffic between East and
West is a thread linking together all these lands in a common historical process. There
have always been two main lines of traffic; the land route across central Asia and the sea
route through the Straits of Malacca with the Straits of Sunda as a possible alternative.
When the land route was blocked communications could still be maintained by sea, and
vice versa; when both were blocked there were several by-roads through Burma to the sea
coast beyond the Straits or else overland to Yunnan, and it was at such times that the
countries on the main land were involved, with consequently major changes in their internal
history.
The influence of diversions of the trade routes can be traced back to the very dawn of
history, when, shortly before the Christian era, tribal migrations in central Asia cut off
the supply of gold to India from the north and turned attention to the land which came to
be known as Suvannabhumi Again about 120 B.C. trouble from the tribes along the
main land route to the west led Wu-ti to look for a safer southern route, and it was by the
route across the north of Burma that the so-called mission from the Roman Empire reached
China in A.D. 97 By this time Hippalos had discovered how to use the monsoons, and
Indian and Chinese merchants had learned in the Red Sea to build large ocean-going junks.
There was a rapid development of maritime trade, and subsequent missions from the West
took the sea route and crossed the Isthmus of Kra or made their way to China round the
coast. Shortly afterwards, about A.D. 250 the Great King of Funan founded the first maritime
empire, achieved mastery over the Malay archipelago, built a city on the peninsula, and

In the 7th century Fu-nan finally collapsed. One may find a possible explanation
of its fall in other changes during the same period. It has been suggested that just as

Then in the 11th century, Srivijaya, exhausted by its long effort to control the eastern seas,
was challenged by a new rival for sea-power, the Chola empire of South India. Shortly
afterwards Anawrahta was pressing southward into Tenasserim, and the Cholas make a
fleeting apperance in the history of Burma. During these long centuries the sea route
governed the destinies of South-east Asia, but relics of the past in Arakan and at Pagan,
Prome, Thaton and the Three Pagodas near Martaban and various places further east
suggest a land route as a by-road which the succesive sea-powers of Funan and Srivijaya were
unable to control. One may find suggestions also that the maritime powers attempted to
control the upland route and that upland peoples pressed southwards either to capture the
sea-route or merely to loot the cities along the sea route that had gathered wealth from
trade. Only by looking at the region as a whole does its political history become intell-
igible.
A comprehensive survey of the whole region is no less important for an understanding
of the culrural history of each country; the evolution of writing, religion, architecture and
the other arts, and law. It was from India that all these peoples learned to write, even in
these parts which have since adopted Chinese. Among the traders from India who, during
the early years of the Christian era, were founding out posts along the sea coast and up
the main rivers were some who bequeathed their civilisation to the Pyu of ancient Burma.
Now there is something very remarkable about the Pyu inscriptions. In other parts of
South-east Asia we can follow the transformation of Indian characters into a local script; a
gradually degenerating Sanskrit giving place to the local vernacular, Indian names for people
and places giving place to local names, and the emergence of local princes, probably of
mixed origin, and other signs of the gradual assimilation by the local people of the civilisa-
tion which they took over from the Indian traders. In Champa, more than a hundred
years elapsed between the first extant Sanskrit inscription and the first in Cham.
hundred years before the Burmese began to use their own language instead of Mon in their
inscriptions. Yet in the Pyu inscriptions there is very scanty evidence of any such process.

No one, I suppose, would question the paramount importance of the evolution of
religion in this part of the world, and here especially comparative study is indispensible.
Everywhere in South-east Asia the early inscriptions reflect the influence of various forms
of Hinduism and Buddhism. It has been suggested that the Indian impulse to oversea
travel was largely due to the expansion of Buddhism with a consequent relaxation of caste
restrictions. Yet there are hardly any traces of Buddhism in the material relics of the
earliest stage of Indian expansion over South-east Asia. The most ancient, however the
inscription at Vocanh, dating perhaps from A. D. 150 in South Annam is Buddhist, and so
also is the Red Earth Rakta-mrittika inscription on the mainland opposite Penang, dating
apparently from not long after A. D. 400. One feature of interest in these inscriptions is
that they are in Sanskrit, indicating presumably the Sarvāstivādin sect which in its origin
differed from the Theravädins in little else than the use of Sanskrit instead of Pali for their
scriptures. Whether the Buddhists in Java to whom Fa Hien seems to refer as extirpated
by a wicked prince were also Sarvastivadin is unknown; in any case they have left no
material remains. But the Sanskrit found in some of the early Pyu inscriptions has been
held by Dr. Ray 1 to indicate Sarvāstivādinism, and this would seem still more probable if,
as appears almost certain, the Indian father of Pyu culture flourished in Prome long before
the presumed date of the inscriptions.

It must be admitted that details regarding the early permeation of South-east Asia by
Indian influence are largely conjectural, but it can hardly be questioned that the whole
region was involved in a common process. This is no less apparent in the new era which
began with the rise of Mongol power in China. One outstanding achievement of the
Mongols was the reopening of communications by land between China and the West by land;
never, says Yule, was contact so free from artificial obstacles as between 1250 and 1350. 2
But the Mongols were not satitfied with mastery over the land route and their intervention
in South-east Asia resulted about 1300 in the atmost simultaneous over-throw of all the
major dynasties from Pagan to Annam and Indonesia, and the emergence of numerous
small principalties whose obedience the Mongol rulers of China could more easily command.
Similarly the decline of Mongol power about 1350 opend a new chapter, with the birth of
new states in Toungoo, Hanthawaddy, Laos and Sumatra. Still more significant was the
contemporaneous rise of a new wholly commercial power. The link between China and
the West by land was broken and the sea route more than regained its former primacy.
By this time the control of the spice trade was passing to Muslims from the west of
India and from Arabia. Some Muslims however, sailing to Malayan waters from Bengal,
left their mark at ports in Arakan and Tenasserim, and in Arakan infused new strength
into a line of kings that was destined to intervene at a crucial point in the history of
Burma. The significant development however was that the rise of a commercial power in
Malaya favoured the growth of two new empires on the eastern side of the Straits in Ayuthia
and Majapahit, both land powers rather than maritime powers.
A few significant episodes must suffice to illustrate subsequent links in the common
history of the region. When the Portuguese challenged the Moors they made straight
for Malacca and the Spice Islands. It was not until they had been turned out of the
Spice Islands about 1580 by a general revolt that they became prominent on the mainland,
and de Brito, Nga Zinga, under the patronage of Arakan, aspired to become an independent
prince in Burma. The Dutch had some thought of short-circuiting Portuguese sea power
by a land route through Burma to Yunnan, but they gave up the idea as soon as they had
hemmed in the Portuguese at Malacca and could sail freely in Malayan waters. They
retained a longer interest in Arakan, but here again they withdrew when they were strong
enough to obtain rice in Java and slaves in the eastern islands. The English in turn had
a passing glance at the land route to Yunnan through Burma, but chose rather to risk their
fortunes at sea, either in alliance or competition with the Dutch. Shortly afterwards it was
the Dutch command over the archipelago that impelled the English and French to open

So much then, for the common history of the region. It should hardly be necessary
to argue the fundamental identity of the problems which face all these peoples at the
present day. In all these matters there is a need for co-operative research between all these
peoples in the solution of their common problems and I would like to believe that this
Conference may be a step in that direction.
Finally, since I have been cast for the part of Polonius in this performance, perhaps
I may be allowed to indulge in the vanity of reminiscent moralsing. When we set about
founding the Society not many people had great confidence in its longevity. When asked
to become life members, they would suggest that, as they hoped to live rather longer, it
would cost less to pay as annual subscription. I hoped that it would live for fifty years,
though I cearainly did not anticipate that after fifty years I should still be addressing it.
About that time I had recently come across a phrase in a letter of William Blake.
It is a
phrase that has stuck in my memory. “perhaps and I verily believe". I commend it to
your attention.
Belief that things we want will happen is no sure guarantee that they will
happen; but unless we believe in them, they will not happen. Our Society will live to
celebrate its centenary. But it will not do so unless we believe in it and act up to our
belief. Some years later, a cognate association chose for its motto "The bricks have fallen
down, but we will build with hewn stone" Dr. Julian Huxley quoted this to UNESCO as
an ancient Burmese proverb. If he had been as intimately acquainted with the Bible as
with human physiology, he might have recognized it for a text from Isaiah, with a sub-
acid flavour of scepticism. For hewn stone may crumble, and each succeeding generation
must face anew the task of conservation and reconstruction, and will do well to bear in
mind the apocryphal beatitude: “Blessed are they that expect nothing, for they will not be
too deeply disappointed". With these few memorabilia I commend our Society to the care
of a younger, a much younger, generation, and may conclude with Nunc dimittis, Let thy
servant now depart in peace.
