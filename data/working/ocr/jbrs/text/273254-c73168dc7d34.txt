1. Recruitment.
The Burmese Army.*
I propose to begin at the beginning and show how a Burman joined
the army. In time of war, of course, every male Burmans of any age
was liable to compulsory service; and not only Burman men but even
Burmese women. It was King Bodawpaya who said, "It would matter
little if all the men were dead, for we could then enroll and arm the
women." There is nothing unique about this, for women have always
been fighters: e. g. the Amazons, Queen Boadicea, a Siamese Queen in
the sixteenth century, and so on. Burma itself suffered from female
opponents; when the same King launched four armies against Siam in
1785, one attacked Junk Ceylon, beyond Mergui, but was sternly resist-
ed by the islanders under the Lady Chan, widow of the late Governor,
and her younger sister, and had to retreat within a month. I only know
one example of ladies fighting for a Burmese army, and that was the
case of the three beautiful daughters of the Sawbwa of Laikha, who
fought against the British near Prome in 1825 and were supposed to
be able to turn bullets aside. When her contingent was defeated at the
battle of Natpadi, one of them retired on her pony across a stream, but
a shell exploded over her head and she fell into the water. Captain
Snodgrass adds, not very gallantly, "whether killed or only frightened
could not be ascertained." The wife of the Yewun of Rangoon accom-
panied her husband on the same campaign, but there is no evidence
that she fought. Not only Burmans but Shans were liable to recruit-
ment; the Shan States, in fact, had each a fixed quota, which was
supposed to add up to 90,000 men, and about a tenth of that number
of Shans was actually called to the colours for the first Anglo-Burmese
War. But our present concern is recruitment in times of peace for the
standing army. Naturally enough, this was mostly from Upper Burma.
The people in Lower Burma were largely Mons, Karens, Arakanese,
and so on. King Alompra won his throne with the help of Upper
Burmans, and he was one himself. His capital and those of his suc-
cessors were in the upper country. The Upper Burmans were close
at hand and therefore much more easy to recruit and control, and they
had a tremendous tradition of loyalty to the throne of Burma. Cer-
tain regions were noteworthy as recruiting-grounds; such were the
present Monywa, Mandalay and Kyaukse Districts for infantry; Shwebo,

recruitment grew up in a haphazard way. In the reigns of Alompra
and his immediate successors there was a lot of fighting, and mass
levies were the order of the day. But the regiments were gradually
being formed, and when more peaceful times arrived the quotas were
fixed. There were two censuses in the reign of King Bodawpaya in
which the peace-time and war-time quotas were settled. In actual fact
the regiments never managed to keep up to their peace-time strength.
Many villagers disliked military service. Some of them entered the
service of Queens or Princes, or contracted to supply them with oil or
tobacco, or something of that sort, and so secured exemption from
military service. Others bribed the recruiting officers, or simply lay
low. In the published Hlutdaw records we can read the complaints
made by officers that men were evading service. Very roughly
speaking, each regiment was supposed to be about 500 strong.
As long as a man was in a military family, there seem to have
been few other qualifications required. No cripple, of course, civilian
or military was ever allowed in the palace. But a soldier could be
almost any age. I met a man in the Lower Chindwin who joined up
at 15, and I have been told that some of those on duty were as much
as 60. There were about ten platoons or thwethauksus in a regiment
and each platoon was recruited from a certain number of villages.
Each village had to supply so many men at a time, and the families
took turns. Headquarters asked no questions and did not worry as
long as the correct number was furnished. Some of them did, as a
matter of fact, become reduced from deaths and migrations and could
not keep up their quota. If a man died or deserted the village had to
replace him. In some regions, there were daings which were originally
groups of blood-related families. These daings later became simply
a collection of neighbouring households. Each daing supplied usually
one man at a time, but a big daing might supply several men.
Those who stayed at home had to make a contribution called
nauktaukkye or daingkye to support the man who was on duty. This

in service, especially after the introduction of regular pay.
When it was his turn, the recruit would pack his few belongings
into a bundle and set forth on foot. He would get a lift on a bullock-
cart or a boat where he could. Men from the Lower Chindwin made
for Myinmu whence they went by country-boat up the Irrawaddy to
Mandalay. In later days, I am told they occasionally had the thrill of
being able to board one of the King's steamers for the journey. If a
man were married, his family went with him. In fact this was one of
the few qualifications for a soldier that the authorities normally insisted
upon. Service away from home was unpopular with many and a family
was a comfort to a man and could also be held as hostages to prevent
his deserting. There were occasional investigations and the unmarried
were ordered to provide themselves with wives. This they did on the
usual hiring system, a Mandalay woman receiving a fee to officiate as
wife until the officers' vigilance relaxed again. Old soldiers have told.
me that on one marriage parade couples were ordered to embrace, and
that those who were really strangers to each other were betrayed by
their awkwardness amidst loud laughter. I imagine, however, that the
great majority of men brought their wives from their home village or
else married at the Capital and had genuine households. Finally the
recruit reaches Mandalay. He lands at Gawinzeik and walks through
the western suburbs to the block where his regiment lives. The minor
infantry battalions lived outside the walled city to the west of the inoat,
and the cavalry and artillery were in the open country to the east. If
he is in the Household Brigade, he crosses the bridge over the west

Brigade also had their own rice granaries in their quarters.
2. Tattooing.
Next came the recruit's enrolment. In times of war soldiers were
sometimes made to drink oathwater (thitsa-ye) but in peace-time only
high officials did this. The recruit became a full-blown private when
he was tattooed, a hse-daw-hto ahmudan. There may have been
some idea that to tattoo a man made him invulnerable or gave him
other powers, but the main object was to identify him as a soldier.
Once he had been tattooed, he could not deny his liability to military
service, and also it was his passport into palace grounds. The only
entrance to these was the Taga-ni or Red gate and the guard allowed
only those to enter who had a tattoo mark or who bore a permit issued
by a Minister of State.
I have been told that there were once two professional military
tattooers, Mg Ba Baw and Mg E. When they died, the work was done
by anyone in the regiment who knew how to, or else by a civilian from
Mandalay town. The best of the latter were Shans who had a lighter
touch and traced clearer designs. By some accounts, the operation
took place in the palace itself; and one man has even told me that on
occasion the King himself would be present. He would trace the tattoo
mark on the man's body with some wooden instrument, presumably a
sort of stylus. I have never seen any confirmation of this, and would
be interested to know if other members have heard of it. The Govern
ment paid a fee of four annas a man to the saya.
The parts of the body that were tattooed were the back of the palm,
or the nape of the neck, or the hips, or the thighs, or the legs. A

3. Uniform.
Our next concern is to get the soldier suitably attired. At first
there were no fixed regimental uniforms. These were first decreed in
1832 for seven regiments. They became more general in King
Mindon's reign after the arrival of the French and Italian instructors.
Finally near the beginning of King Thibaw's reign it was decreed that
all must have uniforms. They could not get enough material in
Mandalay or Rangoon, so the whole order was placed in Calcutta. The
Konbaungset tells us that an official was sent there with over three
lakhs of rupees, which he spent on broadcloth tunics and trousers, and
that on his return he was given a reward of Rs. 3,000. The supply was
limited and if a man's uniform were lost or damaged he had to replace
it himself. Sometimes his women. folk could weave it for him, but other-
wise he had to buy it. A gaungbaung cost four annas, a jacket twelve
annas, and a paso two or three rupees. The coats and trousers and
possibly other items were the King's property; they were kept in a
brick building to the east of the palace, and were drawn on the morning
of a parade by one regiment at a time, under the supervision of one of
its sergeants, who was supposed to check the issues with a list. Going
from top to bottom, the first thing is the helmet or hkamauk. There

Next we come to their weapons.
4. Arms.
The oldest and most familiar and in many ways the most useful
weapon was the dah. I have heard that most of the military dahs
were made at Mondan near Pyawbwe, which is still a great centre for
metalwork. I have not seen one, but I imagine they were more like the
dalwe than the dashe of the present time. The recruit was already
familiar with this in his village, and could make excellent use of it on
campaign. He could cut his way through jungle, cut and fashion tim-
ber for stockades, and use it for hand-to-hand fighting. Till recent
times the men on guard at the palace were armed only with dahs.
Another popular weapon was a spear, usually only a pointed bamboo
though some had iron spikes. The dah and the spear, however,
gradually gave way to firearms. Like most other things, the Chinese
claim that they invented firearms, but they were used in Europe and by
the Saracens long before they were used in the East. They are said to
_ ve been first used with any effect in India when they won the battle
of Panipat for the Emperor Babur in 1526. They appear in Burma the
very next year, when a shot from a jingal, presumably Portuguese,
killed the Chief of Ava. The traveller Caesar Frederick found well
made cannon and arquebusses at Pegu only forty years later. These,
too, were probably Portuguese. Most of our early examples are in fact
from the Mons in Lower Burma, who had more direct access to
Europ n influence and employed foreign mercenaries before the Burmese
did. In about 1553 the Governor of Sittaung was wounded by a mus-
ket shot by a Portuguese named Gonzalo Neto, who was given a reward
of ten thousand crowns for his skill. Some of the cannon that Alompra
took from the Mons in the 1750's were reputed to be already two

5. Duties.
Now that we have the Burmese soldier suitably clad and equipped,
we must teach him his duties. European methods of drill were
probably introduced after the first Anglo-Burmese War. Six Indians,
who had served in a British-Indian regiment, were taken on as drill-
instructors, and were even sent out to Tabayin where they drilled
recruits for about three months in 1831. They received a salary of
twenty-five ticals each. In the same year Coloned Burney saw some
soldiers drilling at Ava. They countermarched, did manual and
platoon exercises with some Burmese variations, and fired some quite
good volleys. A good deal later, in King Mindon's reign, French and
Italian officers were appointed as instructors. These men had most
interesting careers, and it is a pity there is no connected account of
them. Near the end of 1879 a camp of 16,000 men was established
under these instructors outside Mandalay, and parades and manoevres
were held. From that year onwards, grand reviews took place once a
month during most of the dry season, usually on about the full-moon
day. The troops mustered outside the palace-stockade, were sometimes.
reviewed by the King himself, and then marched out of the city gate
and round the moat. Perhaps the biggest was the one held in October
1879, soon after the arrival of the new uniforms from Calcutta. The
cavalry were nearly 5,000 strong, and the other arms made up a further
24,000. They started their rounds from the Sinshin gate, and the last
detachments were only just passing through the gate as the vanguard
got back to it. There were also assaults-at-arms for infantry and
cavalry, polo matches, and other forms of exercise and training.
Shooting also was taught, and a certain amount of target-practice took
place. The range seems to have been somewhere in the open country
to the east or north-east of Mandalay. The foreign instructors were
sometimes in charge, but not always. Shooting practice was not an
entirely new thing. As long before as the seventeenth century, Caesar
Frederick noted that daily practice was held at Pegu and that the men
became reasonably good shots. In 1824 Bandula soon realised its
importance, and the working of his heavy guns was seen to show a
considerable improvement. Old soldiers have told me that they did not
go to the range very often and that most of them were indifferent
marksmen,

Myelat Wun moved his headquarters to Payagon, which was near the
borders of Karenni and British Burma, and a strong post of at least
fifty men under a tathmu was maintained there until the annexation.
Soldiers were also employed by the authorities in miscellaneous
civil duties. They are said to have dug the moat at Mandalay, and
they were put on to making canals and tanks to irrigate royal lands.
They were also employed as carpenters and masons to build monasteries,
and as gardeners.
6. Discipline.
As regards discipline, we know a fair amount about how this worked
in the Burmese army. If a man had a grievance he could lay his case
before a senior officer or he could adopt the civilian mode of redress
by putting a written plaint in one of the four petition-boxes on the walls,
and I have heard of several cases where this was done and redress was
obtained. We can read in Sangermano and Harvey and in the Konbaung-
set that military discipline in wartime was very severe. Even in peace-
time the men were liable to heavy punishments, but these were not
always exacted. A number of old soldiers have given me instances of

particulars of the man's offence. For a serious offence like murder a
soldier was usually handed over to the civil authority, namely the
Myowun. Some soldiers have told me that they disliked their military
service, but others stayed on for many years longer than they need and
evidently enjoyed it. There were very few desertions from the peace.
time army.
7. Pay.
Having made the soldier work and even punished him, it is high
time that we paid him. In early days pay was occasionally given to
those fighting in a war, but a regular monthly pay for each soldier on
duty in peace time as well as in war was only introduced in King
Mindon's reign. Till then the soldier's main cash receipt was his
nauktaukkye, which, as we have seen, was the contribution that he got
from his village. But he had a number of other emoluments, the chief
of which was land.
There were huge areas of service land all over Upper Burma. In
theory, the King owned all the land but allotted lands to his soldiers in
return for their services. Mr. Hardiman, who was Settlement Officer
in the Lower Chindwin, thought that the villagers already owned the
land and that the Government imposed military obligations on them.
By calling them service lands, Government guaranteed their security of
possession. Whatever their origin, they were common by the seven-
teenth century, if not before They were demarcated by a rope, and
were sometimes called kyodwin mye in consequence. The record of
the soldier's lands and obligations was entered in a deed of grant, called
a kyolan sittan, from the seventeenth century onwards, and this was
kept at the capital. Normally speaking, no land revenue was paid on
service lands. The general name for the lands was ahmudan sa mye,
and they were divided into sa mye and ne mye. The former were

in theory, though not entirely in practice. The land was supposed to be

the money into the royal treasury. In exchange for the supposed loss
of their lands, the soldiers actually on duty were to receive a salary of
ten rupees a month from 1872 onwards.

We next come to the organisation of the regiments. I am speaking
mainly of the infantry, but much of my information applies equally to
the other arms. For both peace and war time, the privates were
divided into groups. Each five men or so formed one o za: that is,
they were supposed to share a common rice-pot and on active service
to occupy one tent or hut or other form of bivouac. Two messes form-
ed a squad, a kyat, under a corporal. About ten o zas or five kyats
formed a thwethauk su. Thus means a blood-drinking group. Its
origin is well known, and it was formerly quite common in other parts
of the world. Each man made a cut in his arm or on the tip of a
finger; sometimes the blood would be mixed with spirits, and some-
times an animal's blood was provided instead. The concoction, which-
ever it was, was then drunk by the men of the group who swore that
they would stand by each other just as firmly as blood-brothers. There
are many instances of the forming of thwethauks, civil as well as
military, in the chronicles; but I do not know how long the actual
ceremonies of blood-drinking lasted.
Nor for the officers and other ranks. The man in charge of a
squad was an akyat, which we may consider about equal to a corporal.
He was practically always a man appointed from the ranks of the same
regiment, as were the sergeants or thwethaukgyis. These in fact may
be considered the N. C. O.'s. They alone formed part of the village
organisation and received nauktaukkye when on duty. Officers of
higher grades usually had no military connection with any particular
village, and received no subsidy and were sometimes appointed from
other regiments. An akyat corresponded in seniority to the head of
a daing or perhaps a hse ein gaung in a village, and his duties seem to
have been purely military. He had an allotment of service land of
about ten pes. He received a village contribution (presumably at a
higher rank than a private) when on service; and latterly he drew pay
at the rate of Rs. 15 or 20 a month. The thwethaukgyis were the
backbone of the regiment, as I dare say the sergeants are at the present
day. They were old soldiers promoted usually on account of merit,
and they did most of the work in organising and drilling the fifty men
under them and in general regimental duties. In some parts of Burma
they do not seem to have had any powers in their villages; but else-
where such as Sagaing and Pakokku they had considerable powers,
and were able to extend them at the expense of purely civilian head-
men. In fact, it is hard to tell what were their official duties, and what
were the duties that they took over from the headman. In some places
the same man was both, and both jobs descended in his family. They

10. The Artillery.
These foreigners take us on to the Artillery, another very interest-

11. The Cavalry.
That finishes with the foot-soldiers. I have just time to say a few
words about the cavalry. These were only 5,000 strong in King
Thibaw's reign, and usually fewer than that in earlier times. The truth
of the matter was that cavalry could only be effective in dry country
like Upper Burma. They could not get about for half the year in wet
countries like Lower Burma, Tenasserim, Arakan, or parts of Siam;
and they were of little value in thick jungle or in hills at any time of
the year. The units at Mandalay had no guard-duties, but there was
a cavalry troop in Pyinmana which preserved internal order, manned
police stations, and sent out patrols. A number of the regiments were
not formed until King Mindon's reign. The most famous of the Bur-
mese cavalry were the Cassay Horse, who are mentioned in nearly all
accounts of the first and second Anglo-Burmese Wars. English writers
in fact gave them a reputation that they never enjoyed among the
Burmans. They consisted of descendants of men captured in Manipur
in the eighteenth century and settled around Amarapura and Mandalay.
They spoke Burmese and dressed in Burmese clothes, and in later days
they were hardly distinguishable from Burmans. Actually there were
two regiments, Kathe Myin and Ekkabut Myin, each under its Myin
Wun, who was usually a Burman. Their quarters were close to those
of the other cavalry units to the east of the moat. Shwe Yoe tells us
they were one of the smartest detachments he saw on parade and that
they wore red pasos and jackets of French grey. There were also
Shan squadrons, and they formed one half of the Myinsugyi regiment.
The organisation of the cavalry was not unlike that of the infantry.
There was a myinthugyi or a myin zi who controlled about ten men
like an akyat. Then there was a myingaung who ruled another fifty
to one hundred men, like a thwethaukgyi. He was of equal rank to
a headman, and in fact often was a headman in civil life. Then there

same pay. Most of them had no special uniform, though some were

or gilt flaps of buffalo leather which hung over the saddle girth. These
were to protect the bare legs of the troopers as they rode through
jungle. The flaps prevented a man from using spurs or even from
gripping with his knees, but he could use the noise of the flaps to urge
his pony on. Yule also has a picture of a cavalryman, which shows
the flaps well, and he says they were nearly three feet in diameter,
and that they may be a relic of horse armour and were probably intro-
duced from Manipur. They were usually white. The saddles had a
high peaked pummel and cantle, which helped a man not to fall off.
The style of riding was the same as it is now in the Burmese villages,
a matter of balance rather than grip, and the ponies were no doubt
similar. Some of the men carried spears or lances that might be seven
feet long, and all were armed with a dah.
From the time of Alompra, pony gymkhanas were frequently held
near the palace, and there are interesting accounts of them in the
Gazeteer of Upper Burma and elsewhere, and old soldiers have given
me some further details. The events were in two classes, for amateurs
and professionals. The former were open only to persons like princes,
civil officials, and infantry officers. One of their events was to throw
spears at the gallop at targets on high poles. In the events for cavalry
officers and men, they sliced a lime on top of a five-foot plantain stalk,
or cut the stalk so deftly that a pot on top of it did not fall to the
ground. They speared a lime on the ground, or shot at targets with
their muskets at full gallop. The men of at least one regiment could
ride standing in the saddle with the reins in one hand and the spear in
the other, and they would throw these from one hand to the other.
Apparently there was no entry fee for these competitions; but a rider
who fell off or otherwise disgraced himself was put in the cattle-pound
with his charger and only released when he had paid a fine. This went
to swell the amount of prize-money given by the authorities, and prizes
of clothing were also given.

Last of all, a few words about the war-boats and their crews
These were quite effective in medieval warfare, but obviously could
not deal with large fast moving warships well-armed with quick-firing
guns. The boats, however, were kept on for ceremonial purposes, and
the boatmen were also employed as crews on the King's ships, which
were built from the 1840's onwards in the marine workshops on Amara-
pura shore. These ships were for both peace and war and actually took
part in the Third Anglo-Burmese War. The men were well disciplined
