FOUR NOTABLES OF THE LOWER CHINDWIN.
In the reign of King Bodawpaya four noteworthy Buddhist
monks flourished in a small group of villages to the north-west
of Budalin, three of them becoming national figures.
The present sketch only deals with their lives in their home
villages, their careers in the wider world of the capital being suffi-
ciently well-known.
My information was almost entirely derived from elderly learned
men in these villages in 1933 and 1934, and has for the most part
not been verified from other sources. I place it on record, so that
their knowledge shall not die with them.
1. U NYANA.
1. Younger Days.
A certain U San U fought for King Alompra in the wars against
the Mons and was given the title of Natshin Yeikkha. He married
Ma Kwe of Maungdaung, a large village to the north-west of
Budalin, and a son was born to them in 1753 in a house that stood
to the west of U Oh Gyi's compound in present-day Maungdaung.
At the age of ten, he became a novice in the Tawya Kyaung
monastery a mile north of the village, and ten years later became
a monk under the name of U Nyand. I have also heard him called
U Nemeinda, which appears to be wrong. He acquired great learn-
ing and spent much of his time at Amarapura, going back to the
Tawya Kyaung to keep the lent each year. In 1779 he wrote a
grammar called the Neik Hti Tiga Thit, and in 1781 the Thiha
Letkhan Tiga Thit, which described the rules for making religious
offerings and the benefits thereby obtained. He became the saya of
King Singu Min's uncle, U Waing, Prince Badon, who in 1782
reigned as King Bodawpaya. The capital was moved to Amarapura,
and U Nyana was made Thathanabaing with four other learned
monks with a view to enforcing uniformity of doctrine upon the
whole church. His views, however, differed from those of his
colleagues, and he was made sole Thathanabaing under the title of
Nyana Bi Kawi Taza Maha Dhamma Yarzadi Yaza Guru. He had
three Sayadaws to assist him. In 1793 he and his mother began
the Myagundaung Pagoda which still preserves his memory at his
native village, and in 1801 they built the neighbouring thein.
U Nyana's monastic name was Nyana Bi Wuntha Dhamma
Thenapati. He was a prolific writer, being the author of no less
than forty different manuscripts. Among his works were the Nya
Daw Wi Sin Kyan, the Pitagat Linga Ya Mi Thanetti Tiga Thit and

as well as his failing powers allowed, dying in 1833 at Ava aged
about eighty and leaving no issue.
2. Buildings at Maungdaung.
Two buildings of interest remain a mile north of Maungdaung
close to the Public Works Department road from Budalin to Kudaw,
and in the Ngayewin village tract. One is the Myagundaung
Pagoda, so called because the shape of the upper portions is said to
resemble a betel leaf. It is described as the Kwandaung Pagoda
at page 90 of the Upper Burma Gazetteer Volume 2, Part 2, which
adds that it is "not known by whom the pagoda was built." Most
of our information is derived from a stone inscription which was
formerly visible but has now been bricked up in the pagoda, after a
copy had been made and placed in the house of U Oh Gyi. The
land was owned by U Nyana's mother who probably built the
Tawya Kyaung at which he studied. On the same site the base
of the pagoda was completed on Tuesday the fourth waxing of
Tabodwè 1154, which is the middle of February 1793. Valuable
offerings were placed in the middle of the pagoda on Wednesday
the twelfth waxing of Tabodwè. In 1801 he built the Kundaung
Payagyi thein on the north of the pagoda, a small brick building
now in poor repair. His mother gave an area of 1,600 seiks for
it. It was customary to obtain the consent of the king and of the
Myosa or Ywasa before consecrating land, and this was done, and
the Twinthintaik Wun, Maha Sithu, himself a native of Maungdaung,
was sent thither from the capital. He made a list of the revenues
and property of the thein. Many headmen were also present at the
consecration, which is described at length on the inscription-stone.
The ceremony took place on Saturday, the tenth waxing of
Tabaung 1162, that is the middle of March 1801. Eleven learn-
ed Sayadaws gave their approval, and seventy-four monks were
present. To avoid the three possible irregularities in consecrating
the thein, they cleared a space of four cubits from east to west

2. THE HERMIT OF KADO.
Kado is thirteen miles north-west of Budalin. A furlong to
the north-east is a ruined monastery called the Mok-teik kyaung,
where is a cave in which the hermit used to live. In the reign of
King Bodawpaya he was a pongyi, and he wrote a work named
Myin Gyan, which described the good and bad characteristics of
ponies, at the request of U Yan Gin, apparently another local man,
the son of Dwaya Kyaw Din, a keeper of the palace gates at Amara-
pura. The book was published latterly by U Kyaw Yan of Mandalay.
The original manuscript is said to be in the hands of U Po Sin of
Maungdaung. Latterly the author became a hermit, living and
dying at Kado.

4. U NYEYA.
1. HIS FAMILY AND YOUNGER DAYS.
The present sketch of U Nyeya deals mainly with his early
days in the present Budalin and Kani townships of the Lower
Chindwin District, and is largely based on information obtained from
members of his family and other residents near his birth-place.
His grandfather was headman of Wetyè in the north-west of
Budalin, in those days in the north-west of the Alon wunship.

His sister married a man of Wetyè and lived there for many
years. Near the end of U Nyeya's life, she was offered a house
near his monastery in Mandalay, and she built a pagoda near his
famous one at Payagyi. She probably died before he did.
Her son Tha Ni became a hermit and dwelt in an existing red
cave near Payagyi; his descendants are still to be found in
Maungdaung tract. The two grand-daughters of her other son are
also living in Maungdaung. U Nyeya's brother Tha Hmu had
two grand-sons, of whom the elder became a pongyi; the son of
the younger is U Oh Gyi, first Chairman of Lower Chindwin
District Council, a member of Maungdaung Village Committee, and
a mine of information about the storied past. Ma Nu his sister
married U Tun Thaung, the present headman of Maungdaung.
Maung Ya Saung, great-grandson of U Nyeya's other brother is
another resident of Maungdaung. Maung Tha Hmo as the future
prelate was called, was born in 1163 B.E. (1801) on the spot where
the Payagyi Pagoda now stands. His mother died not long after and
his father married again. His step-mother was Ma Ngwe U, and
her children were Tha Sin and Ma Mè Tha, who both had
descendants. Tha Hmo's relations with her were strained, and
he absconded to Alon, but was brought back. He however soon
found consolation in his studies, at which he proved peculiarly apt,

expenses of his ordination being borne by a certain U Waing, who
built the Tawya Kyaung and was an ancestor of the present
headman of Kani. He returned awhile to Chaingzauk, but soon
resumed his education under U Kyan. His teacher seems to have
been dissatisfied with his progress, and is alleged to have struck
him with a leather slipper, U Nyeya thus joining the goodly com-
pany of famous men who as boys were 'bottom of their form'. He
was either sent by U Kyan, or from unhappiness at home fled
away of his own accord, to Ava the capital, to continue his
education under the The-in Sayadaw.

2. HIS WORKS OF MERIT.
From almost any place in the north of Monywa District the
twin peaks of Natyindaung are conspicuous. The northernmost
is only 776 feet high, and the southern 625, but they rise from a
wide flat plain and can be seen from afar. There are some good
views from the west bank of the Chindwin, especially from Kani hill,
and the view from the two humps themselves is an equally extensive
one. The Lawkamarazani pagoda, or the Paya-gyi as it is always
called, is at the western foot of the hill. It has a height of 180 feet
and cost Rs. 26,000, as is recorded on two tablets in the precincts.
U Nyeya came at least twice to superintend the building, probably
the only occasion that he re-visited his home village. King Mindon
and the royal family provided some of the money and Wuttagan
lands for its upkeep. In later years U Nyeya himself bought fifty

Whilst at Payagyi, he resided in a monastery called the
Sangyaung which is just south of the pagoda. It is in good order,
having been repaired a few years ago. He also dug a tank not
far away.
There is a small pagoda at Wetyè, which is said to have been
founded by King Asoka. The present building is of similar design
to the Payagyi, and the Upper Burma Gazetteer 2-2-91 states that
it was built by a chief Buddhist priest' of the village, that the
date of its erection is unknown, and that there is a festival in
October. Actually it was the work of U Nyeya. At the time of
the famine, the villagers moved to Maungdaung, Inbaung and
other villages: but U Nyeya presently brought them back and
re-founded the village as Ywadaw. They were exempted from
'thathameda' as long as they kept up the pagoda. There used to
be a small concrete statue of an an elephant in the pagoda, which
the guardian pongyi sold for about Rs. 1,500 some thirty years ago.
A dispute as to its ownership arose, and there was a civil suit,
which ended in the Thakuttanè headman getting a decree for
Rs. 1,500 against U Nyun, the then Township Officer of Budalin.
The headman gave the money to the pongyi and kept the statue in
his house, whence it is still brought out on the occasion of 'ahlus'
and other ceremonies. The exemption from payment of 'thathameda'
was revoked in King Thibaw's reign, and Ywadaw became Wetyè
again. U Nyeya also dug the Myittagan tank at Wetyè, which
remains to the present day.
The eighty-nine households of U Nyeya's relations in
Maungdaung were also exempt from taxation during King Mindon's
reign. The memory of U Nyeya is still held in reverence in the
neighbourhood.
