Most authors maintain that rice starch cannot be prepared by
tabling the slurries. They base their opinion on the fact that the rice
starch granules are very minute, ranging in size from only 3 to 5 micra
(Brautlecht). Thus when rice starch slurries are tabled the rate of
settlement of the starch granules will be very slow. Separation, there-
fore, is usually achieved by sedimenting and centrifuging or filtering
the slurry. When settling vats are used, the sieved slurry can
be diluted to the desired density with a caustic soda solution. The
heavier impurities will then settle in about an hour. The top portion
containing the dispersed starch can then be allowed to settle in tanks,
where the starch separates out in two or three days' time, leaving a
cloudy supernatant protein water. The settled starch is resuspended,
washed and resettled and the process is repeated again. The starch
which has settled for the third time is resuspended and filtered to obtain
starch with a definite moisture content ready for drying; or it may be
centrifuged directly, which though it represents a greater investment,
actually reduces space, time and sieving operations. ³ In some
factories imperforate bowl centrifuges are coming into popular use.
Some other factories make combined use of sieve, vat and centrifugal
separation and purification methods. However, the object of the
authors is to show that the tabling method can be used in separating
rice starch, rathar than to comment on the best possible method of
separating and purifying the same.
Materials and Method
The rice starch granules are the smallest of the starches of
commerce, the largest ones being about the size of the smallest ones of
corn. In shape they are definitely polygonal, and because of their small
size, features such as hilium, striations and lines are not clear even by
polarised light. The granules may aggregate together and form
compound granules.

Rice starch has not, hitherto, been manufactured in Burma, but
its manufacture is now possible even with the use of tabling methods.
The production of rice starch presents similar problems as the produc-
tion of maize starch, because unlike wheat starch, the gluten is not
present as a whole cohesive structure (Jones). If once the very
compact layer of gluten that forms the coat of each individual granule
is properly softened, it may be possible for the purification and
separation of the rice starch from the gluten to be effected by using the
very same tables at Thingangyun, once used by The Indian Starch
Products Ltd. for the manufacture of maize starch. In this starch
production factory, fibre can be effectively eliminated by using sieves of
proper sizes and by also using a rotary drier. Probably here, Sweco 4
separation may be found to be superior to the present shaking sieves.
Rice is generally classified into head rice, broken rice or screenings,
and brewer's rice; to these a "second head", composed of the larger
broken kernels is sometimes added. Since head rice is decidedly more
valuable than the other qualities, it is damaged or broken rice which
usually consitutes the starting raw material for starch prodution
(Copland). As the processes of hulling, skinning and polishing of rice
at the mills all involve breakage of the grains, broken rice accounts for
20 to 50 percent of the rough rice (Brautlecht ).

We have used our analytical figures for all calculations in the
experiments conducted to determine the outturn percentage of starch
by the tabling method.
Alkali steeping is an important process preparatory to the making
of the rice slurry for sieving and tabling. It may be recalled that it
was Jones who first described and used the alkali steeping process in
1840, and his method is still used with minor modifications. Because
it is important to liberate individual granules from the cementing gluten,
steeping the rice grains in chemical agents is essential. Caustic soda is
usually employed for this loosening effect and also to purify the starch.
In the Department of Applied Chemistry Research of the U. B. A. R. I.,
the first group who successfuly conducted the steeping experiments were
Daw Kyin Yi and Ma Than Myint. They found that with the use of
0.4% NaOH as the steeping solution, the maximum amount of water is
absorbed by the grains at room temperature within the first 10 to 14
hours.
In practice, about 208 lbs. of rice (raw material or R. M.) is
soaked in about 50 to 99 gallons of 0.3-0.5% NaOH solution for about
21 to 48 hours in a steel tank with perforated false bottom. Initially it
is stirred. The first lye solution is often drained off within the first 10
hours of steeping and second steep added. At the end of steeping in
the above reagent (R1), the steeped rice (treated material) is usually
soft enough to crush between thumb and finger. The starchy residue
is then thoroughly washed in water, between the hands.
In order that the caustic soda solution that is used may not run
down in strength, the steeping water should not be hard. It is fortunate
that Rangoon water is not as hard as Mandalay water. In fact, it is
soft and any salts of iron that might impart a brown colour to the
starch can be easily eliminated by aeration and settling. It has been
argued that some organic acids formed by enzymes and also the
protein of rice can react with caustic soda and may weaken the
solution. However, there was no such difficulty in our experiments.
This steeped and washedʻrice (treated material or T.M. I) with
about 58% of water (according to Yi) is then ground between mill
stones to a slurry (T.M.II) of about 5-11 Be. A dilute solution of
about 0.25% NaOH or just water (R2) is fed during milling. (See
the experiment of September 19 to 21 for grinding results). The slurry
(T.M.II) is then bulked with water or 0.25% NaOH (R3) to a desired
density of about 3 to 4. Be, but never above 5. Be. Lyall and Tin.
maintain that 3 Be should be strictly adhered to. This slurry of the

desired density (T.M.IIIa) is then sifted on shaking sieves fitted with a

The separation and purification of the starch, especially from
protein hydrolysates and fibre that have passed through the sieve, is
effected by tabling. The tables are about 22 inches wide and 85 feet
long. Since the tables at the factory are with a slope of depression of
about 2 to 3 inches in 100 feet, a new perfectly horizontal table was
made. Unfortunately this table was available only for the November 12
experiment.
The starch on first collection (T.M.V) may be redispersed again
and retabled for better quality, or, it may go directly to the centrifuge.
The centrifuge used is an ordinary perforate one, which is efficient in
separating the starch and gluten from the dissolved impurities, and
should not be confused with the imperforate bowl type. This means
that the starch collected from the perforate centrifuge (T.M.VI) is not
free from gluten at this stage. Hence, the value of retabling lies in
eliminating the gluten in the form of soluble hydrolysates.
The starch collected from the centrifuge is sun-dried in all the
experiments. This gives dry starch (T.M.VII).

Discussion
The Outurn of Starch will be much below 94% after the starch
has gone beyond the stage VII to the actual milling, where dirt and the
remaining fibre will be eliminated by a process called "Rotary Drying
and Sifting". However, the experiment of November 12, 1955 is
expected to be the best, for the following reasons:
1. The first successful sample was run on the level table.
2. The highest outurn of starch was obtained, viz: 94%.
3. There was a high elimination of fibre off the sieves, with
the result that the sample was exceptionally free from
fibre, as found by eye-inspection after the sample,
dispersed in water to a density of about 5. Be, was
centrifuged. It was also noted that the elimination of
fibre off the sieves was exceptional in the sense that it
did not involve too much loss of starch as experienced in
previous experiments.
4. Good settling was obtained. The fraction that was tabled
off (i.e. S.M.c) gave a density reading of only 0.5. Be as
against 3. Be at the head of the table.

This may be regarded as the ideal sample. Although an ideal
stage is often defined as one that cannot be
reality, and although it may not be realistic to carry out the ideal
process on absolutely level tables, at least it is gratifying to have, on
this occasion, approached the ideal situation to the nearest possible.
All subsequent experiments can thus be judged in comparison to this
one.
However, considerable attention has yet to be paid to the purity of
the samples of the final Starch Product (S.P.), which cannot be done at
the present moment, due to lack of some facilities such as a boiler
giving at least 100 lbs. per square inch pressure of steam to dry the
starch. "Dry Sifting" to eliminate the least traces of impurities can
only be done when the starch is dry by the time it reaches the Rotary
Drier. The work of U Htay and Daw Ma Ma Lay, of the Analysis
Department, seems to point out that the stage will soon be reached
where commercially pure grades of Starch Products can be obtained.

Summary
1. It has been maintained by many authors that in the
separation of the rice starch, rice starch slurries cannot
be tabled due to the very slow settling rate of the starch
granules.
2. Its manufacture is thought possible by the authors with
the use of the tabling method.
3. In fact, the production of rice starch offers similar
problems to the production of maize starch, and in both
cases steeping in caustic soda solution has to be done in
order to free granules of starch from gluten.
4. For the tabling of maize starch slurry, tables with a slope
are used.
5. The nearest approach to the ideal stage can be reached in
the case of rice starch separation by (a) using level tables
(b) adjusting the density of the slurry to be immediately
tabled to 3 or 4° but never above 5. Be, (c) the introduc-
tion of calcium ions, long known in soil studies as a
flocculating agent, (d) retabling the "fraction off the
tables", and finally, (e) careful inspection of the perforate
centrifuge so that nothing more than water with solutes
can escape, carrying with it as little starch as possible.
