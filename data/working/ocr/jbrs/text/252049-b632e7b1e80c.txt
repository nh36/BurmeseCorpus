PREFACE
In March 1921, Mr. G. M. Luce, then Hon. Secretary of the Burma Research Society
wrote to me suggesting that I should do for the Burma Research Society what I had done
for the Siam Society, namely to give the story of the relations between Burma and Siam
as recorded in Siamese Chronicles. Thirteen years have elapsed since that suggestion was
made and now I am giving the first installment of the translation made from P'ra Raj
P'onsawadan Chabab P'ra Raj H`at'alek'a called the "Royal Autograph Raj p' Edition, by
Mr. W. A. R. Wood, C.I.E., author of A History of Siam. This edition of the Siamese
Chronicles was revised by King Maha Mongkut (Rama IV) and first printed in three
volumes in 1913. Though some of the dates given in it have been found to be not quite
accurate, it is nevertheless an authoritative work in Slam just as the Hmannan Yazawin is
in Burma.
The first volume begins with the foundation of Ayut'ia in Cula Sakkaraj 712 (A.D.
1350), and the first invasion of Siam by the Burmese took place in Sakkaraj 892 (A.D.
1530). Without starting the translation where the narrative of the invasion begins, I have
thought it better to begin with the foundation of Ayut'ia, giving briefly the history of the
kings that reigned during the intervening 180 years, and the events in Siam immediately
preceding the Burmese invasion. Only in this way can a good perspective of the events
chronicled be obtained. Moreover, one can get an idea of how the Siamese of old wrote
their history. The style in which the Siamese history was written is very similar to that of
the Hmannan history, containing many imaginary speeches made by prominent
characters who played important parts in the events narrated. It is my intention to make
the translation as close as possible to the original so that readers may have an like idea of
'what a History of Siam written in Siamese is like.
In transliterating Siamese names, titles and places, I have followed the method
adopted by Mr. W. A. R. Wood for the reasons given by him in the introduction to his
History of Siam. I think Mr. Woods method is a very practical one for those who read
English and who do not Intend to study Siamese deeply. For the benefit of those who
may not have access to Mr. Wood's work, I reproduce below his remarks on
transliteration. The transliteration of Siamese names, titles and places presents a very
much greater difficulty than the transliteration of Burmese names, because most of the
Siamese names are drawn from Sanskrit and Pall, and while retaining all the letters of the
original, the pronunciation bears no semblance to what it should be according to the
letters composing the original. For Instance there is a town called Nak'on Sawan which
when written according to its spelling as derived from Sanskrit IS Nagara Svarga. One
can easily imagine the impossibility of writing down Nagara Svarga and expecting people
to pronounce it as Nak'on Sawan.

book, trust that it will not be found entirely unsatisfactory. There are two main systems
in use in Siam, namely the phonetic, which gives the sound of each word as heard

There are two man classes of Siamese guttural, labial and dental consonants,
namely the unaspirated and the aspirated. The latter are often represented by adding an
h", e.g. Phya, Thien; but this misleads many people into pronouncing those combinations
as in English. I have, therefore indicated the aspirated consonants by adding an
apostrophe, e.g. P'hya, T'ien.
"
"There are two Siamese classes of letters which are usually transliterated by "ch".
One is more or less soft, as in "church", the other is hard, rather like the "tch" at the end
of the word pitch." To distinguish between these two classes of letters 1 have
represented the soft sound by "j" and the hard sound by "ch." An exception has been
made in the names of such well known places as Chiengmai and Chiengsen, and perhaps
a few more slight inconsistencies may be found.
"In regard to this matter, I crave the indulgence of my readers. No two persons can
be found to agree as to the best method of transliterating Siamese names, and no system
is entirely satisfactory. All I can hope for is to cause as little confusion as possible to
readers who do not know Siamese or Sanscrit." .
The spelling of the names of certain towns and titles has been so fixed in Siam and in
books on Siam written in English that it would lead to misunderstanding in some cases if
Mr. Wood's method were strictly followed. He himself has made an exception in the case
of the names of such towns as Chiengmai and Chiengsen. But there are other towns such
as Chainat, Pichai, Pichit, Chumpon, Petchburi etc. where the spelling with "ch" has
been fixed and is met with so often that it may lead to misunderstanding by using " "j"
Instead of," ch ". Then the words Phya and Phra, denoting ranks in Siamese titles, are so
common, appearing in all English newspapers published in Siam, and in correspondence
both official and private, that I have thought it better to retain Phya instead of P'ya and
Phra instead of P'ra.
The scientific or classical spelling of the names of kings, persons titles and places is
given in the foot-note on the page where they occur first, but not in other places where
they may occur again.
His Royal Highness Prince Damrong's work, Our Wars with the Burmese, in two
volumes, has been of the greatest help to me in understanding certain parts of the "Royal
Autograph Edition" of the History of Siam. I may mention here that these two books
were given me by His Royal Highness as a present and I take this opportunity of
tendering him grateful thanks for the books, and for the help they have given me in my
translation.

Samrid (Samriddhi) does not mean tenth, but it means complete, finished, end.
The two cycles run concurrently and so at the end of every sixty years, each year in
the twelve years' cycle returns to the same position in the ten years' cycle. The period of
sixty years la called the Great Cycle.
In Siam if one asks a man in what year he was born, he generally answers not the
Sakkaraj, but the name of the animal corresponding to that Sakkaraj, e.g., (I was born in
the year of the Rat or the year of the Horse" as the case may be.

FIRST ACCESSION TO THE THRONE BY PHRA RAMESUAN

REIGIN OF PHRA BOROM RAJAT'IRAT I.
In the year 732 (A.D. 1370) Phra Borom Rajat'irat came over to Ayut'ia from
Sup'anburi. When the king was informed of the arrival of his uncle, the ruler of
Sup'anburi, he went out and invited his uncle to enter the city and ascend the throne; then
taking leave of the new king he went back to his old town of Lopburi.
Almost every year of this King's reign was spent In expeditions against the rulers of
neighbouring towns and bringing them under his sway. The Governors of P’itsanulok and
Kamp'engp'et acknowledged his suzerainty. He died in the year 744 (A.D. 1383).
REIGN OF PHRA CHAO TONG CHAN
Chao T'ong Chan, the deceased king's son, who was only 15 years of age, ascended
the throne but his reign lasted only seven days.
SECOND ACCESSION TO THE THRONE OF PHRA RAMESUAN
Phra Ramesuan came down from Lopburi. He succeeded in entering the palace and
capturing his young cousin whom he ordered to be executed. He then ascended the

used to cover the image weighed 286 chang. The consecration of the image was
performed on Friday the 11th waxing moon of the 8th Siamese month (July) in the year
845 (A.D. 1483).

prevailed in Siam, according to which "not only was all land in the kingdom the actual
property of the crown, but every human being living within the frontiers of the state was
considered as a mere chattel of the king, who had absolute right to dispose of the person,
property or life of all such in any manner that might seem best to him." This right the
king exercised " by demanding personal service from each man for a certain part of each
year. "2 This service might be in any department of the state such as army, navy, public
works, etc., or even menial work in the royal household. This service was hereditary, that
is if the father served in any branch of service, the son on coming of age must serve in the
same branch as that in which his father served, and so with the other departments. A
complete register was maintained of the persons belonging to different department, and
different marks were tattooed on the wrist of the men, indicating to which department
they belonged. Every year, as occasion arose, this register was revised, and those coming
of age and becoming able-bodied were called up, entered I the register and the mark
indicative of the department to which each belonged was tattooed on the wrist. It was in
effect a census roll of all those liable to render service to the king. Therefore, the officer
in charge of this Registration Office knew exactly how many men there were in the
several department, and if he could persuade them to espouse his cause and to serve him
in case of need, he could gauge what strength he would have behind him in any
hazardous adventure he might under take. Moreover, as he probably had the power or the
opportunity of transferring men from one department to another, he could get a large
number of men royal to him.

Phya T'arama led Phya Chakri into the presence of King Phra Mahint'rat'irat, who,
believing that Phya Chakri had really escaped from confinement, was very glad. The
chains were removed, and rewards were showered on him, and he was given the chief
command in the defence of the city with absolute power of life and death. For about a
month he repaired forts, gates, moats, battlements, and gun platforms, and took other
measures as if he really wanted to defend the city to the best of his ability. But when the
month was over there came a complete change, whenever he saw a man brave, clever,
energetic and attentive in the discharge of his duties, he found fault with him as being
negligent, inattentive and careless, and punished him. He posted civil servants who had
no military training or experience in the place of military officers, and put military
officers in charge of civil appointments. The efficiency of the defence fell off in
consequence. He then sent a secret letter to the King of Hanthawaddy, fixing Friday the
10th waning moon of the 9th Siamese month (August) for a combined attack on the city.
On the appointed day his Majesty of Burma arranged four columns: one column was
dressed in black coats and armed with sword and shield; a second dressed in green coats
and armed with two-handed swords; a third dressed in red coats and armed with muskets
fired with a fuse; and a fourth dressed in blue coats and armed with lances and swords in
scabbards slung over the shoulder. Phra Maha Tammaraja and Maha Uparaja were
ordered to attack form the causeway in front of Kaw Keo island; the King of Ava and the
King of Prome each along the other two causeways. The defenders sent volleys of musket
fire, hurled javelins and sharpened wooden staves; killing a great many of the attackers,

brought about by the treachery of one who was born and bred a Siamese, of noble
descent, and whom the Siamese King delighted to honour by raising him to noble rank,
and by appointing him to high office. In extenuation of his treachery one should take into
consideration the circumstances in which he was placed. In the first place his life was
completely at the disposal of the King of Burma, and refusal to accept the treacherous
part assigned to him would have meant instant death or miserable confinement, with
perhaps torture and unusual suffering added for daring to defy the wish of His Burman
Majesty. It was a case of a choice between being unpatriotic and treacherous and
continuing to keep the flame of life burning, or being patriotic by defying the vengeance
of a ruthless King, and sacrificing his life for the welfare of his king and country. Love of
life, of mere existence, is exceedingly strong in human nature. Moreover, it was quite
possible that Phra Maha T'ammaraja may have used all manner of persuasion, with
abundant promises of a happy future, to overcome any patriotic feelings in the poor
nobleman placed in such trying circumstances; because to Phra Maha Tammaraja the fall
of Ayut'ia meant his accession to its throne, and he was striving for it, just as much as the
Burmese King, for the attainment of his highest ambition. To Phya Chakri, to be patriotic
and to refuse to be a party in bringing about the fall of Ayut'ia, meant the sacrifice of a
thing held most dear by ordinary humanity. The first fall of Ayut'ia was not a capture by
force, but a surrender by its King.

The King of Hanthawaddy left behind Phra Sunt'on Songkram to be the new Kings
elder minister and adviser. This nobleman had been taken away to Hanthawaddy together
with Phra Ramesuan and four white elephants, when Ayut'ia capitulated after investment,

the King of Hanthawaddy. He commissioned the Governor of Muang Nak'on Prom,
with a force of three thousand Burmese soldiers, to garrison Ayut'ia and assist the new
King in guarding the city and maintaining peace and order. He then told Phra
Mahint'rat'irat, the deposed King, to make preparations for departure, and ordered the
new King to give the royal regalia and all the necessary royal utensils, decorations, orders
etc, to the deposed King, and to allow him to take his queens, concubines, and personal
attendants.
When everything was ready His Majesty of Burma left Ayut'ia, going by way of
Kamp'engp'et. When they reached Muang K'reng (Gyaing in Amherst District). Phra
Mahint'rat'irat became very ill, and Lakwai T'ammu who was in attendance on him,
reported the illness to His Majesty of Burma. Eleven Burmese, Mon and Siamese doctors
were ordered to attend to the sick King, and they were made to understand that if they
failed to effect a cure and death ensued, they were made to understand that if they failed
to effect a cure and death ensued, they themselves would be punished with death. The
next morning the Burmese King visited the sick man, told him not to lose heart and
encouraged him to take the medicines prescribed and the nourishment offered. He
encamped and waited for the recovery of the sick man but unfortunately Phra
Mahint'rat'irat died after an illness of eleven days.² The eleven doctors were punished,
but the record does not state definitely that they were executed; probably they were,
because the Burmese King seldom failed to do what he had said he would do. The
remains of the dead king were cremated, and the ashes and the whole of the deceased's
retinue and belongings were sent back to Ayut'ia, under the charge of a Burmese, Mon
and Lao escort. His Burman Majesty then resumed his journey home.
