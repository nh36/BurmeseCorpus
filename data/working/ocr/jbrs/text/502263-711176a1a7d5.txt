0. "Probably the most profoundly
different from any American point of view
[in linguistics] is the so-called London
School, the followers of Professor J.R.
Firth. Not only is their view of language
basically different; their terminology con-

other linguists. As a result Americans
find difficulty in reading their papers,

The justification for this criticism is
perhaps in large part due to the fact that
the department of which Firth was head
is at the School of Oriental and African
Studies; the linguistic interests of his
followers have, in consequence, been mainly
concentrated on the languages of Africa
and the Orient rather than on languages
that have been prominent enough to attract
analyses in accordance with other theories.

continues this Oriental and African
tradition: it is an analysis of certain
phonetic features of an Oriental language;
but, since both an American phonemic
analysis and an American phonemic analysis
and grammar of Burmese are available for

The essentials of this theory have
been stated as follows. "The aim of
prosodic analysis in phonology is not
that of transcription or unilinear repre-
sentation of languages, but rather
phonological analysis in terms which take
account not only of paradigmatic relations
and contrasts, but also of the equally
important syntagmatic relations and func-
tions which are operative in speech.
Prosodic analysis is, in fact, an abbreviated
designation of an analysis that makes use
of two types of element, Prosodies and
Phonematic Units; ... Phonematic units
refer to those features or aspects of the
phonic material which are best regarded
as referable to minimal segments having
serial order in relation to each other in
structures. In the most general terms such
units constitute the consonant and vowel
elements or C and V units of a phonological
structure a great part, sometimes the
greater part, of the phonic material is
referable to prosodies, which are, by
definition, of more than one segment in
scope or domain of relevance, and may
in fact belong to structures of any length,...
We may thus speak of syllable prosodies,
prosodies of syllable groups, phrase or
sentence-part prosodies, and sentence pro-
sodies; and since grammatically defined
elements may also be characterized by
prosodic features...we may have in addition
word and morpheme prosodies."4

The reason why the basic-form con-
cept is unacceptable to those who favour
prosodic analysis is that they are impressed
by the fact that each of the different
phonetic forms of a given lexical item (or,
for that matter, the different phonemic
forms into which these phonetic forms are
analysed phonemically) is not only appro-
priate to its own peculiar environment, but
is, further, apart from free variation,
uniquely appropriate to it. Each form can-
not, in fact, be replaced in its proper
environment by any other form, phonetic
or phonemic. This irreplaceability of each
form in its environment gives to each, in this
view, equal status with any other form of
that lexical item, as though, in fact, each
were the norm in its own environment,
to the exclusion of any other of the variant
forms. Each of the variant forms ought
therefore to enjoy the parity of esteem
earned for it by its unique status in the
environment appropriate to it, and should
not be subordinated to any other one of
the variants.
Firth's position with regard to the
status of variant forms is re-inforced by
a weakness in the basic-form approach:
the need, on occasions, to have recourse
to an arbitrary decision; when none of
the variant forms offers any advantage
for derivation over the other variants,
