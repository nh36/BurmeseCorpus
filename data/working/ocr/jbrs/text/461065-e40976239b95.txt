ONE CENTENARY RECALLS ANOTHER
On the "Storia del Cristianesimo nell'Impero Barmano)
Preceduta dalle notizie del Paese" by Luigi Gallo,
Oblate of Turin, in the centenary of his death-
16 April 1863–1963.
by
VIVIAN BA*
Paris, April 1963.
All historians of the History of Burma, have had recourse to the diaries and travel
accounts of European travellers, adventurers, ambassadors and emissaries. But the great
stock of precious, first hand information, left by the early Catholic missionaries in Burma
have so far been neglected. The archives of the Barnabite Fathers, the Oblates of Turin,
Foreign Missions of Paris, etc., are a treasure-throve of Burmology, and the Secret Archives
of the Vatican, Archives of the Congregation of the Faith in Rome, Library of the Propa-
gation of the Faith in Rome, Library of the Propagation of the Faith and the Lateran also
contain precious manuscripts, documents, parabeiks and the first texts on Buddhism to be
transported to Europe and translated for European intellectuals. In my researches, I am
indebted to the kind services of His Eminence Cardinal Marella, the encouragement of
His Eminence the Cardinal Prefect of the Propagation of the Faith and the help of the
Archivists of the Barnabites, Oblates and the French Foreign Missions.
This year we celebrate the centenary of the death of the Oblate historian, Luigi Gallo
who died in 1863. He was a missionary in Burma for several years and on his return to
Italy, went to the first hand sources and reports of the first missionaries to the Burmese
Empire, and wrote three volumes on the history of missionary activity in the extensive
Burma of the Kings, preceeded by a general history of Burma.
Gallo's "Storia del Cristianesimo" - its publication centenary was last year (1862-
1962) -- is a mine of information on Burma's history, social life, religion etc. He reproduces
several letters of the early missionaries to their superiors in Italy, which throw much light
on the history of the day. The letters are happily preserved in their original form, in the
several archives quoted above.

The "Storia" was the first printed account in extenso of the History of the Catholic
Church in Burma, and the several publications on the same subject are not as complete as
this, in detail and facts. Gallo travelled far and wide to gather the facts for his book,
and died before he concluded the third volume. Due to the insistence of the Barnabites,
he submitted his manuscripts for publication while in hospital at Nizza and the first two
volumes were printed in July 1862.
The Sources of Gallo's History
In his Forward, he tells us, that his sources were:- 1) The life of Monsignor Percoto,
who was one of the greatest European scholars of Pali and Burmese and who compiled,
with Carpani's co-operation, the first printed Burmese Grammar in 1776.2 2) Collections
of letters and other documents from the archives of the various Barnabite Colleges in
Rome, Milan, Monza and Turin. (These documents are still preserved.) 3) Collections
of letters of his own Conferences of the Oblates of Turin. (These too still exist in the
archives of the Oblates). 4) Two thick memoirs of the Barnabite, Marcello Cortenovis,
who died in Rangoon, in 1802. 5) Printed works in Latin, Italian, French and English. 6)
"Finally" says Gallo, "the explorations which I myself, as a missionary have made on
the spot".
He ends by saying that he has made his love for the truth govern the whole work
and was guided by charity and prudence in the choice of the facts and that he believed
that what he had accomplished, if not perfect, was at least conscientious." Anybody
reading these volumes cannot but be satisfied with the efforts of Gallo and praise him for
his choice of facts and lively descriptions.
Sources for his short hisiory of Burma
He gave a seventy page description of Burma before beginning the History of the
Catholic Church in Burma, in order to render the reading more understandable to his
Italian audience. Burma, then, as much as now, was indeed a far away land for many in
Italy. The description was compiled from his own on-the-spot observations, the writings
of learned missionaries and witnesses, and the books and articles of several British officials
and travellers and especially the work of Sangermano. (Gallo went through the manu-
scripts and papers of Sangermano in the Barnabite archives, which the learned Barnabite

10. For the bibliography of Gaetano Mantegazza see my article in JBRS, XLV, ii, Dec. 1962, page 212,
note (4). Bishop Mantegazza who was also the author of the second edition of the Alphabetum
Barmanorum, wrote a History of the Kingdoms of Ava and Pegu, laying particular stress on the
language, culture, religion and customs of the Barmas. He wrote it in 1784 on his way to Italy with
two Burmans. The original manuscript is in French and accompanied by two maps. Mantegazza
claims that they were the first of the kind to be made of Burma. He is certainly right if he means
the first detailed maps as in his larger map of Burma the names of towns, villages are all given and he
even goes so far as to mark walled towns, those with a market, those ruled by a prince, those with
walls around, etc. The map of Burma illustrating Syme's book was compiled by the editor and not
drawn by Symes. Mantegazza's history preceeded Symes's by a decade. It numbers three hundred
closely written pages, with interesting marginal notes, It was discovered among the papers of
another great Barnabite historian, Sangermano, 120 years after it was written, by the Bibliographer
the Barnabite Boffito, who included it in the Bibliotheca Barnabitica in 1933. This history has been
overlooked so far by all English historians of Burma. I have outlined it in the Guardian Monthly,
Rangoon, January 1963. The most important chapters of Mantegazza have been translated by me
into English, and submitted for publication in Burma, last year. In Italy, the manuscript had
already been studied first by Father Michele Cristallo who because of blindness could not continue
his researches. The enterprise of Cristallo was continued by the Barnabite, De Ruggiero, who
presented it in 1938 as his Thesis, for the Laurea, in the University of Rome: "Gaetano Mantegazza
barnabita......e la sua Opera. (249 type written pages) Then came Professor Carmignani who had
the History of Mantegazza printed in the original French and added comments in Italian with a long
bibliography and an interesting Introduction. Carmingnani did not know of the existence of de
Ruggiero's thesis.:" Renzo Carmignani, LA BIRMANIA, Relazione inedita del 1784 del Missionario
Barnabita G.M. Mantegazza. Con introduzione, Note, Illustrazioni, Carte geografiche, Bibliografia-
Ed. Anno Santo, Roma (Via Romagna 14), 1950, pp. 208. The editorial offices no longer exist in
Rome. There is a copy in the British Museum.

Gallo's style
In writing his "Storia" the Oblate historian does not spare any pains to go to the
very sources of his theme. He travelled to all parts of Italy to check his facts. But his
history is not merely a string of dry dates and happenings. His narration is interesting,
simple and alluring. It is nevertheless real factual history. He gives his sources, adds
footnotes and explanations wherever necessary and gives the Bulls and Papal Briefs
regarding Burma to help the more scholarly readers. In Volume One, page 27 he repro-
duces an interesting letter of his to a friend in Turin, dated Moulmein May 1846, in which
he minutely described a huge flying fox or Vampire Vespertilio Spectrum. It shows that
he was not a story teller but a keen observer of nature and was highly capable of writing his
chapters on the Flora and Fauna of Burma. Gallo did not lack the poetic sense in his
narrations. In describing the perrenial freshness and verdure of the Burmese flora he
quotes this couplet on everlasting spring:-
Co' fiori eterni eterno il frutto dura,
E mentre spunta l'un l'altro matura.
He writes and describes his experiences and narrates what he has done and seen
very vividly. In Tavoy, he tasted the Durian and says "it is a real delicacy for the Indians,
but for the writer who has only once tasted it, it is enough.”

He intermingles the History of the Mission with the dynastic changes in the King-
doms of Pegu and Ava and narrates the wars, victories and reverses of the two Kingdoms.
He gives the names of the Kings and their relations with the missionaries and recalls the
good and the bad in their characters.
To show that missionary activity was not only evangelisation, but a total dedication
to the people among whom the missionaries laboured, he showed their efforts to imbibe
the language and culture of their flock and their adaptation to their environment. Every
time he has a chance, he tells us whether the missionary was learned in Pali and Burmese
or Mon and the different written works of the missionary. Paradoxically, the Pali and
Burmese manuscripts sent to Rome by the Barnabites, made Burmese Hinayana Buddhism
known to the West. In the printing of Burmese alphabets and the translation of Buddhist
manuscripts the Barnabites of Burma were encouraged by Cardinal Borgia. Gallo devotes
several pages to the literary efforts of the first Barnabites.
Speaking of the English designs on Burma, the Oblate begins his chapter on the
Embassy of Symes with the simplicity and beauty which characterises his whole book:
"The Burmans", he says, "had just put down their arms after routing the Siamese troops,
when they saw that a European nation, still quite unknown. and less feared (ancor poco
conosciuta e meno ancora temuta,) was ready to jump on them; this nation was already
mistress of many parts of India, and it was showing signs of wishing to set foo: in Burma.
It was the English Nation.”
Gallo's history which has completed its 100th birthday last year will still afford the
research worker with interesting and factual reading today as yesterday.
Gallo, Sangermano and Mantegazza
Gal'o tells us that he consulted the sources of Sangermano before writing his history.
He does not mention Mantegazza's History of 1784.* Neither does Sangermano mention
the work of Mantegazza. But comparing the original Italian edition of Sangermano with
the history of Mantegazza, certain similarities can be detected. It shows that Sangermano
had consulted the papers of Mantegazza, all the more so, because Boffito is said to have
discovered the long lost MS of Mantegazza among the papers of Sangermano.
The parallelisms between Sangermano (Italian edition) and Mantegazza have been
pointed out by Carmignani in his work which I have cited.**
