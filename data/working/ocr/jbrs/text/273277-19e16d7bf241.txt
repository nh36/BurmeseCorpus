Early Newspapers in Burma.*

The War of 1852 added to British Burma the very rich Province of
Pegu, which had been famed for its wealth by travellers for a couple of
centuries or more. The wealth was there all right, but had to be deve-
loped. So it came to pass that, as soon as the War had ended, there
flocked into Burma a troop of optimists who expected to get rich in a
hurry. Some of them did, and the prosperous firms they established
are still with us to the present day. Amongst the seekers of fame and
fortune was a Mr. H. W. Lewis. He was a Pleader, and had been dab-
bling in law and journalism in Moulmein before the War of 1852 opened
a new avenue for him in Rangoon. On January 5th 1853, he supplied
Rangoon with what junior newspaper reporters are fond of calling a
long felt want. " That is to say, he provided Rangoon with its first
newspaper, which he desired to be known to posterity as the Rangoon
Chronicle. It consisted of four medium sized pages published on
Wednesdays and Saturdays. It was sometimes indifferently printed and
sometimes not printed at all, owing to disputes between Mr. Lewis and
his staff. The policy of the new-comer, so far as one may judge it at
this distance of time, was to redress the wrongs that needed resistance
and to uphold the rights that needed assistance, Mr. Lewis, apparently,
being the sole judge of what was wrong and what was right. The
result was that he made a host of enemies, and yet, in the end, did fairly
well for himself.
Before the advent of the Rangoon Chronicle the people of
Rangoon had to depend for their news, and the merchants for their
advertisements, on the newspapers published in Calcutta, the chief of
which were the Englishman, which is now merged in the Calcutta States-
man, and the Harkaru, which subsquently became the Indian Daily
News, but is now defunct. The publication of a newspaper in Rangoon
in Burmese times was quite out of the question. The king would tolerate
no comment whatever. This fact Mr. Crisp discovered to his cost. His
firm had existed in Rangoon long before the British occupation of Pegu
and did considerable business as ship-builders and timber merchants.
While on a visit to Calcutta Mr. Crisp incautiously related to one of the
newspapers there how things were conducted in Rangoon. The result
came quickly and drastically. Mr. Crisp's son, who was managing his
father's business in Rangoon, was arrested and cast into the prison, and
Mr. Crisp had to hurry back to Burma to obtain his release. But that is

not the end of the story. The whole Crisp family were banished from
Burmese territory and had to start life again in Moulmein, which became
British territory after the War of 1824.
As there were very few persons in Rangoon who could read
English at the time when Mr. Lewis closed down the Maulmain Chronicle
and re-opened it in Rangoon as the Rangoon Chronicle, he could not
have reasonably expected to make a fortune out of his subscribers, who
were expected to pay a monthly rate of Rs. 3, nor out of the few
advertisements he managed to secure.
But he had his law business and
he started his firm of Lewis & Co., auctioneers and dealers in sundry
goods, so as to add grist to the mill. He had some reason to believe
that Rangoon would develop rapidly under British rule, and that he
would share in its prosperity.
Now let us glance for a moment at the Rangoon of 1853, as the
Editor of the Rangoon Chronicle, saw it. Here is a characteristic para-
graph from his newspaper. "We beg to draw the serious attention of
the Police Magistrate to various parts of the town where decaying
wrecks of mortality, dead dogs in an advanced stage of decomposition,
putrid carcases of cats and rats, rank vegetation, etc., so surround the
place as to be killing". And here is a picture of the Government of
Burma as painted by Mr. Lewis. "We are governed by the most des-
potic, the most illiberal, the most unwise, the most improvident, the
most heartless and unjust Government which could have been devised
by the united efforts of half a dozen Dalhousies, supposing (which
heaven forbid) that so many of the stamp could be found". Very
frequently the Rangoon Chronicle, had nothing but abuse for both
Government officials and the public, but amongst its pet aversions was
Lord Dalhousie, then Viceroy of India, whom it delighted to call "an
incompetent snob."
Presumably, on the ground that one good turn deserves another,
the Rangoon correspondent of the Englishman decided to enlighten the
people of Bengal as to what Rangoonites thought of the Kangoon
Chronicle. So, in a letter to that newspaper, dated December 2nd 1853,
he wrote, "There is another want in this country, and a very pressing
one. I mean a well conducted newspaper with an honest Editor, who is
a gentleman who will chronicle events as they really happen without
distortion either for the sake of exerting a false interest, or of serving
the purpose of self-interested people. At present we have nothing of
the kind."
Nothing daunted by this scathing attack, Mr. Lewis carried on in
the old way till Government decided to get rid of him. But how? He
might have been deported as an undesirable resident in the newly
acquired British possession, but he was a lawyer and therefore likely to

cause as much trouble as possible had action been taken against him
under the law of 1818. Then there was the awkward questions of the
liberty of the Press. But Government surmounted all difficulties in a
really brilliant manner. They got rid of the truculent Mr. Lewis by
making a Government servant of him. And to keep him out of harm's
way, they sent him away from Rangoon to Twante to act as Police
Court Prosecutor, according to one account, or Assistant Commissioner,
according to another account. So in the Rangoon Chronicle of July 1st
1854, we read:-" The management of this paper has changed hands."
The next proprietor of the Rangoon Chronicle was a Mr. Marshall,
who appears to have been a soldier, a Purser on board ship, and many
other things. He came to Rangoon from Moulmein and admits that he
was enrolled as a Pleader," without examination." He conducted the
Rangoon Chronicle very much as Mr. Lewis had done, but failed to gain
a Government appointment for his trouble. His only reward, he says,
was to make more enemies during his brief ownership of the Chronicle
than he had ever managed to do either previously or subsequently to
that event. After about six months of stormy journalism he sold the
paper to Mr. Godfree, merchant and property owner. Mr. Marshall
returned to Moulmein, where his health failed, so he said good-bye to
Burma and sailed for England in the barque Avondhu, Captain J. Camp-
bell.
Mr. Godfree soon tired of running the Chronicle and sold his
interest to Mr. George J. Curran, an Irish Barrister. He decided to
conduct the Chronicle on first rate lines, and so imported a young
journalist from Calcutta to edit the paper. But Mr. Curran had an up-
hill task before him. First of all, the Chronicle had earned for itself a
bad name; it was not fashionable to read or advertise in it. The people
of Rangoon preferred to depend upon Calcutta for their supply of news,
which the Englishmau and Harkaru provided in acceptable form and
which reached Rangoon about once a week by mail steamers whose top
speed was between seven and eight knots an hour. The change of name
from the Rangoon Chronicle to the Pegu Gazette did not blot out the
discordant memories associated with the Rangoon Chronicle, and Mr.
Curran's Irish and wit cutting sarcasm did not appeal to the people of
Rangoon. So, bereft of supporters, Rangoon's first newspaper faded
away and expired in May 1858, after a very rough journalistic voyage
extending over five years. Its demise is noted briefly in one of the issues
of the Harkaru of June 1858, in these words: "The stock-in-trade of
the late proprietor was knocked down to the Editor, to whom it had
been mortgaged for arrears of salary."
On June 2nd 1858, Rangoon's first newspaper was revived as a bi-
weekly under the name of the Rangoon Times, which is still with us.

In Twentieth Century Impressions of Burma the name of the new
proprietor is given as Mr. Newton, a lawyer. I have not been able to
discover anything useful about him, but the next proprietor, Dr. Dawson
was a very well known character, whom the Rangoon Chronicle tried
hard to ruin. Captains of ships visiting Rangoon paid a fee towards the
support of the Seamen's Hospital, of which Dr. Dawson, who was a
Baptist Missionary Doctor, was Honorary Superintendent. He appears
to have been a worthy man ; but, owing to the attacks of the Chronicle
about the publication of accounts, he handed over charge of the hospital
to the Civil Surgeon, who had no time to attend to it and the Hospital,
with the charitable dispensary attached to it, closed down. The result
was that residents, who could not afford to pay high fees for medical
attendance and nursing in their homes, had no alternative but to share
the jail hospital with the convicts, the only difference being that out-
siders had the privilege of paying up to Rs. 5 daily for food and atten-
dance.
But to return to the Rangoon Timcs. Its prosperity increased
under various owners, but in 1861 its high sense of independence brought
about an important change in the journalistic affairs of Rangoon. The
merchants in meeting declared that the views sometimes expressed by
the Rangoon Times were distasteful to them, and that the Editor
declined at times to permit them to state their side of the case.
The
result was the birth of the Rangoon Gazette in 1861 under the editor-
ship of Mr. Caddy, who died four years later and was replaced by Mr.
Chanter, who sailed for England, reached Paris, and mysteriously dis-
appeared, never to be heard of again. The Rangoon Gazette com-
menced its career as a bi-weekly but, under the editorship of Mr. J. A.
Hannay, blossomed into a daily in 1883, as did the Rangoon Times
which was then owned by Mr. S. J. Seth.
The only other early English paper in Burma made its appearance
in Akyab in 1853. That is to say, in the same year in which Mr. Lewis
launched the Chronicle in Rangoon. The name given to the Akyab
journal was the Akyab Commercial News. A few years later Mr.
H. Hay purchased the paper and changed its name to the Arakan News.
In those far away days Akyab was a fairly busy port, and many sailing
ships called there for rice cargoes. Mr. Hay's newspaper did well for a
time, but in about the year 1868 it was sold by auction to Mr. Hla Paw
The, who conducted it for about a quarter of a century, and then handed
it over to his son, Maung Kyin Khine.

English newspaper in this part of the world. That honour belongs to
Moulmein, which commenced the publication of the Moulmein Adver-
tiser in 1846. The prime movers in this enterprise were the civil and
military officers stationed in what then was the capital of British Burma,
and who used their influence to induce the East India Company to subsi-
dise this journalistic enterprise. The Editor himself was formerly in the
service of the East India Company. He was Mr. T. Whittam, who after
retiring from the Dawson Launch Co. (which ran launches on the Moul-
mein river, and also a light railway from Duyinzeik to Thaton) started
a stationery business in Maingay Street and purchased a hand-press
from which he produced the Moulmein Advertiser. During his
very long connection with journalism in Moulmein Mr. Whittam saw
many changes, not only in the name of his own paper, but in those of
his many competitors. For example, in 1850, there were no fewer than
three newspapers in existence in Moulmein, namely The Moulmein
Chronicle owned and edited by Mr. D. F. Lonsdale, The Moulmein
Times, owned by the firm of W. Thomas & Co., and The Friend of
Burma owned and edited by Mr. C. F. Cecil. Other competitors arose
in later years, but all of them either perished, or were absorbed by the
Moulmein Advertiser which still holds the field, though in the long
ago it was itself absorbed for a time by The Moulmein Times.
For these particulars concerning early journalism in Moulmein I am
indebted to Mr. F. B. Peters, the present Editor of the Moulmein Ad-
vertiser and to Mr. P. T. Christensen, an old resident of Moulmein and
still Marine Surveyor in that pretty riverside town, which lost much of
its status and not a little of its trade when Rangoon came to the front
after the war of 1852.

In 1850, there were three newspapers being published in Moulmein,
viz. The Moulmein Chronicle, owned and edited by Mr. D. F. Lonsdale,
The Moulmein Times, owned by W. Thomas and Company, and
The Friend of Burmah owned and edited by Mr. C. F. Cecil. The
Moulmein Times had absorbed The Moulmein Advertiser, so that in
that year there was no newspaper by the name of The Moulmein
Advertiser. The following year however, i.e., 1851, The Moulmein
Advertiser again came into being after it had in turn absorbed The
M ulmein Times, with Mr. T. Whittam again as Printer and Publisher.
In 1852, The Moulmein Advertiser again became The Moulmein Times
with the same Mr. T. Whittam, as Proprietor and Publisher.
Mr. Whittam was one of the oldest residents of Moulmein, with a
literary bent, having come over with the East India Company in some
capacity or other, of which we are not certain. His descendants were
to be found in Moulmein up to a few years ago, his grandson being a
retired Superintendent of Customs, now settled in Rangoon. The paper
in those days published mostly military news with a soupcon of com-
mercial items. A few years later Mr. J. Bayly took over the paper when
it was again called the Moulmein Advertiser. The editor then was Mr.
J. H. Castor, Mr. Bayly being a commercial man and owner of the busi-
ness known as the London Stores, Strand Road, Moulmein. Mr. Bayly
died just a few years ago and his son is now in the service of Messrs.
Steel Brothers and Co. Ltd., Moulmein. Later, Mr. Hughes, Bar-at-Law,
and Mr. (later Sir) Mirza Hoosein, father of the late Mayor of Rangoon,
Mr. M. M. Rafi, took over the paper with Mr. J. H. Castor still as
Editor. After that the late Mr. Charles W. Law, leading lawyer of
Moulmein, and the Doyen of the Burma Bar, took charge of the news-
paper, which he edited in conjuction with Mr. Castor. From 1908 to
1934, Mr. R. Benjamin, Stevedore, and his son Mr. W. Benjamin, ran the
paper, during which period the editors were Mr. J. H. Castor, Mr.
Providence, Mr. McArthur, Mr. A. T. Johns and Mr. F. B. Peters.
The paper changed hands in 1934, when Mr. Leong Ah Choy, Mer-
chant of Moulmein, bought it up and appointed Mr. Maung Maung
Thane, Editor. Mr. Maung Maung Thane died last October and Mr.
F. B. Peters was appointed Editor, with Mr. F. T. Mellican as Assis-
tant Editor, and this is the present position.
