An Introduction to Colloquial Burmese, by J. A. Stewart (British
Burma Press, Rs. 3.)
Under the modest title of An Introduction to Colloquial Burmese,
Mr. J. A. Stewart has produced a work which brings up to date the
intelligent teaching of colloquial Burmese initiated by Mr. G. E. R. Grant
Brown by his Half the Battle in Burmese published 27 years ago.
From now onwards any further publication on this subject must take the
Introduction as its starting point.
I have described the Introduction as a modest title. It is true
that it begins with introductory matters. The first eleven chapters are
in the main elementary. As the book goes on, however, the matter
becomes more elaborate, and by the time Chapter XXI has been
reached any one really familiar with its contents would have attained a
proficiency in colloquial idiom that under ordinary circumstances it
might have taken him years to reach.
The fact is that the book is not merely, as its name would suggest,
an introduction for beginners, but a standard book of reference on the
idiomatic construction of colloquial from which no Englishman, however
well versed in the language, could fail to improve his knowledge of
spoken Burmese. Only Mr. Stewart could have produced the book, and
to have dealt with the matter less fully would have been to have depriv-
ed us of scholarly knowledge that could be obtained nowhere else.
Parts of the first ten Chapters are marred by bad printing which
reaches its climax in Chapter X where the spacing and set up is so
bad as to render the table almost impossible to follow. This however
is a reflexion on the press and not the author and will doubtless be

generally that adopted by the Burmese Phonetic Reader. It is, how-
ever, somewhat simpler and there are certain differences in the symbols
employed. Mr. Stewart has abandoned the Reader's distinction between
the vowel sounds in eager and pin, palm and an, too and put, and uses
a simple i, a, and u for both. As there can never be any confusion and
the Burmese spelling gives a complete clue to the pronunciation, this is
an advantage.

doning the exactness of the Reader it has produced a script so

anyone not brought up on phonetic scripts. The introduction to Chapter

Finally there is the combination of k with y. This is a vexed
question; Ky (05 + 900) has been represented by Ky (standard
transliteration) ty (Grant Brown) to (Phonetic Reader) and c (Stewart).
This is not the place to argue the much discussed pronunciation, but
the use of c, which is never pronounced in this way in English, seems
to me the worst of the four alternatives. My own preference is ky
which follows the Burmese.

The trouble about phoneticians is that they are never satisfied
and are always amending, and as a result all but a few scholars are
put off by their results. For a book for beginners the advantages seem
to me to lie wholly with simplicity. Let anyone compare a page of
Grant Brown with a page of the Reader or Introduction and see which
is the easier to follow.
On the question of the grammar of the language Mr. Stewart
generally follows Grant Brown and only admits two classes of full words,
nouns (including pronouns) and verbs, and form words that are either
mere particles or broken down verbs. These form-words are described
either as particles or affixes. Mr. Stewart's explanations and examples
of the uses of colloquial affixes and particles to form the ordinary
English parts of speech are many degrees advanced beyond anything
