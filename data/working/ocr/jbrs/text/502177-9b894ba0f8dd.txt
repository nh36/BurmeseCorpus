1. THESIS
A RE-EXAMINATION OF BURMESE ‘CLASSIFIERS’*
DR HLA PE**
The thesis of this paper is that the
words in Burmese sometimes called
'classifier' - amongst other names have
not been accurately assessed in previous
studies. They are more consistently and
conveniently divided into three categories,
which I shall call 'classifiers', 'quantifiers'
and repeaters'."
II. STATEMENT
The 'classifiers were first brought to
light by Lieutenant Thomas Latter of the
Bengal Army in his book: A Grammar
of the Language of Burmah. He dedicated
his work to the then Governor General
of India describing it as an endeavor to
promote the acquirement of the vernacular
dialect of one of the numerous provinces
under his sway, and thereby to benefit a
large portion of British subjects... In it
he says:" 'In compounding a Numeral
with a Noun, the Burmese never use a
as "one man": - but
simple numeral
employ, as auxiliary effixes, words or terms
signifying either the class to which the
name belongs, the use to which it is put,
or some shape. form, or idea to which it
may have some resemblance real or other-
wise. These affixes may be styled generic.
that is descriptive of some class or kind.
The term numeral affix would be incorrect;
a numeral affix being one that points out
the number of the root, to which attached'.
-
It seems that Latter wrote his grammar
before he had acquired a working know-
ledge of the language. His analyses leave
much to be desired, and his examples
bristle with errors both idiomatic and
orthographic. But credit should be given
to him for putting on record this important
aspect of the language that had hitherto
been neglected by Burmese scholars. Seven
years later Dr. A. Judson presented these
'auxiliary affixes* in a more scholarly
manner. Judson was an extremely industrious
and perceptive American Baptist Missionary,

III. JUSTIFICATION
It is my submission that the class of
numeratives, with which we are dealing, is
made up of three sets of words, each being
a different subclass which, I suggest, may
be called Classifiers, Quantifiers and
Repeaters.
A classifier is a word for an attribute
of a specifie object, some of which may
have more than one; a repeater is the
specific object itself or part of it, used as
numerative; whilst a quantifier concerns
itself with the estimating of things by some
sort of measure size, extension, weight,
amount or number especially of ten or
multiples of ten. All these are numeratives,
since the patterns! 5 are used in enumera-
ting things:
179
Another common denominator among
classifiers, quantifiers and repeaters is that
all are one-word nouns. Classifiers are,
however, not independent, or they can
never be a determinatum16 of the designa-
tory word they classify, whereas the
quantifiers and repeaters may be dependent
or substantive nouns, and some of the
quantifiers and all repeaters can be deter-
minata. Incidentally, a classifier or a repea-
ter, because of its peculiar nature, can
never be directly rendered into a language
that has no such linguistic features, while
a quantifier lends itself easily to translation.
To give precision to the points that have
been made, I should like to make use of
some examples in a paper by Robbins Burl-
ing, kindly sent to me in manuscript.17

A classifier indicates, an attribute of
the object which assigns it to a class.
Each classifier can be applied to several
objects of similar attribute, and some
of these objects may have more than
one attribute. A person may be classed
as a human being Or as an animal
according to his behaviour; and a horse
or an elephant as merely an animal or
as a mount; and a sword as just a straight
long thing or as a weapon. The choice of
the classifier is prompted by the occasion.
A close examination of the hard core of
these classifiers shows that the Burmese
people's conception of things may be
divided into three distinctive categories,
viz., animate, inanimate and conceptual
object.
