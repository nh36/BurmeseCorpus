A Note on the Petrology and Provenance
of the
Wébu and Marble ('Alabaster')
Inscription Stones of the Kyaukse Area.
by
Professor Tha Hla
Mr. Chairman,
With your kind permission, I wish to add a short note on the
petrology and provenance of the 'webu' stone and marble, formerly
called 'alabaster' about which Professor Luce has spoken in the lecture.
At the request of the learned lecturer, made sometime ago, I visited
the shed in Mandalay recently where some of the inscriptions are kept.
There I inspected a few of the inscriptions referred to by Professor
Luce and also managed to bring back a couple of fragments of the
'webu' stone for laboratory study back here in the Geology Department
of the University of Rangoon.
The 'webu' stone is now identified as a garnetiferous micaschist.
Mineralogically, the rock is composed essentially of biotite mica,
quartz and garnet, the last being in subordinate amounts. It is a high-
grade metamorphic rock. Owing to the extremely fragile nature of the
rock (due to weathering), it was not possible to make a satisfactory
micro-section of the same for optical examination of its detailed
mineral composition. In petrology, schists and gneisses are known as
foliated rocks. Foliation consists essentially of a parallel arrangement
of the constituent minerals of the rocks, and this parallel arrangement
often induces a tendencey to split in definite directions, although the
fracture is usually not as perfect and regular as in properly cleaved
rocks. Foliation is a result of very high pressures and stresses produced
by earth movements, usually associated with regional metamorphism.
When a rock is foliated, the original mineral grains are not only re-
arranged to give a parallel orientation but also more or less completely
recrystallised. In many cases, foliation is also accompanied by
formation of new minerals. The difference between the two classes of

rocks-schists and gneisses-is not very definite. On the whole the
gneisses are coarse-grained rocks, resembling crushed igneous rocks,
while the schists are much finer in texture, approaching that of slates,
and in many cases believed to be derived from sediments.
The 'webu' stone is a typical schist with a finely foliated structure
which lends itself to eassy splitting along foliation planes. The colour
of the rock is dark which is the colour of the biotite mica. Viewed
from certain directions, it presents a highly glossy appearance owing
to the splendent or more or less pearly lustre possessed by the basal
planes of biotite. The stones inspected in the Mandalay shed are now in
a weathered condition and are quite soft. The surface flakes off very
easily with deleterious effects on the inscribed characters.
The few specimens of 'wébu' rock exhibited here were collected by
a student of geology from Kyaukse. They are also in a condition of
weathering even more advanced than that suffered by the inscription
stones kept in Mandalay. The student admitted that these specimens
were loose rock debris, lying on the south-east slope of the Wébu hill.
With such loose stones, exposed to the sun and rain, the advanced stage
of weathering is only to be expected.
In modern times, La Touche was the first geologist who made a
passing reference to the foliated rocks of Kyaukse. In his discussion on
the extent of the Archaean rock outcrops, La Touche states 1:
On the eastern bank (i.e. of the Irrawaddy) the Palaeozoic
rocks of the Shan Plateau come right down to the plains
of the Irrawaddy and the Archaean gneisses are found to
occur only in a few outlying hills rising abruptly from the
alluvium, including the Sagyin Hills, manily composed of
crystalline limestone, which is largely quarried as a statuary
marble, and Mandalay Hill which consists of the same
marble traversed by veins of granite,
The gneisses appear again at the foot of the plateau scarp at
Kyaukse, where there are large marble quarries, 25 miles
south of Mandalay……

During the field season of 1911-12, P, N. Datta of the Indian
Geological Survey, working in the Burma section surveyed the Kyaukse
area and mapped the same (Sheet 93 C:2). Copies of the said sheet
with Datta's geological information are not at present available in the
Burma Geological Department which is unfortunate. In Datta's Field
Progress Report as quoted by Clegg, somewhat more definite
information regarding the mica-schists of the Wébu hill is given in
these worlds 2 :
Near Kyaukse (21 36° 30°: 96° 10° 30°)-- from the very
eastern edge of the town rises a precipitous hill which is
seon to extend E. - W. as a range for about five miles.
The high precipitous hill near the town constitutes the
highest part of the range and is formed of a well-foliated
felsparquartz-biotite gneiss bearing the pagoda with the
trigonometrical station (height 975 feet), conspicuous for
many a mile around. The remainder of the range, i.e., east
of the gneissic mass, is composed of indurated micaceous
sandy shale, grey thin bedded quartzite, argillites, micaceous
schist and crystalline limestone. The shale is almost
unaltered in places, but in others it has been converted into
argillite and mica-schist. The crystalline limestone is
well seen four miles east of Kyaukse.

As regards provenance, there is no doubt that the biotite-schist
blocks on which many of the early--Kyaukse historical records were
inscribed must have come from the ancient quarries situated some-
where along the Wébu hillsides. Here, Datta's 'precipitous Hill', rising
from the very eastern edge of the town and extending E. W. as a
range for about five miles must be our Webu Taung. The specimens.
exhibited are found to match exactly with the similar inscription stones
kept in Mandalay. The only detail which requires field checking seems
to be the probable location of the ancient quarries on the Webu ridge.
From Datta's description of the various rock types froming the ridge,
the mica-schist outcrops are to be found in the more easterly portion
of the Webu ridge. It is therefore probable that the ancient quarries
of biotite-schist lie somewhere in the eastern part of the Webu hill.
Time permitting, I hope to go into the field in the near future to
check the site.

I am glad to note that Professor Luce has changed the terminology
from alabaster to marble for certain white or light-coloured inscription
stones of the Pinya period, found in the Myinsaing-Myingondaing area.
As regards scientific mineral terminology, all modern authorities 3 on
mineralogy are unanimous that the use of the term 'alabaster' should
properly be confined to a fine-grained and compact snow-white or light
coloured massive variety of mineral gypsum, the naturally occurring
hydrated sulphate of calcium. The light-coloured stone on which the
records of the Pinya period were inscribed resembles the fine-grained
massive variety of gypsum. But the mineral is crystalline calcite-
anhydrous carbonate of calcium-of which marble and all other forms of
limestone are composed. Thus, strictly speaking, the proper petrological
name for the inscription stones under reference should be 'marble' and
not 'alabaster'. Inspite of the superficial resemblance, the two stones
can be very easilo distinguished. A field geologist often carries with
him dilute hydrochloric acid in a small glass bottle. Marble or any
other form of limestone will effervesce with the acid. Alabaster which
is mineral gypsum will not do so. A drop of the acid can be put on the
stone surface by means of a glass rod. If this acid method is not
convenient for the field, one could use one's thumb nail. According to
Moh's Scale of Hardness, marble which is mineral calcite has a hardness
of 3 and cannot therefore be scratched with the finger nail. On the
other hand, alabaster has a hardness of 2 and can be scratched with
the finger nail without difficulty.
However, it must be conceded that this confusion of terminology
is a relict of very ancient times. During the days before Christ,
the ancient Greek writers used the word 'alabaster' not only for gypsum
but also for stalagmite which is a variety of limestone usually found as
conical pillars rising up from the floors of many limestone caves.

Describing the forms assumed by mineral calcite in its natural
occurrences, Dana states 4 :
Stalagmite, or a solid kind of travertine (see below) when
on a large scale, is the alabaster of the ancient writers,
that is, the stone of which ointment vases, of a certain form
called alabasters, were made. A locality near Thebes, now
well known, was largely explored by the ancients, and the
material has often been hence called Egyptian alabaster. It
was formerly called onyx and onychites because of its beautiful
banded structure. In the arts, it is often now called Oriental
alabaster or onyx marble.
Thus, having due regard for the ancient wisdom, it may perhaps
be permissible to use the words 'Egyptian alabaster' or 'Oriental
alabaster' for a certain type of limestone. Even then, the use of the
term should be restricted only to the stalagmitic varity of limestone.
and not to the marble or any other variety of limestone, in order to
avoid further confusion. Unfortunately, however, this confusion of
terminology was carried right down to the middle of the 19th century
when Rev. Mason, discussing the 'Acidiferous Earthy Minerals' of the
Tenassarim Coast Mentions 5:
Much of the alabaster of which ornaments are made
is stalagmite; but all the alabaster images of this coast are
made of marble; and not of compact gypsum, which they
much resemble.
If only Mason had stopped with the first part of the sentence quoted
above, the position would have been quite clear for later workers. But
the second part of the above sentence leaves no room for doubt that
Rev. Mason went even beyond the limit imposed by the ancient
writings in equating not only the stalagmite variety but also quite
unwarrantably the marble variety of limestone with alabaster. Indeed,
even for stalagmite the use of the word 'alabaster' should properly be
qualified with the epithet 'Egyptian' or 'Oriental'. In this connection I
should also mention that Dr. Mason was not quite alone in the matter;
Dr. Judson also used the terms marble and alabaster indiscriminately
in his Burmese-English dictionary.

Finally, I wish to compliment Professor Luce for arriving at the
conclusion that the Kyaukse area, and not the Sagyin area, is the true
source of the marble slabs used as inscription stones down to the late-
Ava period. It should be noted that this conclusion was reached
apparently without the prior knowledge that there are outcrops 'of
marble (crystalline limestone) about four miles due east of the present
Kyaukse town. It must have been long since any marble was quarried
for inscription-making purposes in the environs of Kyaukse. In modern
times all the Burmese marble that has been quarried either for statuary
or tombstone purposes comes only from Sagyin, a place some 28 miles
due north of Mandalay. It would have been easy for the unwary to
assume that the Pinya-period marble inscription stones came from
Sagyin, especially when Sagyin is not really far (in the present-day
sense) from the field on which Professor Luce has been working. There
are before you two specimens of marble collected by a student of the
Geology Department from Kyaukse. One is perfectly white and fine-
grained in texture. The other specimen is a little darker or bluish in
colour and the texture is also coarser than that of the first. These
specimens are of the same petrological quality as the Sagyin marble.
I feel that a geological visit to the Webu range at Kyaueks is called
for in order to settle the provenance of both these stones in the field
once and for all.
