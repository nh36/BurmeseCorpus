In Burma natural soda is found in many places of the Dry Zone and especially
in the drier parts of Burma such as Meikhtila, Myingyan, Yamethin, Thazi, Taungdwingyi,
Taungtha, Shwebo and many other places. The soda deposits of Burma are similar to the
natural soda of Parantij, Ahmedbad district of India. After the monsoons the mineral
springs dry up, and natural soda crystals, together with other salts, collect on the sand.
The crude product is collected by scraping the upper layer and sold as such. Due to its
detergent property the crude product is used as cleansing agent for kitchen utensils, hence
the local name "sand soap". In some localities the crude product is lixiviated with water
and filtered through bag of cloth. The filtered solution is crystallised and the product used
for washing. The local name for the product is "seit sappya" meaning "filtered soap".
During the Japanese Regime, large quantities of sand soap were used for the manufacture of
soap.
2. Sampling and Qualitative Analysis
Samples of natural soda were collected as found and sent to the author in air tight
containers. In all determinations in which mixtures of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate
are involved, the contact of the sample with air or moisture must be avoided as far as
possible. Such contacts of the mixture with air of moisture will result in the change of
composition of the mixture. (2)
Furthermore, samples of sand soap cannot be ground because sodium sulphate and
calcium carbonate interact to form calcium sulphate. The author ground a sample of sand
soap and extracted it with water. Although the first filtrate was clear, this filtrate gave a
precipitate when it came in contact with later washings. The reason is that although calcium
sulphate formed during grinding is insoluble in sodium sulphate solution, it becomes
soluble in water when all the sodium sulphate is removed. Thus, these washings contain
calcium sulphate in solution which, when mixed with the first filtrate containing sodium
sulphate, again threw down the calcium sulphate as precipitate.
Ungrouud samples on the other hand give a clear filtrate throughout, on extraction
with water.
From the above facts it is obvious that samples of sand soap cannot be treated by the

3. Quantitative Analysis of Natural Soda
All samples contain the following substances, viz, sodium carbonate, sodium
bicarbonate, sodium sulphate, sodium chloride, calcium carbonate, magnesium carbonate,
aluminium and iron as silicates, silica, organic matter and moisture.
Although all samples contain all the above constituents, the nature of the samples.
are quite different from one another. Some samples are light coloured and the sand or
silica is coarse grained and consequently analysis of such samples are quite easy, and
conventional methods can be used. Some samples on the other hand are highly coloured
and the earthy materials are also fine grained. Such samples on treatment with water will
yield very stable colloidal soluțions and removal of colloidal particles are difficult. Such
samples are difficult to analyse and special methods of analysis have to be adopted. These
will be discussed in due course.
Furthermore, the presence of sodium bicarbonate and sodium carbonate in the
samples require special methods of estimating sodium bicarbonate since the usual
ethod of estimating sodium bicarbonate by dissolving in water will lead
to decomposition of the bicarbonate (2). As mentioned before samples of natural
soda could not be ground due to the danger of the sodium sulphate inter-acting
with the calcium carbonate resulting in the formation of calcium sulphate. Therefore,
to have concordant results between separate determinations, a fairly large quantity of the
sample must be used for the estimation and only then the result can be representative.
Following the above procedure, the author has been able to obtain fairly concordant results
between different determinations.

(a) Hugo Duvonvit (7) gave a method of determining sodium bicarbonate in the
mixture by heating it to about 150°C in a current of dry air free from carbon dioxide, and
absorbing and weighing the gaseous products.
This method, although is suitable for mixture of pure sodium carbonate and bicarbo
nate, is unsuitable for natural soda samples, because at that temperature, the organic matter
present might be oxidised, and silica also will react with sodium carbonate, and both the
above reactions will yield carbon dioxide and hence the result will be too high.
(b) Sodium bicarbonate in natural soda can however be determined by using the
method of H. H. Chesny (6) described in "Scotts standard method of chemical analysis"
In this method, the sodium bicarbonate in the dry sample of natural soda, is quantitatively
converted into the carbonate, by adding slight excess of decinormal sodium hydroxide
solution.

applied to solid mixture of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate.
This method has the advantage over the H. H. Chesny's method, in that, it can be
used to determine sodium bicarbonate in highly coloured natural soda samples.

5. Origin of the Natural Soda
The origin of the sodium carbonate has been explained by three hypothesis:
(a) Percolating water, containing dissolved atmospheric or volcanic carbon dioxide,
can act on volcanic rocks extracting sodium or potassium carbonates or bicarbonate. These
waters will then accumulate in the waters of basin or in the mineral springs. According to
T.M. Chartard (5) the predominance of sodium salts over potassium salts in natural waters
is partly due to the soda rocks being more easily decomposable than potassium rocks, and

partly due to the fact that on passing through the soil, the potassium salts being absorbed
more than the sodium salts.
(b) According Sickenberger (17) the alkali sulphates are reduced by organic matter to
sulphide and the sulphide so formed are acted upon by the atmospheric carbon dioxide to.
form alkali carbonate.
He found algae in the waters of certain soda lakes in Egypt and also noted the
evolution of hydrogen sulphide and the deposition of iron sulphide.
(c) Double decomposition between calcium bicarbonate solution and the solution of
sodium sulphate with the precipitation of calcium sulphate.
Author could not find any trace of sulphide or hydrogen sulphide in any of the
Burmese samples. Thus the second theory suggesting the reduction of alkali sulphate is not
possible.
It is very unlikely that the first theory is possible because places, where natural soda
is found, are not volcanic.
The most possible mode of formation however can be the third hypothesis. Most
of the samples under examination contain fairly high percentage of sodium sulphate and
as a matter of fact there are some samples which contain mostly sodium sulphate with
traces of bicarbonate and carbonate. Thus it is quite possible for the under ground water
containing sodium sulphate, be acted upon by percolating waters containing calcium
bicarbonate with the formation of sodium bicarbonate and calcuim sulphate: The sodium
bicarbonate being soluble will remain in solution and the soda waters rise up to the soda
beds by capillary action. On exposure to the atmosphere, an equilibrium is formed
between sodium bicarbonate and sodium carbonate and the composition of the evaporated
crystals will depend on the conditions prevailing at the salt beds.
Conclusions:
Sodium sulphate, sodium carbonate and bicarbonate are the important
constituents of natural soda. Both of them are very useful and important compounds for
Burma. Sodium carbonate and bicarbonate are the raw material for the manufacture of
caustic soda and sodium sulphate will become very important if Paper industry is put up
in Burma with bamboo as raw material.
Further work on the separation of two substances from natural soda can be carried
out. But the more important work at present is the examination of the extent of the soda
deposits. This is much work for the geologists.

Points of caution: As has been stated before, that this method involves the
conversion of sodium bicarbonate to carbonate by a known volume of excess sodium
hydroxide solution and the unreacted sodium hydroxide is determined by titration with
standard acid after removing sodium carbonate with barium chloride solution. In the
removal of the carbonate by barium chloride, considerable excess of barium chloride must
be avoided because barium ions in the presence of hydroxide ions will absorb carbon dioxide
from the air and the result will be poor. Also, when the precipitation is done in the cold
or in the presence of large excess of sodium hydroxide, a considerable proportion of sodium
hydroxide will also be removed due to formation of basic barium. carbonate and the result
for sodium hydroxide will be too low.
Procedure: Sodium bicarbonate is weighed into a dry 500 cc. conical flask. If
mixture of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate is to be experimented, each of the constituent
is separately weighed into the conical flask and the content is mixed by stirring with a
platinum wire. A known volume of excess of sodium hydroxide solution is run into the
conical flask and it is gently shaken so as to dissolve the mixture. The solution is
then diluted to about 150 cc. and heated to about 80°C. Add few c.c. of barium chloride
solution at a time from a bùrette, allowing the precipitate to settle and noting whether a
further quantity of barium chloride causes more precipitate to form. Total volume of
barium chloride added is noted. Phenolphthalein is added and the resulting solution is
titrated with standard hydrochloric acid. This preliminary result will be too low. Therefore
take another quantity of mixture of sodium carbonate and bicarbonate and add excess of

by the local concentration of the acid. The solution at this stage will contain all the
sodium carbonate with only a slight excess of sodium hydroxide. Heat the solution to about
80°C and run in the required quantity of barium chloride. Add phenolphthalein and com-
plete the titration.
