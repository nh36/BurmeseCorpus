INTRODUCTORY
1. I believe that the thesis set out in this paper is applicable
to all the agglutinative and isolating languages spoken on the
peninsula known as Indo-China or Further India. It is applicable also
to languages of this class spoken much further afield, such as
Mongolian which belong to the Ural-Altaic family of speech. But
it is prudent not to generalise too much, and so I confine it to the
languages spoken in Burma, though many of these extend widely
beyond the borders of Burma. It is based upon the study of materials
collected from seventy or more languages or dialects. It is foresha-
dowed in a chapter included in my book referred to in para 3 below,
which was set up in type in 1938. The first draft of the present paper
was written in 1948. No orginality is claimed for it. I believe that
Dr. Schmidt of Vienna propounded a similar thesis many years ago.
2. In the case of inflected Indo-European languages grammar
is a very complex affair. Nouns, adjectives and pronouns are declined;
not only are there many cases, but also many declensions. Verbs are
conjugated in accordance with person, number, tense and mood
and there are many conjugations. The stem of the word undergoes
various changes, so the various declensions and conjugations must
be exhibited in full. In this way all nouns, pronouns, adjectives and
verbs are included in the grammar, though only selected examples are
used in handbooks to illustrate each declension and conjugation. In
the case of agglutinating and isolating languages all this is different.
Words are not subject to inflection, but maintain their forms unchanged.
Full words, enclitics, particles or affixes, and word-order are the elements
that constitute speech. Nouns, pronouns, adjectives and verbs therefore
belong to the dictionary, and grammar shrinks until it becomes
synonymous with syntax.

4. Purpose of the Present Paper. I may now be expected
to say how far I have advanced in my endeavour to replace the old
unnatural methods of dealing with these languages by a new, natural
and scientific method. It is the purpose of this paper to reply to that
expectation. I shall, of course, have to confine myself to the more
general features since I am dealing with so many families and groups
of languages.
5. The Scientific Approach. Having insisted that the structures
of these languages cannot be formulated and described in the
Procrustean bed of Indo-European categories and linguistic terms
without gross distortion and injury, I now suggest that the natural
and scientific method is just to allow them to reveal their own
-structures and features. We must examine them without preconceived
ideas and describe exactly what we find. This method, which seems
so very obvious, is precisely the one that has rarely been attempted.
I possess many dozens of handbooks of the Burmese language, written
between the years 1815 and 1940. All of them are based upon Indo-
European categories and formulations, and not one of them is really
helpful to my purpose. It is true that R. Grant Brown's very readable

LANGUAGES.
* 6. When we examine these languages without prejudice, and allow
them to exhibit their proper features in their own way, we notice
the following characteristics :-
(1) Morphological Class. Non-inflected, words main-
taining their forms unchanged; agglutinating and isolating or better:
analytical and affixing; generally monosyllabic, though variable in this
respect. Many of the monosyllables betray evidence of being
broken down forms of very complex monosyllables and even of
disyllables.
(Note: The probability is that long ago these languages
were to some extent inflectional, as Tibetan and
its dialects are still, though perhaps not to the
same extent. But this tendency came to an end
and the inflections were obliterated by extensive
phonetic decay. My description of them as
"non-inflected" applies to them in their present
forms.)
(2) Classes of words.
(a) "Full", free and significant words such as can be
used as primary words,

these. They constitute the greater part of the

(b) "Empty", attached, formative or functional elements,
better known as affixes or particles. These largely
take the place and exercise the functions of Indo-
European inflections and indicate syntactical relation-
ships. They cannot stand alone, but are always
found attached to "full" words or to phrases or
sentences.
If full words be likened to the bricks in a structure, then word-
order and particles resemble the respective arrangement of the bricks,
and the mortar that keeps them in their proper places. Or, we may
liken full words to ornate beads upon a string and empty words to
dummy beads or knots that keep them in their proper places upon the
thread of intelligible discourse.
(3) Parts of Speech.
"Full" words divide themselves into
three word-classes which may be determined and defined as
follows:-
A study of the formal structure of narrative or predicative
sentences in these languages soon reveals that they consist of two (and
sometimes more) distinct parts: a subject-phrase and a predicate-phrase.
The former precedes the later and is separated from it by a pause.
Generally too there are special affixes or markers which denote which
phrase is which. We discover also that the primary or head word of
the subject-phrase, or actor-phrase, differs in important respects from
the primary word in the predicate-phrase, or action-phrase. Words
which can fill the position of primary word in the subject-phrase are
never found filling the primary position in the predicate-phrase.
Conversely, words which can fill the position of primary word in the
predicate-phrase can never-unless modified by a proclitic, enclitic or
infix-fill the position of primary word in the subject-phrase. Further,
we discover that many subject-phrase_primary words take possessive or
directional or locative affixes (very similar to English prepositions)
which are never taken by predicate-phrase primary words; while the
latter take temporal affixes only rarely found attached to the former.
Since the primary word in the subject-phrase denotes in most cases
a person, a living creature or an inanimate thing, i. e. an object, it may
conveniently be called a "noun". Similarly since the primary word in

Since particles or affixes can never stand alone but are only found
attatched to "full" words or to sentences or phrases or subordinate
clauses, they can hardly be regarded as words in their own right. They
are not parts of speech so much as parts of parts of speech.
(4) Word-Order: This is a feature of considerable syntactical
importance, particularly so in those languages that make a minimal
use of particles of affixes.
(5) Principle regulating word order: This, as we shall soon
see, is a feature of fundamental importance. In many languages
the parts of the sentence and the order of words are characteristic
of a mental attitude which habitually places an adjunct or qualifying
word before the primary or principal word with which it is associated.
Other languages adopt the opposite attitude, and place the adjunct
word after the primary word. We have therefore to deal with two
types of word order.
Type A, or "Adjunct-Primary” languages, such as Burmese,
which place the adjunct (J) before the primary word (P) with which
it is associated. These may be called the J-P languages.
Type B, or “Primary-Adjunct“ languages, which place the
adjunct after the primary word, as in Shan, Mon and Karen. These
are the P-J languages.
It is apparent that Type B is in many respects the reverse or mirror
image of Type A. This fundamental distinction may be used to distin-
guish the western or Tibeto-Chinese Languages from the eastern or pre-
Chinese languages such as Shan, Karen, and the Mon-Khmer languages.
The former belong to Type A, the latter to Type B.
(6) Position of Particles of Affixes: It is difficult to give
general rules for their use, as some languages make more use of them
than others. Moreover there are many kinds of particles fulfilling
different functions. Those that are similar in function to English
prepositions are used with great regularity. In Type A languages they
follow the noun, whereas the adjunct precedes it, thus: J-N-p, where
p represents such an affix. In Type B languages they precede
the noun whereas the adjunct follows it, thus: p-N-J. Nexus or

(7) Classification of Particles.
(a) Sometimes full words are used functionally. There
are numerous particles that are almost certainly decayed
or broken down full words. But there are also parti-
cles that betray no trace of such an origin.
(b) Some particles are roughly translatable; these are
often decayed full words. Others merely modify the
meanings of words to which they are attached, like
vague adjunts. Others are purely functional and cannot
be translated. Such possess only syntactical and
indicatory functions.
(c) Among the roughly translatable particles are those
signifying from, towards, together with, by means of,
as regards, also, thus, indeed so, please, as for, of
course, not even, more than, yet; and those that can
be translated by the English relative pronouns: who
and which.

(d) Among the untranslatable ones are indicatory particles
such as those indicating the subject and object; those
which close or terminate a sentence; those indicating
interrogation or the imperative. There are also proclitics
and enclitics such as used to derive an abstract noun
from a verb. Finally there are particles that are now
used for euphony only, and others that indicate
deference or permission.
It is evident that much investigation will be required before we
may hope to classify particles satisfactorily and define their correct
usage. This differs somewhat in different languages so that only very
general statements are possible here.

10. Development of Sentence: Just as adjunct words have
been added to primary words to narrow down their general meanings
and make them more definite, so subjuncts may in turn be added to
adjuncts to carry the process further. It is as though in English we
were to develop a sentence as follows:-
2
(a) The man left.
(b) The rich man left yesterday.
(c) The very rich man who built this house left yesterday
for London.
It is upon such lines that the Indo-Chinese sentence is built up.
Subjuncts are usually added directly to adjuncts, as in our phrase "big,
brown, male dog". The formula for languages of Type A would be:
J3-J₂-J1-(p)-N where J3 and J 2 are two subjuncts (such as “big”
and "brown") added to the adjunct J 1 ("male"). The corresponding
formula for Type B languages would be: N-(p)-J₁-J2-J3. The
entire syntactical scheme is founded upon the extension, and hence the
restriction, of the general meanings of the primary words standing as
subject, object and verb; in word-order; and in the functions and use
of affixes or formative or indicating particles.

12. Besides comparing the patterns as a whole, we must seek for
some common elements to compare in detail. Now the formal features
of languages are not arbitrary. They reflect common human experien- :
ces; those found in real life. So we find in discourse and narrative
certain categories which seek grammatical expression in all languages.
Such are: (a) number, in nouns and verbs, (b) sex, (c) degrees of
comparison, including equalities and superlatives, (d) passive forms of
verbs, (e) causatives, (f) negative expressions, (g) interrogative expres-

and compared, and so form a wider basis for genealogical comparison.
It is in the treatment of these categories that we may find the old
handbooks useful, for many of them have assembled the materials for
us. But we shall refrain from using such terms as: declension,
conjugation, case, tense, adjective, adverb, etc. which abound in these
books, but which all bear Indo-European associations alien to our
purpose. Though the comparison of these categories will carry us
further, they will not get us far enough.

to secure the financial assistance that would enable me to complete my

I have, in the foregoing paper, made brief use of a system of
symbolic syntax that I devised for the purpose of illustrating and
clarifying the relations that connect together the various elements that
constitute speech sentences in Indo-Chinese languages.
Mathematicians make extensive use of symbolic forms which
enable them to express complex situations in simple visual form, so
that they can view them objectively, concentrate upon the essentials,
and perform mechanically and easily operations that would otherwise
prove extremely difficult and complex, if not impossible to carry out.

Similarly logicians have felt the need to devise systems of symbolic
logic in order to objectify and clarify their own peculiar problems.
Again chemists have for long used symbols to express the natures and
structures of chemical compounds and reactions. Half a line of symbols
will show at a glance the molecular compositions of two or three
different substances; the nature of the reactions that take place when
they are brought into contact; and the molecular compositions of the
resulting substances. A verbal description of the same chemical
substances and their reactions would have filled five or six [lines, and
made much less impression upon our minds. Indeed the recent deve-
lopment of organic chemistry would have been impossible without
the use of chemical symbolism. The fact is that symbols viewed by the
eye appeal to our minds more clearly and forcibly than strings of
words, whether written or spoken, can ever do.
Moreover they are
more easily manipulated and do not change their significance during
the process.
So, in the same way, I have long felt the need for a system of
symbolic syntax that will exhibit essential relations in concrete visible
form. We need a device that will ease mental labour and present to
the eye at a glance the structural features of every language with which
we have to deal. Beyond that it ought to be of great use when we
come to compare or contrast the features of different languages. In
short it ought to be of use in comparative as well as in descriptive
linguistics. Professor Otto Jespersen published such a system many
years ago, but it was devised for the structures of Indo-European
languages, and had to apply to grammatical inflections as well as to
syntactical features. Consequently it turned out to be deterringly
complicated. My own system, being devised for agglutinating and
isolating languages, is simple, elastic, and sufficiently comprehensive
for our purpose. With suitable modifications it can even be used to
illustrate word-building and word-structure. It can therefore be used
for Symbolic Morphology as well as well as Symbolic Syntax.

3. Practical Test.
117
Although the thesis of the foregoing paper was based upon the
study of seventy languages, nevertheless it must be subjected to the
practical test of verification. After completing the paper, I decided
to test the thesis in the following manner. I have available translations
of the Bible Parable of the Prodigal Son in twenty seven languages
belonging to four different families and eight different language groups.
They are :-
(1) Burmese, Arakanese, Yaw, Tavoyan, Intha, Taungyo, and
Danu. (2) Maru. (3) Lisaw. (4) Lai, Taungtha and Southern
Chin. (5) Kachin. (6) Shan (or Tai) and Khun. (7) Mon (or
Talaing) and Palaung. (8) The Karen dialects: Sgaw, Wewaw, Pwo,
Mopwa, Karenbyu, Bwe, Padaung, Gheko, Taungthu, and Karenni.
Down the left hand sides of four long sheets of paper I indicated
in turn the Rules (a) to (g) formulated in paragraph 8, allowing plenty
of vertical space for examples illustrating or disobeying each rule.
Then on sheet 1 I ruled seven vertical colums, one for each of the
dialects of Burmese. On sheet 2 I ruled six columns, one each for
Maru, Lisaw, Lai, Taungtha, Southern Chin and Kachin. Sheet 3 had
colums for Shan, Khun, Mon and Palaung. Finally sheet 4 had columns
for each of the ten dialects of Karen. The texts were then scrutinised
and entries made at the proper places in the various columns. Excep-
tions as well as regularities were noted. After all the texts had been
dealt with I examined the marked sheets. Although I had expected
considerable regularity, the results astonished me. Not only did I find
that the seven dialects of Burmese agreed exactly with one another, but
the remaining Tibeto-Burmese languages on sheet 2 also agreed exactly
with them. A single column marked Type A would have sufficed for
the lot. Similarly on sheet 3 the four languages, representing two
families, agreed exactly with one another, and also agreed with the ten
dialects of Karen, with the exception of Rule (d) in which Karen had
the order of Type A. So that except for this one exception a single
column marked Type B would have done or all these languages.
All this goes to show how fundamental and pervading the general
principle stated in para 6 (5) really is. It is the principle, I referred
to as the J-P and P-J principle, which characterises the two types of
structure that I have called Type A and Type B. Perhaps the principle
might be called "the adjunct and primary principle" for short.
