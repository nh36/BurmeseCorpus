Note. Frequent references are made below to the 1945.
"Report of the Commissioner of National Museum,
Collections and Exhibitions", the U Ba Hlaing Committee;
also to Mr. L. F. Taylor's article “A_Burma Provincial
Museum" at Journal of the Burma Research Society,
Vol. XIV, Part 1, pp 1-20.
Before we
1. OBJECTS OF THE MUSEUM.
start to plan our Museum, we must obviously make up our
minds about what we are aiming at. A Museum may
have many objects or only one. In the Committee's
Report and in Taylor's article several possibilities are
stated; all, or nearly all, are worthy objects; and they are
not exhaustive. Here, in brief, are some of them:-
(i) Window-dressing. Muscum a show-piece in the
capital and chief port of Burma. To im-
press, interest or amuse, especially foreign
visitors and globe-trotters, also the capital's
idle rich and idle poor.
(ii) Exhibition of the commercial products of Burma
(cf. the Burma Section of the Imperial
Institute, S. Kensington). To interest and
impress foreigners and lead to trade expan
sion overseas. Agriculture, Forestry, Mines
and Minerals, Arts and Crafts etc.
(iii) Conservation. To preserve for posterity relics
or specimens of all the past of Burma.
Geology, Palaeontology, Pre-history, Ar-
chaeology, Epigraphy, History, etc.
(iv) Ditto, but with emphasis on the present.
Geography, Fauna and Flora, Ethnology,
Linguistics, etc.

2. FOUR PRINCIPLES. None of these objects,
except perhaps (i), is to be rejected. (ii) is too narrow an
aim in itself, though an important section of the Muscum
should be based on it. (iii) to (vi) comprise what one
may call the 19th century conception of a Museum,
summed up by Taylor in (vii). The main principles here
are Conservation and Exhibition; in my opinion, they are
basic. But only too often they lead nowhere, and the
Museum remains a dead thing, a mere lumberroom of
curiosities, unrelated to the life of the country. Where
should they lead?-Taylor's (viii) provides an answer,
though not, I think, a complete answer. The purpose,
he says, is primarily educational, speaking to the eye
rather than the ear, for the coolie no less than for the
graduate. But the exhibition, by itself, will only bring
the student up to the fringe of Burma's problems: it
will not help to solve them. It will contain, say, models

explain the "debt" system of the former, nor the

3. INTEGRATION IN THE LIFE OF BURMA.
Yet all this is not enough.
Burma's Museum, in my opinion, must be integra-
ted at every point in the life of the country, play a vital
part in its educational system, answer its needs social,
economic and commercial, and tackle its urgent problems
in every branch of government and industry susceptible
to research. Various Government Departments have
often their own research sections; but these are generally
engaged on short term problems and emergencies. I
should like to see the State Museum, State Library and
State Research Institute, staffed in part by the various
Departments with officers placed on special research
duty, but controlled directly by a Minister for long term
Planning.
4. DISTRICT MUSEUMS. What more can be
done to integrate Burma's Museum in the life of Burma?
Much. What pleases me most in the Committee's Report
is its view of the Museum not as a dusty dead thing, a
mere record of the past, but as a living centre of Burma's
life, culture, education and progress. In Para III 6 they
recommend "a network of local museums in all the
districts", though rather as a pious hope for the future,
and limited in Recommendation (8) to local museums at
Mandalay, Prome, Bassein, Akyab and Moulmein, run
entirely by Government officers or nominees. In my view,
the founding of local museums is essential and urgent;
it should not be postponed indefinitely, but should
proceed pari passu with that of the Central Museum.
The present and impending changes in Burma's constitu-
tion make this coordination doubly urgent. "Frontier
Areas", we are told, are about to merge with "Ministerial
Burma' or at least join it and not be separate as in the

past. Each party, obviously, has an enormous amount to
learn about the other. Major J. H. Green, a great
authority on Burma, has told us (but this was ten years

have never even heard of the existence of the Burmans.
On the other hand I have met Burman undergraduates

increase mutual respect and loyalty, to kill stupid
contempt, neglect, fear and suspicion, what could be

Moreover, in the early stages of the collection of
materials, such cooperation between centre and district.
will be extremely helpful to both parties. Thus Kengtung,
while collecting the materials needed for its own museum,
will collect also for Rangoon, while the all-Burma
experts of Rangoon will always be ready to advise or help
Kengtung in dealing with its local problems and with
the practical work of labelling, mapping, photography,
taxidermy, etc. Taylor even says (and the Committee
support him) that "every decent school ought to possess
a small museum, and the boys ought to take an active
part in the collection of specimens."
5. RELATION OF DISTRICT AND CENTRE.
What should be the relation between District and Central
Museum? Should the former merely duplicate one section
of the latter?- I think not, though I see no harm in
duplication, but on the other hand a great safeguard for
survival. Judged by its choice of district museums, the
Committee seems to have regarded them as a worthy
expression of civic pride in the greater secondary cities
of Burma. I have no objection to this, though it is a
luxury. But I regard district museums as a necessity,
and my choice of them would therefore be different: every
one that I recommend must be justified on the ground of
necessity. In my view, the primary duty of the District
Museum will be Conservation of local cultures on the
fullest scale. Now if we take the languages of Burma as
enumerated in Census Reports as a yardstick (and even
where these "languages" "are only dialects, they still
represent a distinct local culture), there are well over a

hundred of such local cultures to be conserved. I am
utterly opposed to the view that such cultures are of no
value and "should be allowed to die a natural death"
which usually means death by calculated starvation.
That has been the policy of ultra-national régimes in
the past, in regard, e. g., to Mon language and education;
and it has proved a failure. That way lies danger, poli-
tically speaking; and such a view cuts at the root of
all that the Burma Museum stands for. In the variety
of Burma's cultures lie Burma's strength and hope for
the future; and no one who really knows, c. g., the wild
Was or the Head-hunting Nagas-types which the ordi-
nary ignorant man might think undesirable-will deny
that if today, by a stroke of the pen, such Was and Nagas
could, be abolished, Burma would be a loser and not a
gainer.
If this be granted, then the main task of detailed
conservation is bound to fall upon the District Museum:
for a hundred of such cultures, at a moderate estimate,
will fill a hundred rooms; and the Central Museum (which
has other tasks besides conservation) cannot find the
space to do justice to each and every local variety. Where
the District Museum will find a room, the Central
Museum must be content with a showcase.

7. OTHER DUTIES OF
DISTRICT
MUSEUMS. If Conservation is the primary duty of the
District Museum, Education is only less important. In
Kengtung, e. g., beside the large scale relief model of
Kengtung State, will be set a model of Burma as a whole.
At every point the narrower local culture will confront
the broader culture of the rest of Burma. Opposite the
Kachin or Lahu tools and fishtraps will be set the
agricultual and fishing implements of the plains. I should
even allow firms in the big towns which make or import
such implements to rent part of the space in District
Museum's in order to advertise their wares: under due
safe-guards, of course, for we must not turn the Museum
into a shop. And similarly I should encourage District
Museums to send to the Central Museum for exhibit
saleable arts and crafts objects from the remotest fron-
tiers. Furthermore, mutatis mutandis, it would be up
to each District Museum to serve its local town and area
in the same manifold ways as the Central Museum will
serve Burma and Rangoon, as described in detail below.

8. COST AND CONTROL OF DISTRICT
MUSEUMS. Here opinions may vary; and since local
conditions will certainly vary (contrast e. g., Fort Hertz
with Mandalay!), I can only throw out for criticism my
own suggestions, to be modified in each case by local
circumstances:-
(i) Ultimately the district museum should lead
its own independent life, serving and supported by its
own district. It should therefore be controlled mainly by
the local authority, state or municipality. Its running
expenses should also be met, so far as circumstances
permit, by the local authority.
(ii) At first the Central Government should be
prepared to put up grants for buildings and equipment,
mainly of a non-recurring nature. Probably the local
authority can find the site, centrally situated; but I would
strongly urge the Central Government to provide the
whole cost of the buildings. For reasons fully explained
by Taylor, buildings of wood and flimsy materials are
quite unsuitable for museums. Control of light, air and
temperature is essential: otherwise the exhibits will soon.
perish. Even brick buildings, suitable for houses, are not
suitable for museums. A museum must always be built
to suit its exhibits. When the general lay-out of the
district museum is determined, the Government Architect
may be asked to draw up plans for building in some
permanent material, brick or stone, which the P. W. D.
on the spot can erect. A grant for furniture, show cases,
initial equipment, etc. will also probably be necessary.
(iii) In remote frontier posts such as Fort Hertz,
Government must be prepared to put up the whole of the
cost. If here the Museum does its work well, I do not
think Government should grudge the cost. In the
absence of schools, the Museum should be the best agent
to spread education and enlightenment.
(iv) For non-recurrent or extraordinary expen-
diture, the District would always be free to apply to the
Centre for assistance. Such applications, from all over
Burma, would be considered yearly by the Central Com-
mittee, presided over by the Minister for long term
Planning, in reference (a) to the policy of Government,
(b) to the funds at its disposal.

11. THE BUILDING. Sir George Watt's Memo-
randum on this subject, quoted by Taylor (p. 8) bears
repetition: "An Indian Museum....should consist of a
series of long rooms with side skylights near the ceiling.
If windows are found necessary they should be confined
as much as possible to north exposures. The light should
be uniformly distributed and....well under control. But
there should be few, if any, windows in the walls, since all
space is indispensably necessary for the collections. The
walls themselves should be as thick as possible so as to
keep down the temperature. To lower the temperature is
not merely a matter of comfort for those who have to
work in the Museum: it is a necessity for the safe custody
of collections. A high temperature and too much light are
exceedingly injurious to natural history and industrial
art exhibits."
A worse danger than heat, in a Rangoon Museum,
will be damp. It will be necessary to have most, if not
all, the rooms air-conditioned, so as to keep the atmos-
phere dry, dust-less, fresh, and uniform throughout the
year. If this is done, thick walls will not be necessary.
In the absence of windows, artificial light will be
necessary to illuminate the walls, cabinets and showcases.
I would strongly recommend the use of "Daylight Lamps",
(said to involve higher initial outlay, but lower running
costs), as these will diffuse the light and leave the natural
colours unaffected.

12. Plans for District Museums. I have not
attempted to draw up a standard plan for these; I would
merely mention the Patothamya at Pagan-the most
beautiful building in Burma-as a worthy source of
inspiration for the architect (as it has been a model for
Christian Science temples in England). But district
museums will vary considerably in size and scope,
according to the cultures they conserve; and each will
have to be designed accordingly. And perhaps a Bu-
ddhist temple is not the best model for museums in
non-Buddhist arcas, such as the Chin, Naga or Kachin
Hills.
13. MISCELLANEOUS MUSEUM ACTIVI-
TIES. The Imperial Institute, South Kensington, has sets
of pictures, films and lantern-slides which can be had on
loan by any school or institution in England. It is
constantly sending lecturers all over the country to boost
Burma and its products. The Burma Central Museum-
which will have, of course, a large photographic depart-
ment should do the same, but on a much bigger and more
varied scale, since its object is educational in the widest
sense, and not merely commercial. Ultimately it should
even have its own travelling film-vans for touring the
villages and visiting the village schools.
In Rangoon itself the Museum will similarly be at

14. RESEARCH INSTITUTE AND LIBRARY.
The most disastrous loss which Burma suffered during
the past war, was the wholesale looting and destruction
of the University Library. It was unquestionably the
finest Burma library in the world; yet Burma itself knew
little of it. Situated five miles out of town and accessible
to few but staff, Honours students, and graduates of the
University, it did not have the influence it should have
had on the country or the Government. Most of the
research-work done during the twenty years of the Univer-
sity's existence was housed there, and it has all perished
or gone to Japan: the complete card-index of old Burmese
and partial index of Middle Burmese, which

forty files of typed translations from Chinese about all

from which these translations were made; phonetic
