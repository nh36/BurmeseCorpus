has developed more or less gradually and systematically.

in certain cases, one also comes across in most of the

Further still, verbs of two syllables can be classified
as curiosities. Burmese being a monosyllabic, one would
naturally assume the preponderance of monosyllabic verbs
over those of two syllables. But a closer observation would
at once dispose of such assumption. Verbs of two syllables
are apparently preferred to those of one. The moment a
Burman tries to express an idea, he is wont to use two
syllabic verbs, partly so as to be explicit, but mainly to
be more emphatic. After all to think, could, in a
well constructed sentence, be made to do the work of
think, arrange = consider, ponder; or think,
estimate deliberate; or Ɛ think, carry out = purpose,
scheme, endeavour; or $ think, create = think out,
concoct; or think, aim at = intend, design, purport.
Besides, the addition of second syllable improves the
sense and, enhances the sound-effect, which a Burman

blunt one. But, in Burmese, the substitutes are more
than mild or vague; they are polite and ornamental.

Mr. L. R. Palmer (now Professor) states in the
"An Introduction to Modern Linguistics" that language
has properly three functions: it expresses the thoughts,
feelings etc. of the speaker; it influences the behaviour of
the hearer; and it symbolizes the things to which it refers.
And, he adds, language reflects faithfully the whole
history of a nation, its culture, its sports and pastimes, its
beliefs and prejudices. Though the present article affords
very few data to work on, as it deals with just a facet of
the Burmese language, we can, nevertheless, discern the
sort of descriptive words and picturesque phrases a
Burman would use to express his thoughts and feelings
in endeavouring to influence the behaviour of another,
and how those words symbolize the things they refer to.
Further, in those words and phrases, can be found the
history, and in the euphemisms, some of the beliefs and
prejudices of the Burmese nation.
