While this paper is concerned pri-

Spanish. It is a more traditional culture

mainland. Its family system maintains
more of the patriarchical character of
Spain and Europe, although this is
probably changing rapidly particularly
among the upper and middle classes.
All of the respondents in the study are
university students and therefore represent
an upper and middle class elite population.
The results reported are therefore not
intended to apply to the general population
of the countries mentioned.

The husband as the head of the family
Probably the most general and most
sensitive question which tells us if there is
any evidence of the willingness of women
to acknowledges the leadership role and
dominance of the husband in the family,
regardless of the strength or quality of this
leadership, is the simple statement, "The
husband should be the head of the family.”
Ninety-five per cent of the Burmese men
agreed, and just about the same percentage
of women agreed (93%).

This first observation we must make

the support Burmese women give male
leadership. In the five other cultures
studied the results were generally the same.
For example, 90% of the American
(mainland i.e, not Puerto Rico) men
agreed that the husband should be the
head of the family and 84% of the Ameri-
can women. Most Americans, Indians,
Burmese and Malayans agree with the
statement, whether they be men or
women. The women in the Indian
study least agreed, only 69%! The
Burmese respondents agreed more than
those in any of the other cultures studied.
However, what happens if we break
down the agreed category into its finer
parts? When we do, we find that the close
agreement of the Burmese men and the
women weakens a bit. While 67% of the
Burmese men "strongly agree" with the

head of the family, only 58% of the
Burmese women strongly agree. Once
again, however, more Burmese women
"strongly agree" than in any of the four
other cultures studied. It is interesting to
note that in the other cultures studied, it
was also found that the gap grew in agree-
ment between men and women when the

other words in all cultures studied despite
the fact that the majority of all the women

Conclusion
We have reviewed a number of the
attitudes Burmese college students at the
University of Rangoon had on the question
of male dominance. I general we* have
discovered some very interesting compari-
sons not only between Burmese men and
women, but also between the Burmese
students, and students in the United States,
Malaya, India, and Puerto Rico (U.S.A.)
The evidence seems to indicate a relatively
stable family system in Burma, as far, at
least as these data are concerned. In
general, in much of the larger cross cultural
family study, of which this report is a part,
the men of all of the cultures seem to share
with each other some of the same differ-
ences with the women of their own cultures.
Some of these male-female differences
might be roughly classified as a part of the
“battle of the sexes" which apparently has
a universal or international character.
This aspect of the larger study is as yet to
be fully analyzed and reported on.
