Public Finance and Public Policy in Burma, 1870-1940.*
1. Introduction.
U Aye Hlaing.*
This is a sequel to the article
"Trends of Economic Growth and Inco-
me Distribution in Burma, 1870-1940",
which appeared in part I of volume XLVII
of the Journal of the Burma Research
Society. In that article I have mentioned
that the economic expansion in Burma
during that period took place in certain
favourable conditions such as absence of
restrictive customs and traditions, a high
degree of geographic mobility of labour,
continual imp.ovement in both the extor-
nal and internal terms of trade, and
absence of population explosion with
economic growth. Despite these favoura-
ble factors Burma had failed to capitalise
on this economic expansion to achieve a
sustained economic growth. At the end
of the period of nearly three quarters of
a century of economic expansion · Burma
remained an underdeveloped economy.
Towards the end of the period the leading
sector of the economy was in a depressed
condition, with the majority of the agri-
culturists in serious economic troubles.
They were either unable or uninterested
in making significant investment to imp-
rove the agricultural productivity. Nor
was there significant investment in other
sectors, excepting in the mining industry,
to provide outlets for making the neces-
sary adjustments to the changing condi-
tion in agriculture. Instead of a tendency
to correct this deficiency of investment
in Burma, there was in fact an outflow
of capital for investment elsewhere.
I have suggested that this pattern of
economic development was a result of
applying the laissez faire, free trade and
**
open-door policies to a situation where
such economic policies worked in a diffe-
rent way from that which was experien-
ced in Britain itself. Some of the local
British administrators had foreseen, at
an early stage, the impending dangers
of the particular course of eco-
nomic and social development in
Burma. However, until 1937 the seat of
the authority with the power to make the
final decisions on legislation and financial
matters was located in India and in Eng-
land. This remoteness of the ultimate
authority made it ignorant of the actual
conditions and prevented the "local"
administration from taking any effective
action to prevent the dangerous outcomes
of economic stagnation and social disin-
tegration reflected in a series of racial
riots and open rebellion against the Go-
vernment towards the end of the period.
It is usual, but not sufficient, to explain
this pattern of economic expansion as an
effect of colonialism. It is necessary to
study it in more detail. In this paper we
therefore propose to consider the public
policy and public finance in Buema dur-
ing the period of this study.

3. Public Finance: (a) Revenue.
The relationship between the Central
Government in India and the "Local”
Government in Burma we have described
had far reaching economic consequences. The
local government which was in touch with
the local conditions and which knew the
problems and the needs of the area did not
have the power to introduce the necessary
legislation; nor could it raise the necessary
financial resources, either through taxes or
loans, to develop the needed services and
social capital. The level of taxation and
changes therein were determined by the
Central Government, which in turn had
only limited financial autonomv³. The gene-
ral directive of taxation, in accordance with
the laissez faire policy, was "not to impose
more taxes than was necessary for the
maintenance of law and order10. Changes
in the rates of taxes were made with referen-
ce to this general policy and to the needs
of the Central Goverment in India. For
example, free trade policy requires absence
of customs duties and therefore in 1882
these were abolished with the exception of
duties on arms and liquor¹¹. On the other
hand, the need to retain this source of
finance to develop the much needed social
overhead facilities and welfare services did
not get sufficient attention. When these
were re-imposed in 1894 it was because
of the financial difficulties of the Central
Government arising out of the fall in the
value of rupee from 2sh. to the rupee to
Ish.2d. in 1892 as this decline greatly in-
creased the burden of 'home charges". 12 At
the same time to safe-guard the interest of
the Lancashire cotton manufacturers a
countervailing Excise Duty of 5%, subse-
quently reduced to 3%. was imposed on
the domestic textiles manufactured in fac-
tories. The general rate of customs duties
on imports remained at a relatively low
level of 5% ad valorem until 1916, when
it was raised to 7%. again because of the
increased budget deficit of the Central Go-
vernment during the First World War. The
financial difficulties and the minor depression
of 1920 led to the doubling of the general
rate from 7% to 15% in 1922. But by
that time, imports were divided into six
classes for the purpose of customs assess.
ment, classified on the basis of different
degrees of necessities and luxuries. and also
the principle of protection had been
accepted.13
