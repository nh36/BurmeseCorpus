Introduction
Hermann and Magarete Janzen **
It is the intention of this article to
give an example of Grammar Analysis
from an indigenous but not previously
analysed language of Burma.
The method employed in this approach
is taken from different schools of Descriptive
Linguistics, which have been found helpful
in the analysis of many previously unknown
and unanalysed languages in South Ame-
rica, Africa and Asia.
The underlying principle is to describe
any language in terms which fit its own
structure and as far as possible avoiding
categories which are not relevant for that
particular language.
and
The outcome of such a procedure
may be, that some terms may be missing
in the description of a language which
are quite common, especially in Classical
Grammar. For instance there may be no
tenses such as present, past or future
used in one language. In the phonology
there may be certain sounds missing, which
are found in classical languages,
sounds (phonemes) may be present which
are not common in previously known
languages. Though in many cases certain
phenomena in a language must be explained
in terms known to the reader, it must be
born in mind, that these terms are not
necessarily identical with those applied for
other languages. For instance, if the term
"Plural" is mentioned it may not have
the same meaning as commonly thought
of, signifying more than one item, but
there may be a three member-system, sin-
gular, dual and plural. Here plural means
that there are not one or two items invol-
ved, but many, certainly more than two.
So every term must be assessed in rela-
tionship to the whole system of the par-
ticular language described. Even in lan-
guages which belong to the same language
family certain systems have changed more
or less rapidly throughout the history of
one language, and therefore more or less
fixed forms of systems are found in each
language at a certain time. So for instance,
the case system of the noun is more
less developed in different Indogermanic
Languages and therefore forms distinctly
different systems in each of these languages.
or
A common mistake in Grammar has
been to describe a language in terms of
the system of another language which is
quite different, but which has been gene-
rally regarded as "superior" or "more
systematic". e.g. Greek, Latin, Sanskrit
and Pali have been regarded as so superior
to other languages that in many languages
their systems have been adopted without
question and without regard for the lan-
guage to which they have been applied.
The descriptive linguist is wary of adopting
other systems, and also the reader of a
description has to beware of comparison,
assumption and interpretation of some
items of a system which may seem to
correspond to something he knows from
his own language or from languages which
he has learned.
In the hundreds of languages which
have been analysed by descriptive methods
in this century some general principles
have been found, which may apply to any
new language which is to be analysed.

The model employed in this analysis
called the Syntagmatic Model" or simply
"Structure-Function" model. This model
is a hirarchical model which has been
developed by experience in analysing
languages. Insights from other descriptive
grammar models, especially the tagmemic
and systemic grammar, have been applied
in developing this mod 1 with the aim of
keeping the model as simple and at the
same time as useful as possible.
The term which are used in this
model are used in a similar, but not
always identical way as in the other models
above mentioned. The term “level” has
been adopted from tagmemics and there-
fore does not correspond to the term
"level" as used by Halliday in this
Systemic Grammar Model. The terms
"Unit", ',Class", "Type" come close to
the usage of the same terms in Systemic
Grammar. The term “Syntagmatic feature”
is unique in the Syntagmatic model. It
is used as a means of describing gram-
matic relationships, and by this means

II. 1. 2. Unit, Class, Type
The unit which operates on a given
level bears the same name as its level. So
there may be morphemes, words, phrases,
clauses and sentences, which are units
operating on their respective levels. Every
unit has a certain s ructure and a specific
function. A unit functions as an element
in the structure of the unit of the level
above, and the structural elements of a
unit consist of units from the level below.
Classes of units are defined according
to their external function as an element on
the next level above in the hierarchy. So
there may be a verbal phrase class function-
ing as the Nucleus (Head) element of the
cluse. Therefore a class of units consists
of two or more units which function at
the same place in the structure of the unit
on the level above. The criterion for units
becoming members of one class is their
paradigmatic substitutability.
e.g. in the clause "the student learns well”.
the phrase "learns well" could be replaced
by the phrase: "stays idle". Both phrases
are verb phrases, functioning at the same
place in the clause, and therefore belong
to the same class, the verbal phrase class.

Such structural groupings into types
may either parallel the functional groupings
into classes or cut across them.
Whenever there are restrictions of
substitutability of members of one functio-
nal class, this class is divided into subclasses.
Minor structural differences of types
constitute subtypes. These subtypes are
distinguished and marked by syntagmatic
features. Types and subtypes or classes
and subclasses may crosscut each other
and form an interlocking system, which
may be demonstrated in a matrix. (see
e.g. types and subtypes of Palaung clauses.)
II. 1.3. System
If there are two or more subclasses
of units which function alternatively as
choice classes at a given place in the
structure they from a system If the number
of items is limited they form a system
within a closed-class. If different larger.
sets of items appear mutually exclusively
at one place in structure they form a
system of different subclasses (See examples
from Pale below). The minimum repuire-
ment for establishing a system are two
mutually exclusive items. The grammati-
cal value or meaning of one member in a
system is contrastive to the whole sum of
members in that particular system. In
English the singular and plur I forms of
nouns form a system with a twofold con-

threefold contrast.

II. 1.4. Syntagmatic features.
In addition to the structure of a
unit which consists of a certain number
of elements, the relationships of these
elements to one another within that unit
are of vital importance. The elements
may have a certain order, there may be
possible shifts of elements to the front
or to the middle or end in certain cases,
some of the elements are optional and
some obligatory, some elements are closely
related to one another and some are
more loosely affiliated to each other.
There may be phonetic and phonological
markers such as intonation and certain
sound differences, differences of pitch or
tone, or there may be certain particles,
which are characteristic of one type of
unit on a given level. All these are
syntagmatic features, which are to be
described on each level. Some of these
features are overtly marked, by means of
a morpheme, word etc. Some are marked
in current orthographies, and others have
to be described in writion statements. In
English the three clauses: "You go." "You
go?" and "You go!" have a distinctive
syntagmatic feature in each clause. : level
intonation, rising intonation, and a falling
stressed intonation, which are respectively
marked by full stop (.) quession mark (?)
and imparative marker (!)
II. 1.5. Construct.
Normally a unit functions at the next
level above. If it is functioning on a
lower level or feeds back at the same
level it is called a construct. eg. in the
complex clause: "Anybody who visits me
is welcome", the part "who visits me"
is a clause functioning as the modifier of
the subject-phrase, in which "anybody"
51
is the nucleus. Thus the clause functions
on the level belows as a part of the
phrase. In the case of feedback on the
same level one may give the following
example: "The rich man and the poor
people shared their property." Here "the
poor people" is a noun phrase in itself
which is functioning as an adj nct of
"the rich man," and so becomes the part
of another noun phrase, which functions
as subject in the clause.

The grammar description by means
of the Syntagmatic Model as used in this
article will be presented mainly by a writ-

function of a unit on its respective level
as well as the relevant syntagmatic features
of units. Groupings in classes and types
as well as existing systems will be stated.
To highlight certain aspects of the descrip-
tion different forms of presentation can be
employed, in order to give a quicker and
clearer insight into the language described
or into a certain point of the description.
The different forms of presentation are:
(1) the linguistic formula (adopted
from tagmemics).
(2) the box diagram.
(3) the linguistic matrix.
(4) the summarising chart.

IV. Description of Pale Clauses
and Phrases.
Clauses and Phrases of the Pale
language are units which function at
Clause and Phrase level respectively.
There are altogether 6 levels in the gram-
matical hierarchy of the Pale language:
Discourse level
Sentence level
Clause level
Phrase level
Word level
Morpheme level
The highest level is the Discourse level
which can only be described in terms of
its structural markers, which are different
syntagmatic features. Since the Discourse
Level does not function in a higher level
structure but in free context there is no
functional statement possible.
The lowest level in the grammatical
hierachy is the Morpheme level. Only a
few word classes have further structure,
mainly the noun-word class which can
form compounds and the verb-class which
can have a few prefixes. Thus the Mor-
pheme Level requires little discussion, and
most of the words consist of a single
morpheme.
