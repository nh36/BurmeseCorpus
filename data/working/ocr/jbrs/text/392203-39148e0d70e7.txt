Dr. Sundrum has succeeded in giving a very useful description,
with statistical illustrations, of the seasonal characteristics of the
Burmese economy. Many fruitful suggestions are also contained in
his paper on the manner in which such knowledge of the Burmese
economy can be used in the formulation of economic policy.
For example, he mentions that information on seasonality
fish imports and fish prices may be used in any stabilisa-
tion programme of the cost of living during the course of the year;
knowledge of the seasonal pattern of exports and imports may be used
in formulating trade polices and also used in any attempt at a more
efficient use of available shipping and of exchange reserves.

Dr. Sundrum, however, has left out two very important economic
series which are probably also subject to seasonal fluctuations; - (a)
Government receipts and expenditure, and (b) the balance of payments
deficit or surplus on current account. From an economic policy point
of view, these two series are more important than many of the series
covered in the paper. This is so, because while governments at times
are unable to influence decisions of the private sector, they are always
able to take decisions affecting the public sector especially on the
collection of revenues and the spending of money. The close relation-
ship between government budgets and fluctuations in the balance of
payments, especially in Burma, makes it essential that the seasonal
needs for foreign exchange be studied carefully.

mentioned that the wholesale price index of agricultural products is an

It is rather peculiar that the consumers' price index in Burma
seems more sensitive than the wholesale agricultural price index. An
unsatisfactory attempt (as shown below) has been made to partly
explain this by variations in the price of fish. It is hoped that a more
comprehensive explanation of this phenomena will be available at some
future data. As regards the fish price movement, we are not told the
exact weight assigned to fish and fish products in the family budget,
so that we cannot really judge the extent to which the movement in the
consumer price index is due to movements in the price of fish.
As
shown below, the influence does not seem to be great. The statement
in the paper that the behaviour of fish prices parallels very closely the
movement in the consumer price index, also seems to be somewhat
exaggerated. It is true that both the peaks of the consumer price
index and of the fish price index occur in July, but apart from this the
shapes of the two curves are somewhat different. The consumer price
index has more or less a plateau curving over four months, while the
fish price index has a sharp peak. The sharp rise in fish prices by
about 15-20 per cent from May to June does increase the cost of
fiving index by about 10 per cent but the sharp fall in the fish prices
from July to September does not cause much of a fall in the consumer
price index until October. On the whole it seems that the fluctuations
in fish prices account for only a small part of the movement in the
consumer price index.

The section on foreign trade has comparisons between pre-war and
post-war, but the usefulness of the section would be increased if data
on seasonal fluctuations in pre-depression years were made available.
The use of the trade data on a value basis (in current prices) has both
advantages and disadvantages. The main disadvantage is that the
great seasonality in quantum exports of agricultural products especially
of rice, tends to be lost in the value data. This is the result of the
seasonality in agricultural prices (probably greater in pre-war years)
especially of rice, which are lower in the main exporting season and

Dr. Sundrum has posed a very difficult question, namely as to
why there is a dip in the value index of both the exports and imports.
in April-a dip which was very pronounced in pre-war years but not
so pronounced in postwar. A proper answer can only be obtained by
checking the seasonal characteristics of the key export commodities
such as rice, timber and petroleum, and the key import commodities
such as textiles, iron and steel etc. I would, however, venture to
throw out the following possible explanations. In pre-war, the fiscal
year was from April to March and the business community was more
alive to the possible effects of changes in export and import duties on
its price quotations and on the trade position. These facts may have
had something to do with the April dip in exports and imports-in
March, orders for imports would tend not to be placed and offers for
exports not made, 3 pending the new customs duties which may be
enforced. Since in pre-war there was only about a month's time lag
between most orders and shipments arrivals, a decline in contracts
made in March would show in the shipments and arrivals of April.
Another possible explanation for the dip in April (and also in
October) may be found in the explanation which Dr. Sundrum has
given for the seasonal characteristics in bank clearings: that is to say
he the change wer from the busy to the slack season
sides to the busy season which occurs around April and
October. It should be noted that there are dips occurring also around
September or October in both pre-war and post-war years.
It is doubtful whether there has been a permanent shift in the
seasonal pattern of imports and exports and I for one would like to
reserve my position. The years 1949-1954 have been abnormal years,

In the section on transport Dr. Sundrum, without adequate
evidence, draws the conclusion that domestic trade does not seem to
enter very significantly into railway activity. Only a detailed analysis
of the commodities transported between different points can reveal
whether this is true. It is not generally realised that not all of the
rice which is handled by the railways is for export. I doubt also
whether one can draw the conclusion that the seasonal fall in currency
in circulation indicates that the rural population in the agriculture
sector follow a fairly rigid time schedule in their economic dealings
with the rest of the economy. There may be a rigid time schedule of
payments by the rural sector, but the seasonal decline in currency .in.
circulation is not due to this; the decline in currency in circulation is
the result of three factors, namely balance of payments deficits, excess
of government revenue over government expenditure and the
extinguishing of bank credit by borrowers (traders etc., but not
agriculturists) paying back their loans. As regards bank clearings, I
feel that an analysis of pre-war data would probably reveal seasonal
characteristics which could not be found in post-war years, because of
the abnormal post-war situation. Part of the difficulty in post-war
bank clearings, may have been in the choice of statistics-one does
not know whether the statistics Dr. Sundrum has used are inclusive
or exclusive of government clearings.
