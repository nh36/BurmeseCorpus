FISHERIES IN BURMA. 1
by
Sithu U Khin ²
Burma holds interesting fields of study for students in the several
departments of arts and science. Since the days of Marco Polo a fairly
large number of people with a thirst for knowledge or adventure have
been attracted towards the malarial swamps and the tiger-infested
hill-jungles of the Irrawaddy valley. There have been geographers and
archaeologists, big-game hunters and orchid hunters, anthropologists
and butterfly chasers. To the several aspects from which the country
had already been studied was recently added, much to the
chagrin of the inhabitants, the bomber eye-view approach of the
war-time cartographer. The accumulated knowledge from these various
enterprizes should easily provide sufficient material for a sizeable
Enclyclopedia Burmanica, and if such a work should materialize in the
future one would probably find that it was not lacking entirely in
something of ichthiological interest.
We cannot say for sure if the author of the fish article in that
future publication might successfully delve into ancient history to find
reference to Burmese fishes in the writings of the early Hindu colonisers,
or of the Arabian or Portuguese seafaring adventurers; but he is bound
to mention Dr. Edward Blyth's report published in 1860 on the fish
specimens collected from the Sittang River System by Major Berdmore.
This report was probably the first attempt at a systematic study of the
hydrofauna of this country. This modest beginning was overshadowed.
by Major (afterwards Sir) Francis Day's epoch-making studies of
Burmese fish life, which covered not only the taxonomic but the
ecological aspects as well. His "Marine Fisheries of Burma" was first
published in 1869, to be followed in 1873 by the "Report on the Fresh
Water Fish and Fisheries of India and Burma ". Major Day was at that
time the Inspector-General of Fisheries in India and he visited Burma in
his official capacity to investigate the rich revenue-earning fishery
resources of the new British territories across the Bay. Inspite of the
narrow objectives of his official tour, the results of his studies were of
no mean academic interest and even to this day the Taxonomy of
Burmese fishes has not departed materially from Day's original
classification.

Signor Leonardo Fea's visit a few years after was, on the other
hand, of purely academic purpose. He was making extensive flora
and fauna collections in this country on behalf of the Natural History
Museum of Geneva. Every biology student knows that a taxonomic
study of Burmese fishes is not complete without frequent references to
Dr. D. Vinciguerra, who worked on the fish section of the Fea
collection.
Vinciguerra's
work was followed soon after by Dr.
G. A. Boulenger's "List of fishes collected by Mr. E. W. Oats in the
Shan States and presented by him to the British Museum". Then in
1917 Dr. Anondale of the Zoological Survey of India conducted a
servey of the aquatic life of the Inle Lake, and this visit marked the
beginning of a sustained interest in the Burmese waters shown by that
world-famous scientific service of the Indian Government. Dr.
Chowdhury of that service was working about the same time on
specimens collected by Dr. Murray Stuart from the mountain streams
of Khamti Long. In 1926 a Zoological Survey of India expedition
under Dr. Chopra visited the Indawgyi area and the report on its fish
collection by Drs. Prasad and Mukerji appeared 3 years afterwards.
By this time a large collection of Burmese fishes had accumulated in the
Calcutta Museum and a number of occasional reports on these by
Dr. Hora were published from time to time. Dr. Mukerji's report on
the fish collection made by Lt.-Col. Burton from the tributary streams
of the Malika River in 1930 was also of much interest. The year 1935
witnessed the arrival of the Verney-Hopwood Upper Chindwin Expedi-
tion organised by the American Museum of Natural History. Part of
the fish collection of this expedition was worked on by Hora and Misra
and their report appeared in 1941. Unfortunately events of that
year in this part of the world were to associate this country's name
for some years to come with expeditionary forces rather than with
scientific expeditions. Apart from the slight interest shown
by the Biology Department of the Rangoon University there has been
no serious attempt to study the fish life of this country since the war.
We may note that these spurts of interest in Burmese ichthiology,
spreading over a period of about one century, came almost exclusively
from outside the country. There had been, no doubt, a few private
individuals within the country-more likely a British military or, civil
officer in an outstation than anybody else-who collected fish specimens
as a hobby and sent these to the British Museum or the museums in

India or Malaya for the naming of species. There was also a strong
indication that Dr. Anandale's visit to the Inle Lake was at the
invitation of the Burma Government initiated personally by
H.E. Sir Harcourt Butler. Apart from these insignificant instances the
contribution of the Government and people of this country towards the
advancement of Burmese ichthiology was almost nil. But then it was
not that fish studies just failed to draw sufficient interest of an otherwise
culturally alert people; the dearth of scientific work on fish life was
merely a part of the general story of cultural sterility of that period in
the national history.
Fish as a biological specimen might have failed to whet the
intellectual appetite of the educated Burmese, but fish as a culinary
item-especially when improved upon by the combined effect of human
artifice and bacteriological process-never fails to tickle the Burmese
palate. We may disagree on politics or we may disapprove of the
colour of a man's shirt or the texture of a woman's aingyi material,
but it is always a unanimous vote on the subject of ngapi. From this
national predilection in conjunction with the existence within the
national boundaries of certain favourable hydrographical features have
emerged a few salient facts in the economic life of this nation. These
may be briefly outlined as follows-
(i) Burma possesses fairly rich fishery resources, which have
long since been exploited on a considerable scale; the
annual production in this branch of industry has never
been correctly estimated, but judging from the size of
the revenue from this source it must be fairly big.
(Pre-war fiishery revenue was in the region of Rs. 24
lakhs per annum).
(ii) Fishing is an important rural industry in certain districts
and also provides subsidiary occupation to agriculturists
in these as well as in the other districts of Lower Burma.
(iii) Fish is such an important item of the Burmese dietary
that the annual catch is always far too small for the
requirements of the national larder. Hence the huge
annual import valued at Rs. 75 lakhs pre-war.
The economic importance of fish in this country was not lost sight
of by the pre-war administration. Although there had been hardly any
academic interest in fish-life within the country, there was a long

history of official interest in the subject of conservation of this rich
revenue-earning resource. Dr. Day's visit was specifically for studying
this aspect of the Burmese fishing industry. His recommendations
resulted in the first Burma Fisheries Act (of 1875) which
sought to minimise the enormous destruction of fish fry by
restricting the size of mesh in nets and other fishing implements, and by
prohibition, in certain cases, of the method of catching by bunding
and ladling. The Act marked the beginning of a system of close state
supervision for privately operated fishing enterprizes. In 1880
F. L. Seaton, a senior Revenue Settlement Officer, was detailed to
conduct the survey and demarcation of the delta fisheries with an eye
on proper adjustment of interests between fishing and crop-husbandry.
Fishery matters used to be given special attention in settlement
operations for agricultural land. In the settlement reports references to
the clash of interests between fisheries and agriculture were often made.
These emphasised the complicated nature of the problems involved in
fishery administration. Col. Maxwell was therefore placed on special
duty in 1895-96 to conduct a thorough investigation of the problems.
After two years work he submitted his report containing recommenda-
tions for certain fundamental changes in the existing fishery laws and
administrative procedure. His proposed rules regarding the working
conditions of fisheries were directed towards:―
(a) conservation of fishery resources,
-
(b) preventing the deterioration of fisheries by silting, and
(c) modifying inter-relations of fishery lessees.
The reforms advocated by him were given practical effect in the
present Burma Fishery Act (Act III of 1905) and the Rules and
Directions thereunder.
Official opinion seemed to be divided regarding the working of
this Act, and unfavourable criticism came forward from many quarters.
Probably it was this lack of agreement on Maxwell Reforms that led to
the appointment in 1928 of Mr. A. M. Bown as a Special Fishery
Settlement Officer. His report based on operations conducted in the
Yandoon Subdivision of Ma-u-bin District was submitted the following
year. In it the Maxwell Reforms in all their aspects were subjected to
much searching criticism. Many recommendations were also put
forward. It is not known how far Government have given practical
effect to these in the district administration of fisheries. So fas as
the written fishery laws are concerned these have remained unaffected.

Burma was the first province in India to make determined attempts
to conserve these resources through legislative action and closely
directed administrative measures. It was only in 1897 that the Indian
Fisheries Act was passed, enabling Provincial Governments, excluding
Burma, to make rules for the effective conservation of their fisheries.
This initiative of the Central Government did not seem to have led to
any important active measures on the part of the Provincial
Governments for a considerable number of years. Even to-day the Act
still remains a dead letter in many provinces.
Burma's first Fisheries Act was passed in 1875, nearly a quarter
century earlier than the All-India Act. Since then active measures
have been taken by Government in the interest of conservation and
these have been under constant revision in the light of subsequent
experience. The laudable intentions are there, but whether these
have produced the intended result or not has always remained a
debatable point, simply because the creation of a means whereby we
may safely judge the effectiveness of the measures has been over-
looked. Some say that there has been a progressive depletion of
our fishery resources and argue in favour of more stringent control
measures, while others believe that the Irrawaddy is an inexhaustible
reservoir of fish supply irrespective of any man-made laws and that
these laws and regulations have produced no more results than the
undue harassment of the people. The views of the opposing camps
are based on general observations often vitiated by personal prejudices
and pre-concieved ideas rather than on sound deductions from
reliable scientific and statistical data. If there had been a technical
department charged with the scientific study of fishery problems and a
systematic collection of statistics, we would have had a means of
judging decisively whether our bold experimental measures in fishery
legislation have produced the desired results or not.
The Post-war Government of Burma has realised that a fishery
policy which does not go far beyond the mere conservation of
resources for revenue aims is no longer adequate. The importance
of fish in the national dietary, of fishing in the rural economy and
the size of annual fish imports call for a broadening of Government's
policy regarding fishery matters. Since National Independence the
realisation has grown with greater force than ever that it should be
a positive responsibility of the government to foster expansion of the
fishing industry not merely to improve the revenue position of the
