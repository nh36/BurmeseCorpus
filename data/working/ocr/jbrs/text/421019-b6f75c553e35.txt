Let me tell you briefly how the project came about. Five or six
years ago, when I was in England lecturing at the London School of
Oriental Studies, I had a lot to do with Dr. Henderson of the Phonetics

Why not take a holiday from lecturing, and join me in a language-tour

Rector had been good enough to recall me to my old University, I
received a letter from Dr. Henderson. She wanted to come to Burma to
check her dictionary of Bwè Karen (which, I hope, will soon be pub-

With your permission, Sir, I will devote the middle part of this
lecture to giving you, very briefly, the gist of my historical conclusions.
As a historian, I look at language largely from that angle. Few of the
hill-peoples of Burma have a written history going back more than a
century; so apart from what anthropometry and anthropology can tell
us, our one reliable source about their ancient past, unless they figure
in Chinese sources, must be sought in launguage-comparison. Each
Chin dialect has its own peculiarities, which are valuable in themselves.
But each has also a lot in commn with its neighbours. I concentrate
entirely on this latter aspect-the Common element, as this tells one
more about the history of the people as a whole.

in my seven dialects, I found only about 700 words common to three

At my London Seminar I often met Mr. J. P. Mills, late President
of the Anthropological Institute of Great Britian and Ireland,
the great authority on the Nagas and other hill-peoples of Assam. He
rather surprised me by his attitude to the Chins, or Kukis as they call
them on the Assam side. When campared with the ancient Naga
cultures, he would speak of Chin culture, “mithan-culture" as he
sometimes called it, as a pretty recent thing. It may well be so on the
Assam side; but on our side there are not wanting signs of Chin anti-
quity megaliths in the north; the burning of the dead in the south;
face-tattooing of women in the centre-to mention only three of
them. There are of course plenty of Chins on the Indian and
Pakistani sides of the border - almost, if not quite, as many in Assam,
Manipur and Tripura (India), and in Chittagong Hill Tracts (Pakistan)
as there are in Burma. But Chin movement during the historical
period seems to have been more from east to west, than from north to
south. Thanks to the courtesy of the Indian and Pakistani Embassies
in Rangoon, I have had access to the totals in these countries according
to their 1951 Census Reports. If we exclude the Manipuries or
Meitheis, who total nearly half a million in India, and speak a language
distantly related to Chin, the Kuki-Chin group in India totals 320,739
speakers in India, 2/3 of them in Assam, 1/4 in Manipur, and 1/12 in
Tripura. Half of this total, 163,588, are Lushai.
The Pakistan Census is not clear about languages, and the races,
too, are probably more mixed. The totals of Chin speakers are much
less - perhaps no more than 35,000. But they include peoples with very
old languages, probably everlaid with Chin: the Murang whom we call
Mro in Arakan, and the “Khyeng" (? Khyan) said to be, like the Mro,
the original inhabitants of the Chittagong Hills, but to have " ' negroid
features and often curly hair.

Most Chin dialects still contain a few words of Austric, chiefly
Austroasiatic in origin. They must be ancient, for they fit almost
perfectly into Chin tonal patterns. Here are some of them :-
Dog-ui-tsau (ui is Tibeto-Burman; tsau is Austronesian, and very
likely Polynesian too). Beans; be. Horse: sc ai or s'i. Bird c'im.
s'im. Mole: bwi. Vulture: lang ta. Widowed: hmei, hnei. In the first
millenium B. C., before Tibeto-Burman speakers came south into
Burma, the peoples here spoke Austroasiatic tongues. Of these, Khasi
is still spoken west of Manipur; Palaung, Riang and Wa in the Shan
States: Mon in Lower Burma. It was Naga, Sak and Chin invasions
which split these peoples far apart: but they have left their traces
in the younger languages.
Three western words at least are ancient and widespread in Chin:-
(i) t'ilng or s'ilng, which is the same word as Burmese qε: or Ginger,
an old western culture-word. (ii) Kalā, Indians, from Sanskrit kula-
putra, caste-people. Fitting, as it does, the Chin tone-pattern, the word
cannot be a modern borrowing from Burmese: the Pagan Burman may
well have borrowed it from Chin. (iii) tangka, Burmese 5818, Coin,
Silver, the old Gupta coin of Classical Sanskrit. Gupta expansion in
Assam, and doubtless the coming of its coinage, took place in the
middle of the 4th. century A. D. By then, or not much later, the Chins
were probably in the Hukong Valley and the Upper Chindwin. See

What happened after the fall of Pagan? - The main event in the
north of Burma was the desperate effort of the Shans, who had been
driven south from below Tali by the Mongols, either to recover
Southern Yünnan or else to conquer Burma, as their brothers had
conquered Thailand. They overran the north of Burma, east and west
of the Irawady, down to the walls of Myedu and Tagaung. In 1364
they sacked the twin capitals of Sagaing and Pinya. They had long
wars with the Ming dynasty of China. In 1449 Wang Chi (the cacsūkrī
Chan Swe of the inscriptions) 2 finally drove them west of the Irawady,
captured their western capital Mohnyin, and set up a Chinese inscrip-
tion on the Irawady bank:- "When this stone rots, and this river
dries up, then you may cross !".
These western Shans had now no room to live in or expand.
During the next century the Ahome (it is the same word as Assam,
Shan, Siam, etc.) pushed north over the Patkoi Range and conquered
Assam. The Shans also did their best to conquer Ava, and finally did

As for K’umi, my nu aber of common entries is as small as that
for Asho, and 'the uncertainty still greater. Yet the distance of
Kaletwa from the centre (say Falam) is far less: only half of that from
Falam to Layshi in the north, only a third of that from Falam to
Sandoway in the south. And the hills between Falam and Kaletwa are
supposed to be entirely peopled by Chins, mostly Lakher (Mărā), with
no admixture of Nagas as in the north, nor of Burmans as in the south.
Yet Xôngsai is pure Chin, and Asho essentially so; whereas K'umi,
though largely Chin in its vocabulary, contains other elements (see
Table IV). Some of these non-Chin elements recur in Mro, a
mysterious old North Arakan language, which Mr. Robert Shafer has
analysed in Vol. XXXI of the J.B.R.S. Mro, like K’umi, is overlaid
with Chin; but beneath the top Chin layer he finds an extinct non - Chin
Tibeto-Burman layer, and below that again an element pre-Tibeto-
Burman. If this is true of Mro-and I cannot deny it-it is true also
of K'umi. The 1931 Census Report says, in effeet, that the Chin Hills
are 96 per cent pure Chin. This may be true of the western ranges,
but it is not true of the eastern. In language one nearly always builds.
on others' foundations; and in these secluded mountains, where a tribe
can live a thousand years and not be noticed, history is buried deep in
language as surely as it is beneath the soil of Sri Kṣetra.
Let me conclude with a few general remarks about Chin and its
connections.
TONES. European writers on Chin have nearly all ignored the
tones. I find them so vital to the historical study of the language that
I base the whole of my arrangement of Tables A and B on the 3 (or 5)
Tone-Patterns which permeate and unite the whole language from north
to south Table I is a key, with instances, to the tones in all dialects I
have studied. If any of my Chin hearers can discover a mistake in
them, please point it out to me; for to many of these languages I could
only devote two sessions. On this chart I give, with instances, the 3 (or
5) Tone-Patterns running throughout Chin. From it I infer that there
were originally 3 basic tones: Tone I confined to non-stopped syllables,
ending either in open vowels or nasals; Tones II and III either unstopped
or stopped, Ila and IIb, IIIa and IIIb. IIb and IIIb are of recent origin,
reserved for short and long stopped syllables. The ultimate basis of

There are also fixed tones in Burmese, as old at any rate as the
Pagan dynasty. There are also 6 (or 8) Tonal Patterns in Karen, also
shown on this chart. But when one compares Chin with Burmese and
Karen, one finds that though the vocabulary is often close, the Tone-
Patterns do not closely correspond. So one important check on word-
identification in Table C is missing. The basis of connection here is
merely similarity of Initials and Finals and of meanings. Common to
all these languages, however, is this sharp tonal distinction between
Stopped and Open Finals, the latter including Nasals.
FINALS. A remarkable feature of Chin is its wealth of final
consonants. These are shown on Table II-120 at least in Central Chin,
to go no further than my 700 words. There are probably many more
possibilities, especially in Haka. Contrast these with the poverty of
modern spoken Burmese, with only 23 possible fiinals other than open
vowels. Persons who would substitute Burmese script for the present
Roman system of writing Chin, would do well to ponder this. The
present Roman system is quite adequate except in the marking of tones.
To adopt Burmese script-with only one symbol for five or six distinct
Chin sounds-could only precipitate decay in this fine language.
How easy it is for decay to set in, I heard with my own ears. Dr.
Henderson and I spent one exciting day recording, through an inter-
preter, the dialects of four road-coolies who came from the remote
hills south of Haka-Zotung, Lawt'vɛ, Mără, and Maṭupi. We only
had time to record 128 words. In Mără about 8 stages south of Haka,
I find in my recordings only one final-m, one final-n, and one final-i.
If I were able to check these, very likely I should have to reject all
three, and conclude that of all the 120 or more final consonants in
Haka, none but the glottal stop survives in Miram 8 stages south of it.
When experts tell us that there is no such thing as 'decay in language,
but only 'change', I wish they would visit such lively places as Tiddim,
Falam and Haka, with their eager schools and arts and educational
facilities, and then go south and see what happens when people become
really illiterate. Such ‘changes', alas have befallen most of Burma's
cultures throughout history; and at each revival the shattered language
of the past has taken a century or more to reconstruct, before
it can become again a vehicle for culture.

VERBS. I was brought up to regard Far Eastern languages
generally as (i)-Monosyllabic (consisting of words of one syllable):
(ii) Invariable (not modified by any inflexions); and (iii) Isolating
(destitute of Syntax). Chin is a language which disproves all three
statements. My own concern was Vocabulary, not Syntax; so I will
leave Dr. Henderson to disprove the third. I could not help coming up
against the second; for though I found it easy to fit Nouns into my
Tonal Patterns, I had much more difficulty with Verbs, until I began to
realise that the Chin Verb has normally two forms at least, often with
different tones, vowels and final consonants-just as different, in fact, a
'say' and 'said', or 'may' and 'might' in English. The subject is
a big one; for dialect differs from dialect, so far as I can judge on
short acquaintance. The central dialects (Lushai to Haka) seem to be
the richest in inflexions. Sometimes I found the Imperative gave the
basic tone; more often the Verbal Noun or Gerund. But all this
question of inflexions will require much more study than I was able to
give to it. In my lists I put a ring round all tone-numbers that do not
fit the patterns. Most of these are verbs.
PREFIXES.- The northern dialects (Xôngsai to Tedim and Ngɔ:n
are now mostly monosyllabic; but they were not originally so. There
are far more prefixes in the central dialects; but they usually mean
something, like Old Burmese prefixes, or like the radical in a Chinese
character, giving the generic class of an object. Thus s'a-is a prefix for
Animals, va, - for Birds, p'u, for Insects, etc. Many of these are found
also in Burmese and Karen. They are therefore old; but I doubt if they
are original. In some of the southern dialects, and especially in K'umi,
one finds lots of prefixes which seem to be euphonic and mean nothing
at all. Here the normal word is a dissyllable, not a monosyllable: the
accent nearly always is on the last syllable. All this, I suspect, points
to the fact that in Chin-also, no doubt, in Karen and Burmese-we are
dealing with the mere stumps of original words, their roots decayed,
their branches shorn; but this is too complex a theme to develop here.
