A THEORY OF THE RATE OF INTEREST FOR THE
BURMESE ECONOMY
by
Dr. Tun Thin, M.A., Ph. D. 2
Currently, in the field of theoretical economics, there are two
major theories of the rate of interest, namely the liquidity preference
theory and the loanable fund theory. The liquidity preference theory
states that the rate of interest is determined by the supply of money
and the demand for money. The loanable fund theory on the other
hand says that the rate of interest is determined by the supply of and
the demand for loanable funds.
The various rates of interest in Burma can be differentiated thus:
the commercial bank rate of interest, the black market rate of interest,
and the bond rate of interest. The rates of interest in these three credit
markets are different, though they have been more or less constant
since some time ago. The rate of interest charged by the commercial
banks is from 6 to 8 percent per annum, and the rate in the black
market from 30 to 150 percent per annum, while the rate in the bond
market is 3 percent per annum.
The main forces acting on these three markets are different for
each market, and hence a single formula cannot explain the rate of
interest in Burma.
The bank loans will be considered first. Those who are qualified
to borrow money from the commercial banks are first, people of good
standing, and second, people who can give security for their loars. The
security given may be of three types: merchandise, immoveable pro-
perty, and jewellery (gold and/or diamonds). Generally, immoveable
property and jewellery are accepted only by the Burmese national
banks. These banks charge the rate of interest of 12 to 15 percent per
annum (1%to 1% per month) for jewellery securities and 6 to 8 per-
cent (1% to 1% per month) for other securities. Two points may be
observed firstly, the rates of interest charged by these banks have
remained the same for a long time; secondly, the banks are under-

The demand for loans from these banks may be classified into two
categories. The first category would be loans for fixed investment, e.g.
for opening new factories, constructing apartment houses, etc. The
second category would be loans for circulating capital, e.g. for export,
import, trade purposes, for purchasing raw materials, input for
factories, etc.
Because of the fact that these loans are for income-earning
purposes, the rate of interest paid on these loans will be equal to or
less than the expected rate of return of the investments.
In the demand for fixed investment, though the potential marginal
efficiency of the capital of this investment is rather high, due to the
lack of technological and management experience the would-be
investors would calculate their expected returns very conservatively,
and as a result the expected rate of return is not too high (in figure 1,
it is assumed to be 8 per cent).

In the black market, the borrowers are people who are not
qualified to borrow from the commercial banks at a lower rate of
interest, such as for example, rural labourers and producers who need
capital every year because they cannot save enough, workers both in
urban and rural areas who have to borrow money for everyday
needs, and some business people who are not respectable enough to
borrow from the commercial banks. The demand for loans from these
people is somewhat inelastic. These are people who have to borrow
and who cannot borrow from anywhere else. This is a case of mono-
poly for the lenders, which was at its worst before the independence of
Burma, when about a hundred percent of the credit requirements of the
rural population, who were mostly agricultural workers, was provided
by the Chettyars and when the then British Government did not solve
the problem boldly.
The black market may be divided into two markets, one where
only secured loans are given, and the other where only unsecured loans
are given. In the black market for secured loans, the suppliers of
such loans are mostly Indians - Babus or Chettyars. In Rangoon
most of them are to be found on Mogul Street and they are mostly
jewellery merchants.

The suppliers of the black market capital are these who have
private capital and who may invest in buildings, land, or precious
metals, or carry on trade, but who will not invest in industries. They
are mostly Indians and Chinese living in Burma.
To have a supply curve of the loans of these people, the concept
of the "Assets preference schedule" will have to be introduced. The
definition of assets includes liquid assets, i.e. money, and other assets
like buildings, plots of land, and rare metals. As for money, these
people can either hold it or else lend it out. As for other assets, they
can hold them or else sell or pledge them and the proceeds may be used
for lending. Hence the choice for these people is either to hold or to
lend their assets. Hence a demand curve for holding assets may be
conceived of as follows:
First of all, the suppliers of funds in the black market would like
to hold liquid assets, i.e. money. It is assumed that the demand for
money by them is only for precautionary purpose and that it is there-
fore interest inelastic (curve AA' in figure 4). Then there is a
demand from those people for other assets such as gold and other
precious metals, land, houses, etc. The demand for holding these non-
liquid assets can be for three purposses, namely, transaction purposes,

Adding all these various demands for assets, we will have the
curve BCD as in figure 5. The difference between BCD and the curve
EE, which shows the existing assets, will be the supply of loans at
various rates of interest.
But this is still not the final supply of loans. The final supply of
loans is greater than this and will have to be explained. In this market
there has to be a pledge of jewellery and this works as follows: A
customer will bring in a piece of jewellery, the fair or market price of
which is assumed to be 100. The Chettyar will of course price it
lower, say at 95. Of this forced price, the Chettyar lends 80 percent.
Thus only 76, or 80 percent of 95 is lent. The Chettyar will of
course charge a rate of interest of 2 to 3% per month. He will also
take this jewellery to a commercial bank. A commercial bank will give
the same facilities to him as to any other customer and the result would
be this the jewellery, whose fair price is 100, would be priced by the
commercial bank at a lower price than the Chettyar's, say at 85. We
find that the Chettyar's margin of loan is 80 % of the fair price. The
commercial bank's margin is lower and is around 60 %, which is
60% of the fair price, so that only 51 is lent. Thus the Chettyar,
with the security he obtains, can borrow 51 from a commercial bank at
the low rate of interest of 1 percent per month. His original loan
was 76, but he borrows 51 and hence only 25 percent is his own money.
Thus there is a multiplier effect, and in this example it is three times.
-
It is to be noted here that the fair value and the margin given by
the Chettyars are higher than those of the commercial banks. They
can do this without very much added risk of capital loss, because of
vertical integration of business, their other business being buying and
selling jewellery, so that it is not at all difficult for them to get
proceeds from the jewellery equal to their forced value. Also we find
that because of the difference in the terms of loans offered by the
commercial banks and by the Chettyars, the demand for loans from
these two markets is different. With a given security, those who want
more loans than the commercial banks can offer will come to the latter
market. As seen above, they can get 76 from the Chettyars, but only
51 from the banks.

In the unsecured market, the percentage of Burmese lenders is
greater than in the secured market. Here the supply of loans is as
defined in the secured market. Because of the fact that there is no
security, the offer curve is not multiplied as in the secured market,
but rather, the supply curve is moved up because of the risk factor.
Assuming that the rate of default is 75 percent, the rate of interest will
then have to be adjusted so that the defaulted loans may be recouped.
Since only 25 percent of the amount of the loans will be recovered, the
interest charged will have to be three times the principal itself,
i.e. the rate of interest will be 300 percent.
The rate of interest in the secured and unsecured black market is
determined at the point where the supply and demand curves meet.
The demand for these loans may be considered as somewhat inelastic.
Considering these facts, one can see that the rate of interest in
the unsecured market will be higher than in the secured market.
