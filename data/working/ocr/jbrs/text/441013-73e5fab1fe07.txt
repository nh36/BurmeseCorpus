I Introduction
A MINERAL OCCURANCE, suspected to be the emerald variety of beryl, has been
known to exist in an unmapped area of the Kayah State situated between the Pawn Chaung
and Salween rivers. At the request of the State Government, the authors undertook to
traverse the area in order to examine the reported mineral occurrence and to prepare a
geological map of the major rock types.
The traverse was carried out during the last week in May 1960 at the commence-
ment of the monsoon and was undertaken on foot and on horseback. The route taken is
shown on the map (Fig. 1) which has been traced from the Burma, 4 miles to 1 inch map,
Sheet 94F. The geology around Loikaw and in the vicinity of Namphe and Bawlake
resulted from previous field work by Ba Than Haq and from the interpretation of aerial
photographs.
Loikaw is the administrative centre of Kayah State and all-weather roads connect
this town with the outlying villages of Namphe and Bawlake to the south and with the
hydro-electric power plant on the Balu Chaung at Lawpita to the south-east.
The area may be divided topographically into two parts separated by the north-south
lowing Pawn Chaung. The country to the west of this river is occupied by the Plateau
Limestone Serics, of Permo-Carboniferous age, which forms a flat plateau area approximately
at 3,000 feet around Loikaw. Limestone weathering has produced a more rugged landscape
to the south-east and erosion has exposed older rocks lying beneath the limestone. There
is a gradual decrease in altitude from the 3,000-foot level at Loikaw to 600 feet along the
Paung Chaung valley.
The principal direction of drainage is from north to south and the two consequent
rivers are the Paung Chaung and the Salween; the watershed between the two drainage
areas following the crest of the Paung Chaung scarp.

The Plateau Limestone Series is widespread in the Shan States and has been described
from many localities; the Mawchi Series has been so named as a result of mapping the tin
and wolfram concession area around Mawchi Mines by Hobson and associates; the Pawn
Chaung Series is a newly discovered group of low grade metamorphic rocks, the description
of which forms the basis of this paper.
The area is sparsely inhabited, especially east of the Pawn Chaung, the indigenous
inhabitants being the Kayahs. Their primary occupation is the growing of rice, either on
the steep hill slopes or in paddy field in the narrow valleys. They also keep pigs and a
few head of cattle.
Communications are very poor and much of the area is accessible only by foot.
There is an air-strip at Loikaw which is used by the Union of Burma Airways. The old road
between Loikaw and Rangoon connects with the railhead at Aungban, 80 miles north of
Loikaw and 400 miles from Rangoon. A new, dry-weather road connects Loikaw with
Taungoo which is situated on the Mandalay-Rangoon railway and which shortens the
distance between Loikaw and Rangoon by 180 miles.

(1) Wan Na--man to Wan Loi
The traverse continued in a southerly direction from Wan Na-man, passing firstly a
broad outcrop of low grade schists and phyllites showing a steep easterly dip. Quartz
veining is conspicuous and many large quartz veins measuring up to 500 feet in length and
50 feet in width were noted when crossing the summits of the numerous ridges. Marbles
are less frequent and less conspicuous and their topography not so overbearing as the crags
and scarps further west, The argillaceous rocks continue southwards to Daw Ta-nan village
and beyond to Daw Saw village, east and south-eastwards of which marbles again occur.
The footpath from Daw Saw village leaves in an casterly direction and climbs over
some fine-grained grey marble before turning south along a ridge composed of quartz vein
material flanked by sericite-chlorite-schist and black slaty phyllites. About 1 miles from
Daw Ta-nan village the footpath crosses an exposure of greenish-white marble, striking N/S
and dipping 40°E. Minor pückering and folding is again evident. From here southwards to
within 1 miles of the Shan village of Wantan the footpath follows the general N/S strike
of the chlorite-schists and quartz veins. Near Wanloi, black shaley slates replace the
chlorite schists.
The footpath descends from the teak covered schistose ridges into a broad valley west
of Wanhoi, and after passing through a shallow marble gorge enters a wide and broad

Immediately south of Wanhoi, the footpath climbs a steep 1,000-foot scarp consisting
mainly of a greyish-white marble with thin bands of phyllite and chlorite-schist, having a
N/S strike and a gentle plunging lineaction directed to the south. At the summit of the
scarp the footpath bears away to the south-west and the marbles are followed by a coarser
vatriety of chlorite schist. Similar rocks were noted earlier near Wan Ta-man village and
they form a belt of schist, approximately ½ mile in width, flanking the eastern side of the
main marble horizon. It appears that this belt which includes the marbles outcropping close
to the Paung Chaung scarp ridge represent the highest grade of metamorphism attained in
the area visited.
The chlorite-schists show shining reflection surfaces and much puckering on their
cleavage planes. Although generally of a pale green colour, there are many multi-coloured
varieties such as grey, bluish-grey, purplish-red, greenish yellow and brown, due to degrees
of weathering and to the amount of iron present. Chlorite-schists were followed for over
a mile before the footpath descended into a steep-sided stream (2,400 yds. SSW of Wanhoi
village) where lamps of magnetite some weighing up to 5lbs., were found amongst the
boulders in the stream bed. Green chloritic schists are well exposed in this section and
show dips approaching 50°E.
The footpath now follows the Hwe Ta-la valley, passing close to and eventually
through a large and steep outcrop of crystalline limestone and emerging once again into a
basin-shaped depression situated half a mile from Wan Ta-la village. The limestone of the
gorge through which the Hwa Ta-la stream passes is reminiscent of the Plateau Limestone;
it is finer-grained than the marbles and shows a darker grey colour. Aerial photographs
indicate a possible change in rock type in a small area on this side of the Pawn Chaung-
Safween divide, and it is tentatively suggested that these limestones are Plateau Limestones.
Marbles, rarer chlorite-schists and phyllites, again outcrop in the hills south and west
of Wan Ta-la village until a descent is made to the alluvial plains of Yawthit, the track
following a level course for 3 miles to the town of Ywathit.
Ywathit lies at the southern end of a large alluvial plain and its water supply and
irrigation is obtained from several springs which are situated at the foot of the limestone
scarp to the west of the town. One of the major sources of water emerges from the foot of
the cliffs, 500 yards due east of point 1774 on map 94 E/8. Due west from Yawthit is a
level-topped scarp trending N/S which is not in harmony with the typical rugged and craggy
country behind it. The scarp face comprises a fine-grained dark grey limestones which
appears to have been intruded by a pink coloured rock which under the microscope is seen
to consists predominantly of irregular shaped and brecciated calcite crystals of much coarser
grain size than the mylonitic host rock. The replacive material has not been mylonitized to
the same extent as the earlier limestone but nevertheless shows considerable cataclastic
effects. The carlier grey limestone appears to be more dolomitic than the later material.
Fieid evidence indicates a fault line scarp, the brecciation and secondary replacement result-
ing from the fault movements.

A noticeable platform occurs at the foot of the scarp where a thick covering of red
soil soon passes into an outcrop of reddish-grey coloured grits and fine-grained breccia
consisting of angular fragments of grey and white quartz and soft buff and grey shales; some
arenaceous material is present and together with hematite and limonite make up the matrix.
No pieces of limestone or marble were seen and the matrix is non-calcareous. These grits do
not appear to have a large real extent, the country to the north and south soon falling away
to steep sided valleys, but a study of the topographical map (Sheet No. 94 E/8) suggests
that the grits may again outcrop on some of the spurs to the north and to the south following
the 2,000-foot contour. The thickness of the grits is approximately 25 feet and the dip
appears to be gently inclined to the east.
The breccia and grits pass downwards into a thick group of variegated low
grade metamorphic shales exhibiting typical micaceous cleavage planes. These
argillaceous sediments have a general easterly dip but are highly contorted and in

passing downwards into blue shales and finally into a predominant horizon of purple slates

feet thick.

(7) Bawlake to Namphe
The motor road connecting these two towns first passes through a large area of
residual deposits mainly composed of brownish soil of loose earthy calcareous matter and
loose boulders and pebbles of argillaceous and arenaceous rocks. Beyond the residual
deposits pure grey-brown limestones of Upper Plateau Limestone age dip at approximately

unconformably overlain by the Plateau Limestones. Nearing Namphe, the Plateau
Limestones, with nearly horizontal dip, are seen to be occupying the top of the high ridge
which overlooks the Namphe valley which in turn consists of Namphe Beds of the Mawchi
Series.
The Namphe Beds consist of slates, shaly limestones, calcareous shales and black
limestones all of which dip at high angles to the east. Intercallations of thin bedded
limestones and calcareous shales are typical of the Nampho Beds. Pure jet black limestone
bands with widths varying from 20 feet to 25 feet also occur in the area. Unconformable
overlap of Plateau Limestone over the rocks of the Namphe Reds is clearly shown by the
topographic profiles. Hobson regarded the Namphe Beds as a calcareous facies of the
Mawchi Scries.
III Structure and Geological Conclusions
As stated earlier, on structural and geological grounds, the area has been divided
into three main divisions, viz. the Mawchi Series and rocks of equivalent age, the
Plateau Limestones and younger rocks and the Pawn Chaung Series.
The Mawchi Series with the overlap of the Plateau Limestones is mostly confined to
the area west of the Pawn Chaung whilst the Pawn Chaung Series has been limited to the
area between the Pawn Chaung and Salween rivers. Plateau Limestones with inliers of
Mawchi Series and a few outliers of younger rocks is the most widespread in the area.
Two major faults affect the area; the Western Fault, post Plateau Limestone in age,
runs from north of Kyebogyi where it brought down younger rocks against the Plateau
Limestone, to south of Sawlon where it joins the Eastern Fault. The Eastern
Fault, occurring to the west of Pawn Chaung, runs parallel to the Pawn Chaung
Series and is pre-Plateau Limestone in age. The movement along this fault affected the
Mawchi Series only and the Lower Plateau Limestone seems to be resting on this fault
without any sign of having been disturbed.

The Plateau Limestones
Detailed descriptions of Plateau Limestone has already been given by previous
workers such as La Touche, Coggin-Brown, Sondhi and others. In this area the Plateau
Limestones rest upon Mawchi rocks and their equivalents. The distinction between the
Lower and Upper Plateau Limestone is not very clear and hence the boundary has been
drawn arbitrarily. Increase of dolomitization, brecciation and lack of fossils has been
taken as criteria to divide the Lower from the Upper. The Lower Plateau Limestone with
underlying conglomerates is confined to the east of the area where it rests unconformably on
the Pawn Chaung Series. The entire Plateau Limestone is gently folded into large flat
anticlines and tight sqeezing synclines; one such syncline with younger rocks occurs a
little to the east of Kyebogyi. It seems that the thickness of Plateau Limestone is not so great
as has been assumed by earlies workers. The general low dips with occassional
horizontal bedding are typical. Difficulties such as the determination of dip and the
distinction between high angle joint planes and bedding planes may have given wrong
impressions to earlier workers.
There is little doubt that the conglomerate observed in the valley of the Pawn Chaung
represents the basal conglomerate of the Plateau Limestone Series. The vertical and lateral
extent does not appear great but this may be due to a large overlap of higher horizons in
the Plateau Limestone Series. The pebbles and boulders comprising the conglomerate were
derived from nearby sources, including the chlorite-schists and marbles of the Pawn Chaung
Series. The absence of Plateau Limestone pebbles in the conglomerate clearly indicates the
basal stratigraphical position of these conglomerates.
The Pawn Chaung scarp was obviously a land barrier at the time of the formation of
the basal conglomerate, since they abut against it and are not found to the east of the scarp
nor on the scarp itself. Different palaeogeographical conditions may have existed in Upper
Limestone times and it is possible that the Limestone sea broke through the land barrier or
circumnavigated it in order to deposit Upper Plateau Limestones on the terrain around
Ywathit. Although the Pawn Chaung scarp has the appearance of a fault line scarp, no
evidence was seen to indicate that it is present. The position of the conglomerates and the
overlap of the Upper Limestones suggests that the scarp was already in existence prior to the
invasion of the Plateau Limestone sea.

schists, is confined to the area between the Pawn Chaung and Salween rivers. Field evidence,
including the dips and minor structures, indicate that the Series has been folded into
recumbent folds plunging towards the south. The highest ground, ranging from 2,000 to
5,000 feet, has preserved the large recumbent syncline which pitches to the east and the

Additional, though at present not confirmed evidence, that the Pawn Chaung Series
is older than the Mawchi Serics, is afforded by the occurrence of marble and schistose rock
to the south west of Loikaw which is known as the Biya Limestone Series. These rock have
been studied by one of the authors and it seems that they may be similar to the Pawn
Chaung Series. The Biya Series is regarded as a domed inlier of older rocks.
IV Economic Considerations
(7)
Radio activity
Geiger counter readings were noted from many places along the route but there was
no significant change from a low background count (8 = .015 mR/hr). Areas most likely to
have shown higher readings were the basal conglomerates in the Pawn Chaung valley, the
chlorite-schist and phyllitic areas east of the main marble ridge and the breccia at the foot of
the marble scarp east of Hawkham.
The Plateau Limestone conglomerate contains material derived from older rocks and
therefore would be expected to show evidence of Pre-Plateau Limestone mineralization if
such existed. However, little evidence was found and there was little variety in the heavy
mineral content; various altered forms of iron being the only recognizable mineral in the
concentrate. Geiger readings on the exposures in the field gave a maximum of g .02 mR/hr.
Many small streams and some larger ones containing gravel beds were radiometrically
tested and their heavy mineral content noted. Iron ore, actinolite, tremolite and rare apatite
and rutile were observed. Readings were again low.

(2). Decorative stone and cement
The Pawn Chaung marbles are generally exceedingly fine grained, hard and compact
and free from iron oxides and sulphide minerals. They are pale greenish-white or white in
colour and would make an excellent decorative stone. Minute chevron folding in the pale
grey marbles at the foot of the scarp where the path begins its ascent of the scarp from the
Lya-be-lya valley is especially attractive when cut and polished. Other colour-banded marbles
occur on the scarp face and also at the summit and diligent searching should provide a variety
of design and colour,
There is some fiine black marble, hard and compact, in the vicinity of Namphe which
also takes a good polish and with fine white streaks is also highly decorative.
The suitability of any particular marble or limestone for cement manufacture is
dependant upon its Mg0 content. Many of the limestone bands are dolomitic but there
are also suitable limestone or marble for cement manufacture. The area abounds in shales
of various composition which may be suitable for mixing purposes.
(3). Magnetite
Although large pieces of magnetite, some weighing up to 5 lbs, were found in a small
stream, the absence of a much wider distribution of this mineral precludes the possibility of a
large economic magnetite deposit. The lumps found in the stream bed are probably associa-
ted with local iron mineralization in shear and fault zones in the chlorite-schist.
