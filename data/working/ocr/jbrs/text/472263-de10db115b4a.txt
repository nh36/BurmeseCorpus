Conclusions and Summary
Throughout the preceding discussion
we have treated of personality and to
some extent ideology, as if they were to
be found unimodally in Burmese society.
For convenience of exposition we have
preferred to sidestep, for the time being,
the important issue of multimodality in
personality and ideology and of differentia-
tion within social structures. In doing
so we may have inadvertently given the
impression of claiming more homogeneity
in terms of these constructs than probably
does, in fact, exist and of implying that
a one-to-one degree of congruence obtains
between these different levels of social
action. While the relative homogeneity
of the Burmese population enables us to
speak meaningfully in terms of a typical
pattern or mode of behavior, it would
be extremely rash to regard this
being necessarily the only possible mode.
The tremendous variability and complexity
to be found within a modern nation-state
like that of the Burmese, renders any
such assumption of unimodality extremely
unlikely. This highlights the necessity of
undertaking further empirical research to
determine not only the central tendencies
but also the variability; not just the
most typical, but the alternative modes
as well.
