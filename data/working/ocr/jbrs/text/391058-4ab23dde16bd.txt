Introduction
The umbrella is an essential part of life to the people of tropical
countries like India, Burma, and other Southeast Asian countries,
where the rains, as well as the sun, are extreme.
At present, most of
the demand is met by imported umbrellas from western countries like
Germany, Switzerland, Italy, and the United Kingdom. In India
some factories have been engaged in the manufacture of umbrella ribs
for the last seven or eight years. The ribs constitute the most
important component of a modern umbrella. But even now the ribs
that are being mannfactured in these factories are made from imported
heat treated wires which form the only important raw material for the
umbrella ribs industry. In Burma, most of the modern umbrellas and
umbrella ribs are imported from foreign countries, though some
traditional designs of umbrellas from bamboo etc. are still
manufactured in this country. The author of this paper is of strong.
opinion that umbrella ribs can be manufactured in this country very
easily with indigenous raw materials. It is this idea which has made
him take up the study of possibilities for establishing the umbrella rib
industry from indigenous raw materials, and submit a workable scheme
for the same, so that it may be useful to those employed, now and in
the future, in producing umbrella ribs from indigenous raw materials.
Wire Drawing
Umbrella ribs demand a wire of good quality steel, rolled to exact
dimensions and heat treated to the proper specifications. The slender
rods from which wire is drawn are known as wire rods which are now
produced by hot rolling. These may be made in various shapes and
sizes, but for common wires and fine wires, that is, No. 8 and finer,
No. 5 round rods may be considered as the standard. A No. 5 rod is
the sinallest round practicable to be produced on a rolling mill, and

After the steel for umbrella wire has been made according to
specifications, it is cast into ingots and rolled on the blooming mill in
the usual manner. The product of the blooming mill is a 4" X 4"
billet which is cut into lengths to give the desired weight, allowed to
cool, inspected, reheated and then rolled directly into rods. In some
cases the blooming mill is succeeded by a billet mill which gives
billets 2" square or less in size Rods are rolled generally from
2" or " square billets. The process of rolling is accomplished
by means of heating the billets in reheating furnaces and giving it
different passes. When the billets have been rolled to the required
size, they are passed on to the coiling bed where they are coiled
down and cooled.
To produce a satisfactory rod requires exercise of proper care in
every department concerned with its preparation. To begin with, the
steel must be properly made and its composition should be uniform
and as near to the specification as possible; also, it is essential that the
ingot be properly cast and rolled at a suitable temperature, and that the
billet be free from pipe, seams, and slivers. Even with perfect billets,
considerable care and skill are required to produce satisfactory rods.
Assuming that the rod mill itself has been properly designed and speeded
and equipped with all necessary accessories, unsatisfactory rods may still
be produced by improper heating, by the use of improperly designed
ragging on the roughing rolls, by improper adjustment of the rolls and
guides or by using the grooves too iong. In order to finish the rod
correctly, every pass in the mill should be made to produce, as near as
possible, the section it was originally designed to produce, and to bring
about this result all the passes must be kept full though none must be
allowed to overfill. Great emphasis is to be laid upon all these features
of operation, because from the point of view of the wire mill it is
essential that the rod be uniform in composition and also in section,
that it be close to the size and shape required, and that it be free
from such surface defects as slivers, seams and fins.

In order to prepare rods for wire drawing, they are first given a
number of preliminary treatments, depending upon the grade of steel,
amount of drawing to be done, and the kind of finish desired. In the
case of certain high carbon wires like umbrella wire, for example, the
rods will first be heat treated to give them a uniform structure and to
develop their drawing properties. In the case of low carbon rods this
treatment is not necessary.
The first very important operation prior to wire drawing is the
cleaning of the hard, black brittle oxide, commonly called scale, which
forms on the surface of hot rolled rods and annealed wire, and also the
slow-forming red rust which forms on long exposure to the air. This
must be entirely removed before drawing, as otherwise the holes in the
dies would be quickly cut out of shape and the surface of the wire
spoiled.
The method employed for cleaning the material consists of dipping
the coils into a vat of hot dilute sulphuric acid. The concentration of
the acid varies from about 1 per cent to about 7 per cent, depending
upon the grade of steel, the finishing temperature of the rods, and the
compactness of the bundles. The action by which this acid removes
the scale is more mechanical than chemical, for ferroso-ferric oxide,
Fe 3 0 4, which is the chief constituent of the scale, is but slightly
soluble in sulphuric acid. The acid, however, is able to penetrate to
the metal beneath the scale, where it acts upon the iron, forming iron
sulphate, a soluble neutral salt, and liberating a mixture of gases, mainly
composed of hydrogen. This action results in loosening and detaching
the scale from the surface of the metal, whereupon it sinks to the
bottom of the vat where it accumulates and must be removed at
frequent intervals, normally from 12 to 24 hours.
The temperature of the pickling bath is normally maintained at
180° F by passing steam through the bath. The velocity of reaction
between metal and acid is greatly affected by the temperature, being
about 100 times greater at 190° F than at normal atmospheric tempera_
tures. But a mean has to be chosen as higher temperatures lead to the
loss of steam, and of as much as 15 per cent of the acid being carried
away with the steam. In addition, the direct action of the acid causes
an excessively heavy loss of steel, great injury to building and machinery,
and discomfort to the men.

The time factor depends upon the size, kind and condition of the
rod to be cleaned and upon the condition of the acid solution. In
general this time varies from 10 to 30 minutes. The time element is
especially important in cleaning the high carbon rods used for umbrella
wires. The effects of the acid treatment are more pronounced upon
high carbon material than upon low carbon material. If high carbon
steel is etched too long, the action of the acid may leave a carbonaceous
deposit upon the surface, a defect known as a "smutty surface". Great
care is also required in pickling high carbon stock to prevent pitting the
surface.
As soon as the material has been pickled, the pin of coil is hoisted
from the acid vat and dipped into a cold fresh-water vat to remove the
acid. A stream of water under high pressure is injected against the coils
to dislodge any pieces of scale or other foreign substances adhering to
the surface.
After thorough rinsing of the coils, a coating of slaked lime is
given to the coils which prevents wear of the die and makes less power
required. The chief function of the coating is to serve as an absorbent
and carrier of the die lubricant. Also, being alkaline in character, it
tends to neutralize any traces of acid remaining after the cleaning
process and protects the material from oxidation during the interval
between the cleaning and drawing, and also, to some extent, after
drawing. The coating liquid is usually kept at a temperature of about
140 F and the consistency of the emulsion is regulated according to the
thickness of the coating desired.
The final treatment given to the material is baking. The first
object is to dry the coating thoroughly so that it can perform its
function in the drawing operation. The second object is to remove
the acid brittleness produced to a greater or less degree in the cleaning
operation. This brittleness is produced by hydrogen absorbed by the
steel while it is in the pickling bath, the gas being liberated by the action
of the acid upon the metal. By baking the material from 3 to 12
hours at a temperature of 250°F to 400°F, depending upon the kind of
material, this absorbed hydrogen is driven out and the steel is restored
to its original toughness.
These coils are taken to the wire-drawing department. The coils
are placed separately upon a real drum before the wiredrawing frame.

Heat Treatment of Umbrella Wire
The wire drawn for making umbrella ribs is usually No. 14 gauge
which corresponds to approximately 0.09 ".
approximately 0.09". The main property
required for the umbrella ribs is springiness so that the rib should attain
its original position when the stresses are released. This means that the
wire for manufacturing the ribs should be hard as well as ductile.
These properties are easily attained in a wire which has been “Patented".
Patenting is a special process, peculiar to the wire industry, for develop-
ing a sorbitic structure.
The object of patenting is to develop a sorbitic structure to the
practical exclusion of Pearlite and free ferrite or free cementite which
gives high "yield point" and very great tenacity, of the order to
300.000 to 400,000 pounds per square inch, while retaining considerable
toughness. This structure represents a step in the transition from
Austerite to Pearlite, in which the grains are very small and the iron
carbide is distributed in very finely divided form in the ferrite. To
produce sorbite, the rate of cooling should be such that the "Ar point"
is depressed appreciably below its equilibrium value, (to about 550 to
600°C), when austerite would transform direct into sorbite.
For patenting, the article is raised gradually to the hardening
temperature which is slightly above the critical range. For steels of
0.6% to 0.85% carbon which is generally found in umbrella wire, the
hardening temperature is from 800 to 850 C. The heating causes the
ferries to change to the gamma-form, which dissolves the iron carbide,

forming a solid solution. The prevention of damage to the surface of
the steel is effected by using controlled-atmosphere heating furnaces or
lead bath furnaces. The hardening temperature having been attained
and the wire being soaked for a suitable time, which varies from 5 to 15
minutes depending upon carbon content and gauge of wire, it is
quenched in molten lead kept a temperature of about 370° C. This
steel now shows a sorbitic structure. Often, after obtaining the struc-
ture, the wire is drawn once or twice which gives a better tensile strength.
The actual operation of the heat treatment of umbrella wires is all
automatic and therefore simple. Once the conditions are adjusted it
becomes a very simple affair and does not present much difficulty, for
the furnaces used are controlled automatically. To adjust furnace
conditions to the proper requirements requires great skill and
experience. The furnace used is a lead bath furnace which is heated
either electrically or by ordinary fuel coal or gas. Generally the furnace
is divided into two main sections, namely (1) the heating section and
(2) the quenching section. In the heating section the temperature of
the molten bath is maintained at about 900 C by automatic tempera-
ture-controlling devices. Lead is used as heating bath because of its
low melting point (327. C) together with its high boiling point (1525° C).
It also prevents oxidation of the steel wire which would be excessive if
the heating were done in an open atmosphere. The quenching section
of the furnace also consists of a lead bath at a temperature of
350° C. The wire heated at a temperature of 900° C passes directly into
this section and is quenched at such a rate as to produce a sorbitic
structure. Some manufacturers eliminate the quenching section and
cool the wire directly in air. But there is much more control over the
structure in lead quenching than in air cooling.
Sometimes the sorbitic structure is obtained by a method of
hardening and then tempering to high temperatures. The wire is first
heated to the hardening temperature as in "Patenting" and then
suddenly quenched in water to obtain a martensitic structure. This
martensite is then tempered which gradually transforms it into second-
ary troostite and then into sorbite as the tempering temperature is
gradually increased. This process is not generally applicable to
umbrella wire patenting but some foreign manufacturers of umbrella
ribs use this method for obtaining a sorbitic structure.

The Modern Method of Manufacturing Umbrella Ribs
The manufacture of umbrella ribs is done in a continuous way, the
whole work being highly mechanised and done with automatic machines.
A factory producing some 28,000 round ribs per 8 hours rarely requires
more than fifty men, including the administrative staff.
The manufacture of umbrella ribs takes place in the following
steps:
Rib Making At present modern automatic machines are used
for the manufacture of umbrella ribs and stretchers of any wire and
profile. The machine works the heat treated wire or profile directly
from coils of about three feet in diameter, thus making unnecessary the
two further operations of cutting into the required lengths and
annealing. Also, the storing of the raw material is thus greatly
simplified. The wire enters the machines and at each working
operation it is annealed by electrical resistance heating at the place
where it has to be worked at and cut. Afterwards follow the flattening,
forming, punching and cutting, and the finished ribs or stretechers leave
the machine automatically. All these machines can be adjusted for
any required length or rib or stretcher.
Fitting of the Joints: This is done by special small presses with
convertible tools. The joint is cut out from the steel strip and directly
pressed on the ribs.
Joining the Ribs and Stretchers: This is a special rib-joining
machine which has a special eccentric press, with fixed stroke, mounted
on a special table. A special hitch feed brings the strip for the joints
to the tool. The engagement of the press is effected by means of a
guard, which actuates a foot lever.
Polishing The ribs and stretchers are brushed when they leave
the machine to remove the roughness of the edge, by means of an
electrical polishing motor with two free arbors, ready to be fixed with
circular brushes and polishing wheels.
Degreasing: The complete ribs are cleaned of grease and dust
before varnishing, in a Tri-chlore-Ethylene installation. The plant is
attached with a distillation equipment, to clean the used tri-chlore-
ethylene solution.

Experimental Work
The experimental work was carried on in two stages. In the first
part, a study of the properties of some of the popular types of
umbrella ribs and stretchers, imported from different countries, was
made. Some heat treated umbrella wires which are being imported for
the manufacture of umbrella ribs in the country were also examined.
The second part of the experiments performed consisted of the
important steps in the manufacture of the umbrella ribs from
indigenous raw materials.
Part A:-The experimental work was divided into three
sub-divisions:
(i) Chemical analysis.
(ii) Physical properties.
(iii) Microscopic examination.
Under chemical analysis, the first qualitative tests were performed
to search for various alloying elements such as chromium, nickel,
tungsten, molybdenum, vanadium etc. But the tests gave no indication
of these elements even in the smallest amounts. From this it was
inferred that umbrella ribs are manufactured purely from plain carbon
steels.
After this a quantitative determination was made of the various
elements which are generally present in steel, e.g. carbon, silicon,
manganese, sulphur and phosphorus. The results obtained are tabulated
in the adjoining table No. 1.
Physical examination included the measurements, hardness and
tensile strength of the ribs and stretchers. The results tabulated in table
No. 1 show that all the ribs of gents' umbrellas belong to a standard
length and diameter, namely 25" and 0.09" respectively
and the stretchers nearly 11' in length an 0.13" square.

under carbon arc light, with yellow filter inserted. This showed a good
contrast in the structure developed.
During the examination most of the ribs showed invariably the
sorbitic structure of patented wire except one of Japanese origin which
showed only the fibrous structure of cold drawn steel wire. The
structure showed elongated pearlite (rather fibrous) in a matrix of
ferrite. There was no indication of any grain boundary and hence
it is inferred that the wire had been severely cold drawn and that no heat
treatment whatsoever had been given after wire drawing to recrystallise
the grains or to obtain a patented structure as in the other ribs.
Examining the degree of hardness, it was found that the rib was not as
hard as the others. The stretchers of some of the popular umbrellas
showed only elongated grains of ferrite with a very little amount of
pearlite (carbon 0.1%). This type of stretcher is now very common as
no hardening or patenting is required for this type. This is because the
stretchers have only to support the ribs and hence much elasticity is not
required.
The inference is drawn from these examinations of ribs and stret-
chers that chemical composition is not as important as heat treatment.
Any steel wire having a chemical composition within given limits may
be used for making umbrella ribs after suitable heat treatment to obtain
the sorbitic structure of patented wire.

For the establishment of a new industry, the economic considera-
tion is the first and foremost factor. During the investigation period,
an economic survey was also made with all relevant details of the cost
of land, expenditure in constructing the shed and building, cost of
machinery, working capital and expenditures (which includes depreciation
of land, building and machinery, cost of raw materials, labour wages,
staff salaries, power costs, stores and the remuneration of the Managing
Director). It was found that the price of umbrella ribs made with
indigenous raw materials would not be higher, if not less, than the
prices for imported umbrella ribs or wires.
Conclusion
In conclusion, the author has no hesitation in stating that with
increasing industrialization the umbrella ribs industry of indigenous
origin is only a question of time. The "Government of the Union of
Burma is contemplating the installation of an electric steel melting
furnace, to make steel from scrap found in Burma, and of a re-rolling
mill. A wire drawing plant can also be installed with this which will
produce steel wires. This will be the raw material for the umbrella rib
industry. As the umbrella rib industry does not require a very huge
amount of capital, men with industrial outlook and enterprise
may enter into the field. The author is of the strong opinion
that if small industries like the making of umbrella ribs, razor-blades,
needles, and surgical instruments are started with proper technical
guidance and modern equipment, they will fetch handsome profits.
In the beginning the industry may be supported by the Government by
measures such as restriction of imports, imposition of heavy import
duties, and subsidies if necessary.
