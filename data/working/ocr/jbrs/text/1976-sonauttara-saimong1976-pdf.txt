Did Sona and Uttara Come to Lower Burma ?
Sao Saimong Mangrai*
There is a tradition firmly believed by the Theravādins that after the third
Sangãyanā in the 3rd century B. C. the Sāsanā was taken by nine groups of mahā-
theras to various countries within and beyond India. The Mahāvaṁsa and most
chronicles of Burma and Thailand that have anything to do with Buddhism mention
this, probably all having the Mahāvaṁsa as their source. The mission to Ceylon
was led by Mahinda Thera, Asoka's son; the mission to Suvaṇṇabhūmi, the "Land
of Gold”, which the Burmese identify as Lower Burma, was undertaken by the
theras Soņa and Uttara; and the thera Mahārakkhita was sent to the country of
the Yona ("Ionian”, i. e. Greeks or perhaps Hellenized Iranians). Western scholars
take the mission to Ceylon more seriously than the one to Lower Burma because
it is mentioned in the chronicles of Ceylon, and communication between North
India and Ceylon in Asoka's time is confirmed by his inscriptions, while, the
scholars maintain, the exact location of Suvaṇṇabhumi cannot be determined. On
the other hand, missions to the Hellenized states to the west tend to be accepted
as fact, since Asoka himself says he sent embassies to states as distant as Egypt
and Syria, in spite of the fact that Asoka's embassies are not known to be connec-
ted with the missionary work of the theras, and that, in any case, Greek and Latin
sources say nothing of the Buddhist missions, or, for that matter (excepting the
Greco-Aramaic inscription of Kandahar), of Asoka or his embassies.
Let us examine the possibility of the coming of Soņa and Uttara to Lower
Burma in the 3rd century B.C.

Throughout the period of European colonialism, Southeast Asia was regarded
as very much of an appendage of India and China, and it was given the names of
both India and China (Indo-China) or that of Further India or East India. Except
for Siam all the countries that comprise the present-day Southeast Asia were
colonies, and even Siam had to cede a large portion of her eastern territories to
France besides conceding extra-territorial rights to all European and American
powers as a price of her independence. Up to World War II it was customary for
scholars to assume that there was little that Southeast Asia had contributed to the
civilization of Asia and that there was little worthy of scholastic attention until the
arrival of civilizing influence from India and China. Indigenous chronicles were
regarded as fairy tales and were the butts of intellectual jokes, so much
nationalists accused foreign rulers of teaching their compatriots to despise their own
culture and civilization.

This was natural. More funds were available for archaeological explorations
and excavations elsewhere. Then, as now, Southeast Asia was a ferile ground for
commercial exploitation, not for excavation, by foreigners and natives alike, and
scholars were the last people in the world capable of influencing government poli-
cies anywhere. Nationalists were too busy agitating to insist on excavations to prove
their past. Indian scholars who were not politicians satisfied their nationalist
aspirations by claiming without proof that Asoka's conquest of Kalinga and Kani-
shka's establishment of the Kushan empire in western India caused "waves" of
Indian refugees, colonizers and adventurers to emmigrate and colonize practically
all of Southeast Asia. Commerce was another theory put forward by scholars
for the Indian migrational waves, without taking into consideration the return
traffic by the seafaring Malays or Indonesians who are known to have blazed the
sea trail to China and to have gone and actually colonized and settled in Madagas-
car. Intermarriage was another reason given for the theory of Indian settlements,
though there is trace of neither Indian blood nor common dialects having been
left behind. In short, these one-way traffic theories were exactly what European
powers, particularly the Portuguese, the French and the Dutch, had been doing in

and Brahmans from India, it is more likely that only those who could not make
their way in their own homeland would emmigrate. Even then it was admitted
that when the "colonizers" arrived in Southeast Asia they met not savages but
organized societies with a civilization of their own.2 To admit Indian influences,
yes; but to give Southeast Asian States from earliest days to the 14th or 15th
centuries the name of Indian kingdoms,³ no.
Since the end of the last World War, however, things have begun to look
differently. Thanks largely to American scholars and archaeologists who went to
Southeast Asia in the wake of their soldiers, archaeological finds in Northeast and
Northwest Thailand have made scholars reassess past assumptions that every aspect
of civilization in Southeast Asia had come from abroad. They now speculate
seriously that Southeast Asia might have been the cradle of civilization for all Asia;
they think that the first domestication of plants in the world occurred in Southeast
Asia more than 17,000 years ago; bronze tools and pots, some with beautiful fin-
ger-print designs found (at Banchieng) in Northeast Thailand, might be as old as
4500 B.C.; there is a probability that bronze had been exported from Southeast
Asia to the Middle East, which was thought to have been the source of bronze
for Europe.4
With these new and revolutionary ideas based on archaeological diggings it
is possible to think that there were indeed organized societies and settled govern-
ments in Southeast Asia prior to the coming of Indian influences a little before and
around the early centuries of the Christian era, as revealed by excavations of the
19th and 20th centuries. Perhaps the theras Soņa and Uttara were among the
earliest of those influences.
To strengthen the foregoing, old Chinese writings state that there was a land
route between China and India which passed through northern Burma in the 2nd
century B.C.5 This means that the said land route was already in existence by the
time mention was made of it in the Chinese writings, and no one can confirm or
deny that it did not exist long before that.

present Burmese era or Cuța Sakkarāja and added 638 to convert them into dates of
the Christian era; 50, 57, and 80 become 688, 695, and 718 A.D. respectively. Some
Burmese scholars, however, interpret them as Saka era or Mahā Sakkarāja and add
78 to make them 128, 135 and 158 A.D., because they maintain that, according to
the Burmese chronicles, the Saka era came into being in 78 A.D. during the
Tharekhittarā period while the present Burmese or Cula era was inaugurated in 630
A.D. by a king of Pagan named Poppa Sawrahan.
The inaguration of the Saka era at Tharekhittarā was attributed to the work
of Sakka, king of devas, who had calculated it on a rock at locality called
Lakananda Kyauksaga when the Buddha Sāsanã era had reached 624, but, continues
the chronicle account, astrological considerations decided that the new (Saka)
era actually begani n the year 622 of Buddha Sakkarāja, and that therefore the year 624
would be reckoned as year 2 of the new era. What probably happened was that the
court astrologers of Tharekhittarā at the time (some Brahmans, most likely, who could
not make ends meet in India) received the news of the commencement of Saka era
in Western India over a year later and recommended its adoption to their lord and
master the king with astrological adjustments as stated. If this assumption is correct,
one cannot help admiring the astrologers royal's wit in converting Saka into Sakka. It
is important to resolve the 560 years' difference between the two views of the
dates of the Pyū funeral urns in order to coroborate the antiquity of Tharekhittarā
as determined by radio carbon. Only experts can do this, but in view of the proved
existence of Tharekhittarā in the 1st century A.D., is it not reasonable to assume
that the dates on the funeral urns belong to the Saka era (unless the script itself
proves otherwise)?

clear indications that Buddhism at Tharekhittarā from the 5th century onwards was

here and there in Tharekhittara evidence of the Mahāyāna Sect and Brähmanism is
also found. There is nothing extraordinary in this; even in modern times Hindu
temples and Muslim mosques exist side by side with the predominant Buddhist
pagodas and monasteries in Rangoon, Mandalay and other big towns of Burma.
The probability of the existence of organized governments in southeast Asia
before the Christian era, the land route between China and India through northern
Burma in the 2nd century B.C., the existence of Pyù cities from the 1st to the 8th
centuries (together with the artifacts mentioned and others of gold, silver and semi-
precious stones), certainly strengthen the possibility of Soņa and Uttara coming to
Lower Burma in the 3rd century B.C. Who can tell? The whole of Southeast Asia
may have been flourishing at this period on a scale hitherto thought impossible;
though solid evidence is lacking, circumstantial evidence would permit us to assume
that possibility. Full reports are awaited of the recent excavations by the Archaeo-
logy Department of Burma of one of the old sites near the town of old Thaton,
traditionally thought to be the port of Suvaṇṇbhūmi, at the foot of Mount Kelāsa
on which a monastery is believed to have been built for the two theras. It has
been said that a delegation of Rāmañña (Mon) Sangha headed by the lord abbot
of Keläsa Monastery was present at the consecration ceremonies of the great stupa

The walls of Tharekhittarā are roughly circular in shape measuring two miles
and a quarter east to west and two miles and three quarters north to south. "And
Sakra stood in the center of a piece of pleasant level ground and described a circle
by means of a rope dragged round by the Naga." So says the Glass Palace
Chronicle, which goes on to give the dimensions of the city as one yojana in
diameter and three yojana in circumference. Could the Chinese be referring to
Tharekhittarā in their writing when they say the Pyü city's circumference was 160
li? Some Burmese scholars think that this Pyū city is Rājgaha (Yāzagyo) in the
Chindwin river valley midway between Mawlaik in the north and Kalemyo in the
south. The area is referred to as Kaletaungnyo in the Glass Palace Chronicle. 13
That Tharekhittară existed in the 7th century was certified in 648 and 675
respectively by Hsuan Tsang and I Tsing, the Chinese pilgrims to India.14 Thus
the circumstantial evidence is strong for the continuous existence of Tharekhittarā
for a period of nearly one thousand years.
From all this it is difficult to resist the temptation to assume that Theravāda
Nikaya existed in Burma from the 1st to the 9th centuries A.D. If so, its origin
could only have been traced to the coming of the theras Soņa and Uttara.
Authorities in Burma are excavating ancient sites of old Thaton, as stated
above, for it was at old Thaton that Soņa and Uttara are believed to have landed, and
