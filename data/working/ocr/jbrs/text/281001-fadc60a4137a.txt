I. 1786-1804.
Felix Carey, eldest son of Dr William Carey, the pioneer of English
missionary enterprise, was born at Moulton in Northampton. shire on October
20th, 1786. Though the eldest son, Felix was not the eldest child of the family, for
he had been preceded by a daughter, Ann, who had lived only a year. His father at
the time of Felix's birth was earning a precarious livelihood as schoolmaster,
shoemaker, and Baptist preacher, and the first few years of Felix's life must have
been passed in circumstances of some poverty, the more so as his father's family
was growing, two more sons, William and Peter, being born in rapid succession, in
addition to another daughter, Lucy, who also died in infancy, a fact not surprising
in those days of high infant mortality. The family moved to Leicester when Felix
was two years old, and there they lived until William Carey took the momentous
decision that led to his departure to India.
When William Carey first decided to commence missionary work in India,
his wife was unwilling to accompany him, but, apparently after much persuasion,
she agreed that he might take as companion their eldest son, now a boy of six years
of age. Their departure, however, was delayed by the reluctance of the East India
Company to grant permits to missionaries to enter India. At length the captain of
an East-Indiaman agreed to take them to Calcutta without the required licenses,

When, however, the convoy was ready to sail, a message was received by
the ship's captain, apparently from a dissatisfied creditor of Carey's fellow
missionary, John Thomas, warning him of the con-sequences of taking an
unlicensed passenger, and the Earl of Oxford weighed anchor leaving the Carey's
and Thomas behind. After some further delay, passages were obtained on a Danish
ship, the Kron Princessa Maria, which was not affected by the restrictions imposed
on British vessels. One result of the delay was that Mrs. Carey, now recovered
from the confinement which had been the principal reason for her former refusal to
accompany her husband, was able and willing to undertake the voyage, and so on
the 13th June 1793 the whole family, including the newly born Jabez, set out from
Dover.
The voyage seems to have been much like other voyages in those leisurely
days. Immediately on sailing the whole party, including the children, suffered from
sea-sickness, but after a week they recovered. The ship's people seem to have
shown them considerable kindness, and when Mrs. Carey and her children were
sick the captain sent them soup, wine, and "other comfortable things" and came in
person to see them and to ensure that they lacked nothing that they needed. At one
time there was some fear of a water shortage, but a heavy fall of rain remedied the
deficiency. They encountered a violent storm in which the "gibboom" was lost. In
general, however, the voyage benefited the health of the travelers, and though
Carey himself suffered from some illness, yet "the children were complete sailors."
Carey pursued his pastoral activities among his fellow passengers, and also gave
much time to the study of Bengali. Nor did he neglect to consider in what way he
could turn his children's lives towards the missionary field. "I am very desirous,"
he wrote on the 2nd August, "that my children may pursue the same work; and
now intend to bring one up in the study of Sunscrit, and another of Persian." Since
his eldest surviving child was not yet seven years old, his plans seem somewhat
ambitious. 3

At last, on the 7th November, after a voyage which had lasted almost five
months, the Kron Princessa Maria arrived in Balasore Roads, and on the 11th the
party reached Calcutta. 4
For the next few years Carey experienced great difficulties. Shortage of
money compelled him to seek a livelihood as cultivator and later as indigo planter,
though he never neglected his primary object of missionary work. Apart from
financial worries, he was troubled by the illnesses which afflicted his wife and
children. Two months after their arrival in India, he wrote that his wife and two
eldest children had been afflicted with the "bloody flux," i.e., dysentery, and that it
was doubtful whether Felix would recover. Again in August 1794 he states that his
wife and Felix have been afflicted with dysentery for the last eight months, and
that Felix was still very ill, while fevers and agues had been common to them all.
On the 6th January 1795 he writes that his wife and eldest son have only now
recovered from dysentery of twelve months' duration, while his third son, Peter,
five years of age, had died from flux and fever. At the end of the same month Felix
was evidently ill again, for Carey writes that all his household were well except his
eldest son.
The severe illnesses which afflicted him during these early days in India
and the general poverty from which the household at times suffered, must have left
an enduring effect on Felix's physical and mental constitution, and are to be borne
in mind in judging his subsequent career.
Felix's religious training was not neglected, despite his bad health, for in
March 1794 Carey wrote that he was instructing his children in religion and felt
that he was making some Impression. It may be doubted, however, whether during
these early years Felix received the training which he needed. His father's
preoccupation with business, and the mental derangement which increasingly
afflicted his mother, must have left the boy largely to his own devices. He did,
however, pick up the vernaculars with the ease usually experienced by children
brought up mainly by servants, and in October 1795 Carey found that "my children
can speak nearly as well as the natives; and know many things in Bengallee which
they do not know in English," and two years later was able to record that his sons
all knew Bengali and Hindustani as well as any Indian. Felix's health, apparently,
improved to some extent as time went on, and in January 1797 Carey states that
"my whole family enjoy much better health than we did on our first coming."

In the year 1799 a second group of missionaries arrived in Bengal. Among
them was William Ward, whose fame as a missionary pioneer is second only to
Carey's own. In view of the opposition of the East India Company to their work,
Ward and his companions took up their residence in the Danish settlement at
Serampore, near Calcutta, where the Danes were willing to give facilities for
missionary enterprise which the Company denied. Ward came to Carey, who was
then living at Mudnabatti, and in response to his request Catty removed with his
family to Serampore, early in the year 1800; and so was founded the famous
College which still flourishes, and the Serampore Press from Which have been
issued so many works of scholarship and religion. From the beginning of Ward's
association with Dr Carey, Felix received, it would seem, a degree of care and
interest which his father had perhaps never had time and his mother had latterly not
had the ability to give him. Ward from the first seems to have taken an interest in
the lad, now thirteen years of age. He records on the day of his arrival at
Mudnabatti that "Felix is 14 or 15," and from this, since Felix was in fact only
thirteen years of age, it may be concluded that the boy was experiencing that
unhealthily premature development which is common to European children who
remain in the tropics in their teens. He also states that Mrs. Catty was completely
deranged.
At Serampore Ward devoted much time to Felix and the younger William,
whose behaviour had been such as to cause some concern. Felix in particular
appears to have been decidedly undisciplined. He is described as being a "tiger,"
and Ward feared lest, he should drift away altogether from the religion in which he
had been brought up. He therefore endeavoured to supply the religious training
which, it must be feared, the boys, father had not given. His efforts seem to have
met with success, and on the 1st August 1800 he wrote in his diary that "I have
lately had brother Carey's two eldest sons in my room once a week for religious
conversation and instruction. In both of them appears considerable attention Felix
enquires, with tears, the way of salvation. I talk; they weep, they read, they
pray, and ask questions." Carey was much gratified by the effects of Ward's
efforts; "I have great pleasure," he wrote, "in saying that there have lately
been some very hopeful appearances in my two eldest sons. Time only can show
how it may end; but at present I hope well of them. Should they be truly converted,
their faculty of speaking Bengallee and Hindostanee, (both of which they speak as
freely as if they were their vernacular languages) would give them an advantage

From this time Felix was engaged in work on behalf of the mission. He
attached himself particularly to Ward and accompanied him about the country,
participating in the work of preaching. For example, on the 18th January 1801 he
talked to villagers about salvation by Christ; on the 20th November he went with
Ward and the convert Krishnu to a poojah and distributed tracts, some of which
were seized by an English police officer; on the 15th December they went to two
more villages. In addition, he assisted Ward in the printing-office in setting up the
type of the Bengali New Testament.

Truly a strange letter from a boy of only fourteen years, though no doubt
the writer was experiencing the religious enthusiasm which not uncommonly
attends adolescence. At the moment, however, the enthusiasm was obviously
strong, and his repentance for the "sin" of his unregenerate days doubtless sincere.
The Baptist Missionary Society at home, on learning of his conversion, wrote to
him expressing its pleasure at the news, and in addition to sending him a parcel of
books gave formal recognition to him as a missionary.
"You will consider that the work to which you are chosen at so early a

For the time being, however, Felix settled down to this sort of life, varied
by expeditions into the country for the work of preaching. His labours must have
been heavy; printing and preaching were added to by the superintendence of a
Sunday School "to teach catechisms in English and Bengallee"; and his health was
again none too good; his lungs gave some trouble, and there were fears that he
might be consumptive. The doctors decided that this suspicion was groundless, but
Felix appears to have been frequently ill at this period. Chest trouble was m the
family, for his brother William suffered from asthma.

For three years Felix pursued the normal routine at Serampore, adding to
his labours, however, by taking up the study of medicine. He commenced his
medical studies under a Dr Taylor, who afterwards went to Bombay to undertake
missionary work there; Dr Taylor "took considerable pains with Felix Carey, often
giving him lectures as early as 4 o'clock in the morning," and after Taylor's
departure Felix continued his studies at the Calcutta Hospital. He was, however,
evidently restless, for there was at this period a suggestion that he might be sent to
a mission station at Patna, and again an idea that he might, with one of the newly
arrived missionaries, set up a printing business in Calcutta; he also offered himself
for the arduous duty of establishing a branch of the Mission in China. But these
schemes came to nothing, and until the end of the year 1807 he remained at the
Mission's headquarters."
The opposition of the East India Company to the Mission's work still
persisted, and acute difficulties arose when two more missionaries. Messrs Chater
and Robinson, arrived in Bengal in the year 1806. Some other sphere for
missionary activity was clearly called for, and the Mission selected Burma for the
purpose. Great hopes were entertained of the possible results of this enterprise:
Burma was contiguous to China, Cochin China, and Tonkin, and thus might give
opportunities for spreading the gospel to the eastward. It was true that there were
difficulties to contend with; for one thing, the conditions of life in Burma were not
easy for Europeans, for even in Rangoon "bread, milk, butter, cheese, etc., are
scarcely procurable." However, Chater and another missionary named Mardon,
who had come to India three years before, undertook to go to spy out the land; and
on the 24th January 1807 they set sail across the Bay of Bengal to be the pioneers
of Protestant missions in Burma. The missionaries who remained at Serampore
took steps to second their efforts and a "Burmah pundit" was engaged at a salary of
Rs. 32 per mensem to begin translation work into Burmese so that tracts might be
prepared for distribution among the many traders who came to Bengal from Burma
along the coast. There appears to have been no suggestion at this time that
Felix should go to Burma; he is described as being fully employed in
superintending the printing press and in reading Sanskrit proofs.

The work which Chater and Felix had been set to do was, in effect, to
establish in Burma a counterpart of Serampore. Under Dr. Carey's supervision the
missionaries at Serampore were at this period engaged in activities of an
extraordinarily varied nature. Primarily they were devoted to the translation of the
scriptures; Dr. Carey had already translated the whole of the Bible into Bengali and
the whole of the new Testament into Sanskrit, and in the next few years was able
to effect translations into Oriya, Hindi, Marathi, Punjabi, Baluchi, Telugu, to name
only some of the languages in which he worked. The Serampore press was busy
printing and publishing these works. There was a free school for poor Bengalis,
and a Bengali Sunday School. In addition, each missionary was expected to occupy
himself partially in some form of labour which would produce contributions to the

The voyage to Rangoon was not unaccompanied by excitement. There was
danger from French privateers, a danger so acute that the departure of the Anna on

Island. Diamond Island, the missionaries recorded, was famous for its turtles, the
eggs of Which Were worth ten or twelve thousand rupees a year; some of the ship's
crew went ashore and caught about forty turtles, although they were the monopoly
of the King of Ava and killing them was prohibited. There being no news of
privateers, the frigate here left the Anna, which proceeded on its way alone,

In June, before the Judsons arrived, Felix had been sent for to go to the
King's court to undertake the vaccination of the heir to the throne. The local
government was ordered to provide boats and to pay his expenses. Unfortunately
he had no vaccine, and had to go without it; possibly, he suggested, he might have
to go to Bengal for some. However, his journey to Amarapura might be beneficial
to the Mission even without the vaccine, for he would take the opportunity to
mention the printing-press to the King and the Prince. If, as seemed possible, he
was required for the future to reside permanently at Amarapura, it would be well
for another missionary to be sent to Rangoon, as Kerr was proposing to leave.
Evidently he did not know of the approach of the Judsons, though they arrived
only shortly after he left. He did not return to Rangoon until October, when he
arrived full of glory in a "golden boat" in the character of "Rajah Sippey" or Royal
Doctor. He was very well pleased with his reception at the court. The King, despite
the absence of vaccine, treated him well, and showed some interest in the Baptist
Mission; he asked why the Baptists did not worship images like the Portuguese,
and Felix in reply quoted from the Buddhist scriptures about the impossibility of
forming an image of an invisible spirit. The answer pleased the King, and he gave
Felix "a gold medal and a title" and offered him rich apparel if he would assume
Burmese dress; but Felix refused, saying that he would willingly serve the King in
all ways, but that he could not change his dress or his religion. Dr. Carey wrote of
this episode that Felix "was received with rather more honour than he would have
wished for," though this may be open to some doubt in view of later occurrences.
"I have just received a note from him" wrote his father, "saying that he was well,
received at the Burman Court, and that he should be in Bengal in a few days.
Brother Judson, in a note equally short, says he was sent down in a golden, (gilt,)
boat, that there were orders to take up a ship on purpose to bring him to Bengal,
and to take him back again. I am left to conjecture about the whole of this affair.
Felix only says it may eventually be of great importance as it respects the
introduction of the gospel there. We sent a printing-press, types, etc., to the country
about a fortnight ago. I hope these movements are preliminary to something of
greater importance; at any rate, they are new things in the earth"-- from which it
may be deduced that Dr. Carey was some-what perturbed by these strange

shock. That Felix should be unbalanced would not be a surprising result of these
circumstances, and it is probable that stability of mind had never been one of his
characteristics. On the Other hand, it is possible that his conduct was merely the

restricted life at Serampore not to his taste; he had sought a freer field in Burma;

while the post of interpreter at Chittagong was not worth having. So he had
decided to return to Serampore to see his relatives and friends "until such time as
something may turn up," as he explained in Micawberesque phrase. There was a
vacancy in the post of assistant to the Salt Agent at Chittagong and he hoped that
his father might be able to get it for him. He was busy meanwhile translating tracts
into Arakanese for the benefit of the Mugs. The Mugs, he said, wanted him to
settle among them, but he saw no object in doing so unless he had some means of
earning a livelihood. "I however as you may suppose," he told his father, "can find
but very little pleasure in anything. I am completely tired of my unsettled
condition. I shall however leave my ways in the hands of God who does all things
well."

On the 15th April he arrived with Ward at Dacca on his way to see
William at Katwa and to go from thence to Serampore, where he would consult his
father "upon my future lot, my wife and children, etc., etc." He found the future
black. His letters to Tippera remained unanswered; the Raja of Cachar had been
driven out and Chaurjit had his country--"this change," he some what ungratefully
recorded, "will be all the better for the Country and our Government, and in a short
time much may be done there." All his plans had failed, and there seemed to be no
alternative but to abandon all hopes of glory and to seek once more the protection
of the Mission. But his father had plainly warned him that he could do little for
him, that Government would do nothing for him, and that the Mission viewed him
with little favour; "You say I can expect nothing from Government and nothing
from men of learning
Surely my case is hard." He was by now willing to undertake any

So he returned to Serampore, where his long-suffering father presumably
settled his debts for him. But the Brethren regarded him with no favour, and all
that could he done for him was to make use of his old art of printing. In October
his father wrote that; "We have employed Felix to assist in the operations of the
printing-office, particularly reading and correcting proofs." His name does not
appear in any lists of missionaries and he was apparently only a paid employee; his
father describes him as "assisting in the printing office, at a monthly salary."
There he remained till his death four years later. It is not unlikely that the period of
excessive optimism and enthusiasm through which he had gone and which had
ended in such disillusionment, was followed by a period of equally excessive
depression and sense of failure, which would render him incapable of spontaneous
work and capable only of work under the direction of a more powerful and stable
personality. In such circumstances, life at Serampore under his father's care and
guidance was the happiest fate that could befall him.
What happened to his third wife does not appear. While he was engaged in Cachar
she had been in Rangoon; he wrote that he would like to "get her round," that is, to
arrange for her passage by sea to Bengal, but perhaps had better wait until relations
between the Burmese and British governments were better, for the old difficulty
about removing women from Burma remained. It would seem that his wife was in
considerable difficulties; "upon hearing of my not returning, her friends have not
only forsaken her, but seem to have taken advantage of her forlorn condition. That
is Rangoon custom and not to be wondered at." It would also appear that, with the
generosity towards his family that never failed, Dr Carey. had been sending her
money. Whether she ever rejoined Felix is not known, but she certainly must

married for the fourth time. His fourth wife was a Miss Amelia Pope, and they

It is impossible not to be impressed by this record of work; the conclusion
can hardly be avoided that Felix was a man of very exceptional ability. It was his
tragedy that circumstances should have combined with temperamental weaknesses
to lead him into paths where his ability was wasted. He was one of the pioneers of
Burmese studies; to Chater must go the credit of being the first English-speaking
missionary to undertake the arduous labour of translating the scriptures into
Burmese and of printing them in Burmese; 12 but Felix, whose progress in the
language was slower, was second in the field and pursued the matter farther.
Felix was also the first to formulate a Burmese grammar for Englishmen, and the
first to prepare a Burmese-English dictionary (which formed the basis for Judson's
Dictionary of 182614). He was, too, a pioneer of vaccination in Burma. As a
missionary, he helped to prepare the ground for Judson, for though little had been
printed in Burmese when Judson arrived, he was still not in the predicament of
Felix and Chater of having no suitable books at all to study. Moreover, Felix by
helping to establish the Mission House and by familiarising the Burmese officials
with the Mission, eased the way for Judson.
On the other side of the account lie his renunciation of his pastoral status
and his abandonment of his mission to Burma. For this he incurs the reproach
always leveled at the man who, having set his hand to the plough, turns back. And

16 This was more than twenty years after Judson had commenced his work in

possible, in the light of the American Baptist Mission's condition twenty years

decision Felix was perhaps not so wrong as the Brethren imagined. Unfortunately
it was part of Felix's tragedy that he had not the stability which would enable him
to pursue single-handed the line of work which he now adopted; he needed
guidance, and once he had quarreled with the Mission that guidance was removed,
for he ceased to have any real spiritual contact with his father. Thus through
weakness of temperament and worldly ambition he was led into foolish behaviour
which admits of no palliation.
It is also perhaps to be considered that Felix laboured under another
disadvantage apart from his temperamental weakness; he was the son of Dr Carey,
and, being perpetually expected to reach the standard in work and character which
his father attained, was always judged and is still judged by a standard which must
remain inapplicable to any but the most exceptional men.
