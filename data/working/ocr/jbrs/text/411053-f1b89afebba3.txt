Introduction
Science and technology' of fibre may be regarded as one of the
most creative adventures of our time. No doubt, fibres were being
utilised for apparels and garments from the very early times, but this
utilisation was far from economic. The development at early stages
followed a pattern which was alien to the disciplines of science-nothing
to speak of technology which depends for its survival and development
on the result of scientific researches. As a result of investigations with
more and more developed techniques, the study of fibre has been raised
to the status of exact science.
Natural fibres are classified into two major divisions, (a) animal
fibre and (b) vegetable fibre. The latter in turn has been classified
into three sub-divisions, e. g. (a) seed fibre, (b) bast fibre, and (c) leaf
fibre. An extensive study on animal fibres has been made by investiga-
tors like Astbury, Pauling and others, (1), by employing X-ray diffraction
and allied methods. Vegetable fibres also, with the notable exception
of leaf fibres, have been thoroughly investigated by several workers. As
protein is the main constituent of animal fibres, so is cellulose in the
case of vegetable fibres. The cellulose content varies from fibre to
fibre. In cotton, a seed fibre, the cellulose content is about 98 %. In
bast fibres like ramie, flax, hemp and jute the quantity of cellulose
present is comparatively low, the percentages being 83, 78, 71 and 65
respectively. The second major constituent of bast fibres in lignin.
Jute, though one of the most important of bast fibres, has not received
careful study by (X-ray diffraction methods till Saha and Sircar (2) in

Swelling a phenomenon which plays a pre-eminent role in the
family of textile fibres-usually precedes almost all the cellulosic reac-
tions. The swelling of fibre on immersion in liquids may be either
intermicellar. Since natural fibres contain both crystalline and
amorphous regions, it is essential to know whether the swelling agent
penetrates the interior of the crystallites (i.e. intramicellar) or just
remains in between the crystalline areas (i. e. intermicellar). The study
of this phenomenon which throws light on the physical and chemical
properties of fibre could be made with a fair degree of accuracy by
employing X-ray crystallographic analysis. An exhaustive study of the
swelling of fibre in water and other chemical compounds has been
made by many workers. It has been shown Saha (2) in jute that
the swelling of this fibre in water is intermicellar and in NaOH it
is intramicellar. The most important of swelling phenomena is the
action of NaOH on cellulose, which is known as mercerisation. This
depends on the temperature and concentration of the mercerising
solution; the degree of mercerisation is more pronounced as the temper-
ature is lowered. That swelling in this case is intramicellar is evident
from the revealing differences between the X-ray diagrams due to raw
and mercerised fibres. It is well known that an 18% NaOH solution
brings about complete mercerisation of cotton, when it is treated with-
out tension at room temperature. As the mercerisation is accompanied
by an appreciable amount of shrinkage, sometimes about 17%, the
process adopted commercially in the case of cotton is to keep the fibre
under tension. By this process the fibre is only partially mercerised,
but the lustre and strength increase. The degree of mercerisation, can
best be ascertained by X-ray analysis, as shown by Saha in Jute
and Sisson and Saner in cotton. It has been found that the strength
of jute fibre decreases as the strength of mercerising solution
increases.

size of crystallites as well as their orientation with respect to the fibre
axis. A highly oriented fibre is supposed to be more crystalline. An

Textile fibres are built up of chain molecules formed by polymerisa-
tion of simpler units. The chains in fibre lie either closely parallel to or
along a spiral path about the fibre axis; the pitch and inclination
of the spiral vary from fibre to fibre. In ramie, the crystallites are
supposed to be arranged in such a manner that the orientation is
almost parallel to the fibre axis. This parallel orientation, howsoever
perfect from crystallographic point of view, is not really an ideal
configuration so far as the textile properties of a fibre is concerned. It
is true that ramie fibres have the greatest tensile strength of all natural
fibres, but they are brittle and weave into a relatively harsh fabric.
Perfect alignment thus makes for rigidity and good strength in the fibre
direction, but there is an associated brittleness in the transverse
direction. In cotton, the crystallites are distributed in spirals which
make an angle of more than 30° with the fibre axis. Though cotton
does not have the intrinsic strength of ramie, it is more resilent and
weaves into a softer fabric. It, therefore, appears that the spiral
arrangement is more favourable so far as the textile properties are
concerned. Astbury suggests that all fibres have their crystallites
arranged in spiral manner. He argues that even in ramie where the
alignment is supposed to be parallel to the fibre axis, the arrangement
is, in fact, in a spiral form. The spiral in this case is very steep, which
amounts to almost parallel configuration.
X-ray diagrams of natural fibres can be differentiated by the length
and the width of the diffraction spots or arcs. Visual examination
alone, without going into the final analysis, gives sufficient indication as
to how the crystallites are oriented. It also reveals whether a spiral is
steep or flat. It must be pointed out that a cellulose fibre having
crystallites aligned almost parallel to the fibre axis will produce an
X-ray pattern having well defined spots or spots very slightly drawn out
into arcs. But as the spiral organisation is more and more flat, the
reflections will be drawn out into longer and longer arcs. The degree
of fluctuation of the crystallites along the spiral, a lower degree of
lateral order, the small crystallite size and the presence of constituents
other than cellulose find their manifestations in the width of arcs. A
correct idea of the size and orientation of crystallites can, therefore, be

The X-ray diagrams of different cellulose fibres show that all
patterns consist principally of reflections in the form of spots or rings
equidistantly placed on the photographs and the dimension of the unit
cell has been found to be the same in all fibres. These reflections,
however, owe their origin to the crystalline region in the fibre. A
cellulose fibre is not all crystalline. In between the crystalline regions
are some amorphous substances known as "intercrystalline material ".
This intercrystalline material which varies from fibre to fibre is a
characteristic distinguishing feature of a particular type of fibre. The
finer details on the X-ray photographs are due to these intercrystalline
materials. A careful study of these details combined with the details.
supplied by crystalline regions provide a wealth of information
regarding the structure and properties of fibres.
In fact, X-ray analysis is one of the most effective and important
tools not only for determining the molecular structure, but also in
providing valuable information like the folding and unfolding of chains
reorientation and slippage of crystallites during elongation, the
mechanism of mercerisation, etc.
In view of the unwavering superiority of the X-ray method over
any other, and also of the fact that the leaf fibres have not yet received
systematic and exhaustive study by this method, the present investiga-
tion on a particular type of leaf fibre-Agave Vera Cruz-by employing
X-ray diffraction analysis has been undertaken by us.
Experimental
Raw leaves of Agave Vera Cruz, obtained from the Department of
Botony were retted as usual. The fibres obtained from the leaves were
thoroughly washed, dried, cleaned and combed before using for X-ray

analysis. Specimens for X-ray analysis were prepared in the form of a
bundle containing about 20 strands and each cut into 2.5 cm. in length.
The fibres of the specimen were made parallel to each other by applying
a little tension and were fixed on the specimen holder with the fibre axis
perpendicular to the X-ray beam. The specimen was next placed against
the X-ray beam emerging out of a circular slit of 0.05 cm. in diameter
and 6 cm. in length. Cuk x radiation, filtered through Ni foil, from a
Raymax 60 demountable X-ray tube operated at 36 kv and a tube
current of 14 ma was used throughout the investigation. The central
beam passing through the specimen was stopped by a small lead disc
fixed at the centre of the film holder.
A few samples were treated with 15% 20%, 25% and 30% Na OH
solution at room temperature and were washed thoroughly with distilled
water and dried. In order to study swelling in water a sample was kept
in boiling water for about an hour. Two samples, one raw and another
after the removal of lignin, were bleached by passing chlorine gas in
the fibre kept in water.
X-ray photographs of these samples were taken as before. The
physical properties such as intrinsic strength and fineness were
measured of the samples treated with different strengths of the
mercerising solutions.
All the X-ray photographs reproduced in this paper were taken
with Unicam single crystal goniometer with a film to specimen distance
of 3 cm. Calibration was made by superimposing the X-ray pattern of
a copper wire on the photographs.

Our X-ray photographs reveal that the (002) reflections are drawn
out into arcs which are longer than those in bast fibres. The length of
the arc in the case of raw fibre has been found to be about 60°. The
extension of this arc along the diffraction circle indicates that the
pattern corresponds to the diagram of cellulose oriented in spiral
configuration, the spiral making an angle of 30° with the fibre axis.
Figure 1 shows four fainter arcs diagonally on the meridian. They
correspond to the (021) reflections of cellulose and are not as sharp as
they are in ramie or flax. This diffuseness appears to be due to the
flat spiral in Agave fibre. A comparison of (002) reflection of Agave
fibre with that in cotton shows that the arcs are much wider in the
former. This pronounced width suggests that the size of the crystallites
is small. It also indicates the fluctuation of orientation of crystallites
around the spiral as well as a lower degree of lateral order.
The suggestion that lignin helps to retain the fibre strength gets
support from the X-ray photographs of two samples of Agave fibre
bleached under two different conditions. i.e. one bleached after the
removal of lignin and another before. The strength of the predeligni-
fied sample was much less than that of the other. The X-ray photograph
of the former, (Fig 3) particularly the (002) reflection, has been found.
to be as sharp as it is in cotton, which is almost a pure cellulose. The
spacings, however, remained unaltered on bleaching, but the pattern as
a whole became remarkably cleaner. The X-ray pattern due to the
latter specimen was not so clean. This indicates that more and more
of amorphous constituents have been removed in the predelignified
sample. The enhanced loss of strength in the predelignified sample,
therefore, seems to be due to the removal of lignin and other
amorphous materials.
X-ray photograph (Fig 4) of fibre kept in boiling water for about
an hour shows that the reflections are sharp, and the pattern as a whole
is cleaner than that of the raw fibre. This indicates the removal of
some incrusting materials which due to their scattering cause the extra
blackening of the photographs. It is to be noted that the (020)
reflection on the meridian is more clearly visible, and the length of the
(002) arc is less than that in raw fibre, e.g. 23°. No change in the

It can be seen that the raw fibre with spiral angle 30° has an
intrinsic strength of 23.5. When the fibre is treated with 15%, 20%
and 30% NaOH salution, the spiral angles are respectively 25°, 22.5°
and 15° and the corresponding intrinsic strengths are 14.1, 11.8 and 9.7.
It is to be noted that the spiral angle decreases with the decrease in
intrinsic strength. This finding is in contrary to that observed in
cotton, where the spiral angle increases as the intrinsic strength
decreases. It may be pointed out that this fibre, unlike cotton, contains
about 15% lignin and other polysaccharides as intercrystalline materials.
The lignin, as mentioned before, acts as a cementing material; and
though absent in some strong fibres like cotton and ramie, it adds
to the strength of the fibres, if and where it is present. It is
well known that on mercerisation lignin is removed, and as a
result an associated loss of strength is reasonable to expect. The
gradual fading of two reflections near the central spot of the X-ray
diagrams of mercerised fibres supports this view. That the crystallites
are better oriented as a result of mercerisation is evident from teh

It has not been possible for us to gather the details of difficulties
encountered by local manufacturers in the uses of this fibre in longyi
industry, except that the fibre is coarse. The fibres which are taken
by the manufacturers as single fibres are not really so. They consist
of a number of strands. The optical study has revealed that this
so called single fibre gets splitted into finer fibrils after a treatement with
sulphuric acid.
It is well known that the length of the micelles is closely related
with the strength of the fibre; the longer the micelle the greater is the
strength. The length and orientation of the micelles could be deter-
mined by following the method adopted by Saha (2). The intensity of
(002), (020) and (021) reflections will be measured with the help of
microphotometer; the length will be calculated by the application of
Laue (4) equation to the calculated intensity.
In conclusion, it must be mentioned that the present paper
embodies in it the results of our preliminary investigation. It will
however, be admitted that this preliminary investigation has not only
placed us on a much better footing for further work, but has also
opened up a number of avenues, which if systematically pursued, will
unveil a lot of mysteries. These will be of immense importance not only
from the point of view of fundamental research, but as well from the
point of view of industry. An extensive programme of research with
leaf types of fibres available in this country has been undertaken by us
and the work is in progress.
