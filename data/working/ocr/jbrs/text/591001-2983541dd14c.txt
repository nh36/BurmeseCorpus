MIND-BENDING BURMESE POEMS AND SONGS*
HLA PEŤ
1. Introduction
It gives me great pleasure to have to deliver another talk on Burmese poetry
to the members and guests of the Britain-Burma Society. Last time the subject was
the country life in Burmese literature. To ring the changes I shall direct my
attention this time to poems with a regal theme.
The title of my talk is 'Mind-bending Burmese poems and songs." It may
sound rather sinister, but I hasten to assure you that it has no connection what-
soever with psychiatrist's couch or casting magic spells on other people. It is in
fact only an essay on the potency of Poetry over the primitive instincts of man.
For this purpose I have chosen twɔ poems and four songs which, not so
much by virtue of their literary merit as by the dramatic or emotive circumstances
of their composition, tamed the passions in the breasts of their three royal
receipients -twɔ kins and a crown prince. The authors were court poets: they are
Let-wè Thon-dará, Ù Sá and the Princess of Hlaing.
Since all these poems and songs are from the classical or court poetry, which
flourished uring the monarchical period, 1044 to 1885, and since the examples are
in English, I would like to make a few preliminary observations on the poetry and
on the translations so as to facilitate your understanding and appreciation of the
subject matter.

Burmese classical poetry2 is esoteric and fully intelligible only to the
initiated. It falls under three main categories: poems in four syllable lines, which
are recited; songs in variable four syllable lines, which are sung; and drama in
mixed verse and prose which is declaimed. This poetry has four major themes-Budd-
hism, royalty, love and nature. Its style is elevated and its rhyme schemes are
elaborate. The poets were monks, kings, members of the royal family and
courtiers.
As regards translating Burmese poems and songs and dramas into English,
one can never stress enough the validity of the assertion that poetry is untranslatable.
The qualities of rhythm, of shapeliness, of the music of the words cannot be
directly translated from the original to the receiving language. Now to return to
the epoch making poems and songs and their authors.
2. The Authors and Their Peoms and Songs
2.1 Let-wè Thon-dará, 1723-1800³
Named Maung Myat San, this poet is popularly known by one of his titles,
Let-we Thon-dará. He was a comptroller of the royal household, a minister, a
court poet and a judge who served under several Burme e Kings in the late
eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries. He is the author of some fifteen poems
of different genres, including a legal treatise in verse; but his immense fame
among the populace in Burma rests largly on two short poems which have been
'immortalized' by the people.
In 1764 at the age of forty-one, while comptroller of the royal household
of the King Hsin-byu Shin, 1763-76, he incurred his master's displeasure. He
refused to hand over to the officials one of his attendants who had had the
temerity to poach to the preserve of the king: the man was implicated in having
an affair with one of the royal handmaids, and when caught redhanded he resisted
the arrest and escaped. The king meted out a rather harsh punishment to Let-wè
Thon-dará. The poet was exiled to Mè-za, a penal settlement in an unhealthy
valley over a hundred miles to the north of the then capital city, Kòn-baung, the
modern town of Shwei-bo.

Let-wè Thon-dará suddenly found himself in a limbo-like place. A former
courtier and a family man, he was now alone in the forbidding, cold wilder-
ness, deprived of all forms of comfort and the love and affection of his wife and
children. One does not have to strain one's immagination to picture the wretched
plight he was in.
Soon this unbearable existence began to tell on him and he wrote two
poems, cast in the form of yadú (lyrical poem) which consists of three stanzas of
verse with a conventional structure. He wanted to alleviate his ennui and to release
his pent-up feelings.
Let-wè Thon-dará sent the poems to his wife. They eventually reached the ear
of the king, who is said to have been moved by them so much that he immediately
ordered the recall of the poet. The unfortunate man had by then been fretting at
Mè-za for sixty-four days.
These two poems, although short according to Burmese standards, are fairly
long by Western ones. Each of the three stanza yadú contains approximately one
hundred four syllable lines!
To achieve a satisfactory result within a limited time I shall give only some
significant extracts with my comments and the same approach will be adopted in
dealing with the other songs.4

2.2 Ù Sá, 1766–1853
The second poet in our list is Ù Sá, Minister of Myá-wady. He served first
under Hsin-byu Shin's younger brother, King Bò-daw-hpayà, and later under the
king's grandson, Bá-gyi-daw, 1819-37. He was endowed with physical courage as
well as intellectual capacity.
Ù Sá distinguished himself as a general in the campaigns against the
Manipuris and then against the British during the First Anglo-Burmese War,
1824-26, when he was on the wrong side of fifty. He afterwards was appointed a
minister of the court for state affairs.
In spite of his heavy load of duties Ù Sá managed to find time to devote
his intellect to music and poetry, in which his extensive knowledge of the harp
played a vital role. He translated Thai 'Within-the-palace-great-drama' and wrote
into it appropriate songs chiefly pat-pyò (overture).
He attained great heights as a composer of classical songs (more than forty
of them are on record), and he adapted many Mon and Thai or Yo-dayà(Ayudhia)
tunes into Burmese. He is also reputed to have adapted into Burmese certain slow
measures of Christian sacred music, Bayin-gyi Aw-than (Roman Catholic hymns),
which he learned from the Catholic missionaries. No single Burmese poet has
done so great service to the Burmese music and classical songs as Ù Sá; but one
song that captures the people's hearts is a pat-pyò because of its emotive background.
A greatly gifted poet and an extremely capable minister, whom even the
British envoy at that time, Colonel H.Burney, held in high esteem³, Ù Sá had a
sad ending. It was the First-Anglo Burmese War, the one in which he gave a
good account of himself, that enmeshed him in the struggle for power between
Bá-gyi-daw and his younger brother, Tha-rawady. Bá-gyl-daw was a different
man at the end of that disastrous war. The reins of the government were gradually
taken over by his Chief Queen and her brother, Maung O, who later turned against
Tha-rawady. The prince took up arms against them and the king, and seized power
in 1837.

One day as the princess was being driven in a carriage with her mother, the
queen, she spotted Ù Så among a gang of prisoners who were mending the road.
The princess stopped the carriage, sent for the poet whom she deeply admired and
asked him how he had fallen into such a plight. He told her his story and added
that he had been in prison for more than two years. The princess suggested that
he should compose a song in his majesty's honour and send it to the proper quar-
ters and leave the rest to her. He did.
About a month later a dramatic performance was held at the palace, which
was attended by the king and his family including the princess. As part of the
performance Ù Sa's song was sung by the leading singer with the accompaniment
of an orchestra. The king was enraptured by its words and its tune, and duly
enquired the identity of the composer. The princess enlightened her father and no
doubt with tears welling up in her eyes she described the wretched fate of the once
great minister and famous musician. The king was filled with regrets and ordered
the release of Ù Sá.

Ù Sá, as already stated, was released. He was then more than seventy years
old. He wrote a few more songs, but the traumatic experience was too much
for him, and he spent the remaining fifteen years or so of his life as a recluse.
To those of us who are not on the same wavelength as the Burmese people
of the monarchical period, this pat-pyò may sound extravagant, grandiloquent and
far-fetched. But to those who grew up in the atmosphere of the eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries of Burma, the song was part of their normal life. The king,
I feel, was moved more by its tune and melodies, in which the quality of most
classical songs resides, than by its words.

2.3 The Princess of Hlaing, 1833–75
The third in the series is a lady poet, the Princess of Hlaing, or Hlain-
hteik-hkaung-tin as she is known to the Burmese scholars. She was, as already
mentioned under the life of Ù Sá, the only daughter of King Tha-rawady and
Má Myá Galei, queen of the Middle palace, and a celebrated lady poet. Born in
1833, her private name was Má Hpwà Gyi, she received the title, Thi-rí Maha
Thú-yadana San-da Dei-wi, together with the town of Hlaing as her fief from
her father when he ascended the thrown in 1837.

Kneeling before the Buddha
I prayed with special fervour,
Hoping that you would not be untrue to me;
(But now) karma, the law of causality,
Gives no help.
I am alone on my fragrant couch,
In misery,
With unquiet thoughts.
In the bower, under a mass of flowers,
You spoke sweet jwords:
How different you are now
From our early days.
Your honeyed words
Hid a hardened heart.
Now I know my lord,
In the Bee Throne chamber
I am overwhelmed with grief.
They told me, I know, (the truth about you)
But I paid no heed:
All my thoughts were to be with you,
Till the ashes of our bones blew to the earth;
And I turned my mind from the saying of the ancients:
This adage is
'Time till well,
Time will tell.'
