A CENTURY OF PROGRESS IN
BURMESE HISTORY AND
ARCHAEOLOGY.*
By
G.H.Luce
A hundred years ago the history of old Burma was
largely tissue of legend, its chronology wild, and even the
origin of the Burmans quite mistaken. The Burmans,
as we now know, like their predecessors the extinct Pyu,
descended from the north between Tibet and China into
Burma in the first millenium A. D. But culture and
Buddhism came both to Pyu and Burman from the west,
from India: and the lure of India was too strong. So
the older 'Pyusawhti' legend of Burmese origins, like
that of Nanchao in Yünnan, derived them from Asoka,
the Buddhist emperor of India. And when the connec-
tion of this legend with Asoka had worn thin, the new
'Dhajaraja' legend still traced them from the Sakiyan line
of the Buddha himself. On two points only, in this
matter of origins, the Burmese chronicles are clear and
correct: the first great capital of Burma was that of the
the Pyu-Srikṣetra or Old Prome, at the head of the
Irawaddy Delta; and the second was that of the Burmans-
at Pagan, below the mouth of the Chindwin.
Their chronology, as I have said, was wild. The
Chinese Buddhist pilgrims, Hsüan-tsang and I-tsing,
certify that Śrikṣetra, the Pyu capital, flourished in the.
7th. century A.D.: but the Burmans as carly_as_1098,
traced its founding to the supposed year of the Buddha's
death, 544 B.C.! The fall of Srikṣetra probably occurred
in the middle of the 8th. century A.D., and the founding
of Pagan in the 9th.: but most of the chronicles push
both back into the 1st. or 2nd. century, exalting thus the
dignity of the Burmese capital at the expense of the Pyu.
Much padding therefore was necessary to eke out the odd
seven or eight centuries. Indeed, roughly it may be said

that before the reign of Anorahta (ag) in the middle of
the 11th. century, nothing historical is to be found in
Burmese chronicles, except the bare names of a few of his
immediate predecessors, and a vague memory of the Pyu
capital. Śrīkṣetra.
Are they any better for the history of Pagan, from
Anorahta onwards?-Better, yes: but still far from reliable.
The earliest extant Burmese chronicle, the Yazawin Gyaw
(pom) of Shin Thilawuntha (560300300), is dated 1520,
little more than two hundred years after the fall of Pagan.
It has only a page about the Pagan period, and this is
mostly legendary or wrong. Two centuries later U Kala
(3)'s Chronicle, and three centuries later the Glass
Palace ($$$) Chronicle, (1829), have nearly 200 pages
on Pagan. Whence this spate of new information?-
Well, the kings had ordered histories: and kings have to
be obeyed. Tradition, long since moribund, had to be
artificially revived. The authors did their best with the
materials casily available: a very few inscriptions or copies
of inscriptions, dim local memories, legends and folklore.
They eked it out with fiction, stories borrowed from
Jatakas or other Indian books, with miracles, portents
and other moral and diverting stories, jealous always of
the monarch's pride, tender also to their own, who were
his ministers. As men, one can but sympathize with
them. As wise and witty authors, one admires them. As
historians, however, one is bound to note their limitations.
The regnal dates they assign to the kings, vary and
are often wrong, especially in the first half of the dynasty.
Their list of kings is incomplete, and the order sometimes
muddled. The names they give the kings differ usually
from those of the oldest sources. The characters ascribed
to some (at least) of them, are far from accurate. Thus
Kyazwa (3), whom the chroniclers portray as the pious
and incompetent scholar who forgot to rule his country
or to pay the wages of his pagoda-builders, because he
was absorbed in writing handbooks of metaphysics for the
ladies of his harem, we now know to have been a strong
and none-too-pious king, who strove to put an end to
theft and dacoity, and to restore the finances of the
kingdom. The family-relationships of the kings are
generally fictitious. One historical person is sometimes

split into two: or two are merged into one. Thus the
great Queen Saw (605) of the chronicles is a medley of at
least two Saws of history. Nor did she start as a farmer's
daughter, with the lowly if useful function of scratching
the king's back when he was itchy: she was, on the
contrary, the first lady of the land, sister of the late queen,
of royal birth on her mother's side, of high ministerial rank
on her father's. One can easily forgive the chroniclers
their naif love of the miraculous, and even their large
indulgence in fiction. What, personally, I find harder to
condone is the strutting bombast, typical, I fear, of the
period they lived in, but quite unlike anything in Old
Burmese.
A century ago this was all that could be called the
history of old Burma. The ancient capitals, Old Prome
and Pagan, seem to have been little studied, except by
treasure-hunters. But already a move had been made in
a more promising direction. King Bodawpaya, who died
in 1819, had determined to place on permanent record
details of lands dedicated to the Church, which were
thus exempt from taxation. The best evidence of such
dedications was the numerous stone inscriptions scattered
all over the kingdom. Orders therefore were given to
collect these stones and store them at his capital, Ama-
rapura, where they were to be copied, either in gist or at
full length. But this was easier said than done. The search,
whether by intention or otherwise, was not thorough;
for fortunately, the stones left in situ far exceeded
in number those collected. And one notices that small *
stones were removed rather than big ones. Still at least
600 stones were brought to the capital; and a few scholars
began to study them. One or two written in Pyu were
quite unintelligible. If they found any in Old Mon, they
would have been almost as baffling. The Pali inscriptions
were easiest; but they were few, and rarely historical.
The bulk were in old Burmese: and these were sufficiently
obscure. Script, spelling, grammar, syntax and vocabulary
have changed considerably in the past 800 years (though
not so much in Burmese as in Mon); and where words
remain the same, their meaning often has changed.
The least difficult were lateish copies of old originals, and

the early labours of epigraphists in Burma were vitiated
by prominence given to the least trustworthy inscriptions.
Of the rest the 'copies made were usually rough and
inaccurate.
Articles by Col. Burney, the British Resident in
Ava, in Asiatic Researches, and by James Prinsep in the
Journal of the Asiatic Society of Bengal, give a
glimpse of the state of epigraphical knowledge at the time.
The 1st. Anglo-Burmese war had ended in 1826 with the
Treaty of Yandaboo. In Jan. 1833, in connection with
this treaty, Burmese envoys. escorted by Col. Burney's
brother, had been sent from Ava to Calcutta. They took
the opportunity of visiting Bodhgaya, where they
discovered an old Burmese inscription fixed upside down
in a wall. Facsimiles were taken; and studied first by
the Burmese envoys at Calcutta, then by the Burmese
scholars at Ava who had recently compiled the Glass
Palace Chronicle, and finally by Ratna Paula and
Prinsep of the Asiatic Society of Bengal. A fair measure
of agreement about the general sense was reached, with
considerable error of interpretation; but the Burmese
envoys and the Ava scholars insisted on reading the dates
467 and 468, Burmese Era, while Ratna Paula and
Prinsep read them 667 and 668. The correct reading is
657 and 660. Clearly there was room for progress in
epigraphy before Burmese history could be properly tackled.
Scholars like the Twinthintaikwun, and the Monywe
Sayadaw might draw attention to discrepancies in dates
and details between particular inscriptions and the
chronicles; but they had not the time, the means, or (I
am afraid) the courage to collect all the inscriptions, to
perfect readings and interpretations, and then demolish
the jerry-built structure of the chronicles which they
had helped to set up.
For fifty years the subject was dropped. The chief
progress made in the middle of the century was the
scientific work of the Rev. Francis Mason, who in 1850
published the book usually known as Fauna, Flora and
Minerals of Tenasserim, enlarged in 1860 after the 2nd.

Natural Productions. In 1882 the work was brought up
to date by W. Theobald. These works are not historical;
but in their magnificent survey of Burma life in all its
varied forms, are indispensable as background. Mason
found a keen ally in Arthur Phayre, who came to Burma
in 1834, and published many papers especially on the
fauna of Arakan. In 1855 Phavre headed a mission to
the Court at Ava, of which his secretary Henry Yule,
published a splendid Narrative. One chapter of this
devoted to Pagan, where Yule, suffering from fever,
stopped only five days, is still the finest account in print
of the old capital. In 1867. after five years' service as
the first Chief Commissioner of British Burma, Phayre
retired. Long after, in 1882, he published his Coins of
Arakan, of Pegu and of Burma, and in 1883 his History
of Burma. Phayre was always a pioneer in scholarship
as in administration--and a most careful and expert work-
man. His History remained the standard work in English
on the subject until 1925, when Mr. G.E. Harvey's vivid
and humane History of Burma appeared. Phayre's
work, and to a less extent Harvey's, are based primarily
on the Burmese chronicles-an insecure foundation, as
we have seen, where the older periods are concerned.
Since the annexation of Upper Burma in 1886, the
rate of progress naturally accelerated. Questions arose
about the long Sino-Burman frontier; and E.H.Parker of
the Consular Service was called in to translate and
explain the relevant Chinese texts. Many of his papers
remained in the files of the Rangoon Secretariat; some
were published in the Chin Review and other journals;
and a general survey in his book Burma with special
reference to her relations with China (1893). A brilliant
all-round sinologue, Professor Parker was a great pioneer
in these particular studies, whose value, to Burmese his-
tory and archæology, is only second to that of the local
inscriptions. Most of the written sources about Burma
previous to 1050 are Chinese; and from then onwards
they remain a priceless secondary source. Their quality
is uniformly high; and as to quantity, you can fill book-
cases with them. As for the earlier Chinese sources, it
was Professor Pelliot, in 1904, in his famous article Deux

orientalist, the late Edouard Huber, we owe a fine study

Meantime, from 1881, archæology had made a
start in Burma. Our first 'roving' archæologist was a
German, Dr. Emil Forchhammer, who, if he had lived,
would probably have done in scholarship great things for
Burma. We owe to him a number of valuable reports-
on the Rangoon Shwe Dagon, the Pagan Kyaukku
Onhmin, and ancient Arakan; also preliminary notices of
Old Prome and other sites. Wherever he went, he took
rubbings of inscriptions. Some of these went to form the
first and best of the six 'elephant' volumes of transcripts
published by the Burma Government, Inscriptions of
Pagan, Pinya and Ava (1892); a rough English transla-
tion of this volume, by U Tun Nycin, appeared in 1899.
The publication of these transcripts was first, I think,
proposed by Sir Richard Temple, who for a brief period
acted as Archæological Officer, and who, with F.O.Oertel
and Taw Sein Ko, toured the Mon country of Lower
Burma in the early nineties, and published valuable
accounts of their antiquities. The six volumes, printed in
Rangoon between 1892 and 1913, contain about half of
the inscriptions hitherto discovered; but two of them are
merely 'Copies'. They are mostly grouped geographically,
not in order of date: but Duroiselle's List of Inscriptions
found in Burma (1921) furnishes a useful chronological
clue. Spellings are modernized: so they are useless for
linguistic purposes. Doubtful readings are rarely indicated
as such; and omissions and mistakes abound. This is
the less surprising when one remembers that rubbings
were then taken in negative, the stones themselves being
inked, and therefore had to be read through a mirror.
Forchhammer fell ill, and went on leave to Leipzig,
taking much material which he hoped to deal with on
recovery. Unhappily he died in 1890; and no one has
heard of the material since. A further heavy loss was
inexcusable. Another German, the geologist Dr. Fritz

In 1899 Burma suffered a still more ghastly 'blitz'
of German vandalism. A pseudo-Doctor Thomann,
with at least six German assistants, came to devastate
Pagan in the interests of the Hamburg Ethnographical
Museum. The vestibule of the Kubyaukgyi at Wetkyi-
in the pride and triumph of the Burmese painter's
art bears tragic witness to their handiwork, the ragged
ends and newspaper still dangling where they sawed
or half-sawed off the fresco. They ruined also four
small temples west of the Htilominlo, and stole numbers
of terracottas. Thomann was at length detected and
expelled, and part of his loot recovered and replaced on
wrong pagodas. But most of the frescoes that he sawed
seem to have perished; for very little is on show, either
in the Hamburg Museum or in his book Pagan which
he had the impudence to publish, in Germany. When
the war is won, I hope steps will be taken to recover
for Burma this stolen property. The images and
terracottas, at least, can easily be restored to their
pagodas.
Germans, alas, were not the only vandals, though
they were the worst. I have myself met more than one
globetrotter, bag in hand, engaged in 'souveniring' among
the ruins. There used to be statuettes and small objects
of dedication in the temples; one never sees them now.
Nor have the Burmese villagers been free from blame,

by 'repairing' them. They love to drown the external
stucco-mouldings and glazed carvings with lime white-

as soon as he has opened them in admiration.
In 1901 the Viceroy, Lord Curzon, visited Pagan
and Mandalay, and roused the Burma Government to a
sense of its responsibility to safeguard for posterity its
great heritage. The next year saw the real founding of
the Burma Archæological Department.
From 1902
onwards it has published its own Annual Reports, till
1926, after which they were merged in those of the
Archæological Survey of India. From 1938, on Burma's
separation from India, they were resumed. Forchham-
mer's assistant, Taw Sein Ko, a Chinaman, was the first
Superintendent; then M. Charles Duroiselle, a Frenchman;
and finally two Burmans, U Mya and U Lu Pe Win.
Readers of Duroiselle's review "Archaeology in Burma'
in his Report for 1938, will admit that they can show a
fine record of achievement, considering their meagre staff
and meagre funds, cut down in wartime to a minimum.
Year by year, as funds permitted, they undertook repairs
to one or other of the great buildings of Pagan or Prome.
Hereafter, if Burmans still possess unspoiled some worthy
heirlooms, the credit justly will belong to the Burma
Archæological Department. Qualms have at times been
felt in other departments at control passing from Euro-
pean to Burman. In archæology the change occasions
no alarm. Duroiselle assuredly has set a high standard
of scholarship: but Burma has never had a surer or more
patient scholar of its past than U Mya, who was for years
Duroiselle's assistant, tireless and self-effacing. U Lu Pe
Win also is an able scholar, who has already shown
energy and skill as archæologist.

In 1906 the French General, Léon de Beylié, a
great expert in Further Indian archæology, proposed to
make, at his own expense, some trial diggings at Srikṣetra.
The Burma Government approved, and Taw Sein Ko was
asked to assist him. A good beginning was made, recorded
in de Beylie's book Prome et Samara. Thereafter Taw
Sein Ko and his sucessors have pursued their excavations
at Old Prome with perseverance; and the India and Burma
archæological reports record a long series of discoveries. If
on the whole results have been less rich than might be
expected, it is simply that in 99% of the many mounds they
excavate, the treasure-hunter has been there first. Where he
bas not, as in Khin Ba's mound at Kalagangon, opened by
Duroiselle in 1926, splendid finds were made.
Apart from its own Report, the Department has
published about 90 books and articles in learned journals.
It has taken over 4500 photographs, and made nearly 900
architectural drawings. It has maintained a museum at
Pagan, the best in Burma (originally ordered by Lord
Curzon); a small one at the Palace, Mandalay; and a shed
and field museum at Old Prome. It has also gathered a
large collection of rubbings, including many new
inscriptions.
Among those found at Pagan, were the two duplicate
stones now (wrongly) called the Myazedi Inscription,
engraved about 1113 A.D. Each has four faces, in four
languages Pali, Pyu, Old Mon and Old Burmese. The
archaic Old Burmese and Pali faces were soon read, and
their historical importance realised as giving the regnal
dates of the king now called Kyanzittha, and so correcting
big mistakes in the chronology of the first part of the
Pagan dynasty. But it was left to C.O. Blagden, in the
Journal of the Royal Asiatic Society (1910 and 1911),
to read the Mon and Pyu faces and so lay the foundation
of two widely different fields of research. Dr. Blagden is
not only a brilliant pioneer and linguist, a Sherlock
Holmes in epigraphy: he has, I venture to say, very nearly
reached finality. Our debt to him is past all counting.
In Pyu, he went on to edit the Urn Inscriptions of the
Vikrama dynasty in Epigraphia Indica-a model for

Taw Sein Ko had published the six volumes of
transcripts already mentioned. Duroiselle started, on
much sounder lines, the series Epigraphia Birmanica,
where the most important inscriptions were to be edited
with facsimile, notes, transcription and translation. The
series has now reached Part II of Vol. IV: and it has so
far almost been confined to the Old and Middle Mon
inscriptions, mostly edited by Blagden. Mon, of course,
is a member of the ancient Austro-asiatic subfamily of
languages, speakers of which peopled Further India before
the coming of Tibeto-Chinese. In the book Pagan Races
of the Malay Peninsula, Blagden had already proved
the depth of Austro-asiatic civilizing influence in this
part of the world. Burmans themselves freely admit that
many of the arts came to them from the Mons, after
Anorahta's capture of the Mon capital, Thaton, about
1057. Now that Blagden and Duroiselle have edited
the Mon inscriptions of Burma, and Halliday and Coedès
the Mon inscriptions of Siam, we are beginning to gauge
the ancient greatness of their culture, so much superior
then to that of their conquerors. The first half of the
Pagan dynasty, for all its Burmese kings, was a Mon
period, with Mon temples, Mon sculpture, Mon painting,
and even the Mon language dominant at Pagan. It is
only in the latter half of the dynasty, especially the 13th.
century, that Burmese enters into its own.
In 1910 Mr. J.S. Furnivall, who has given to
historical and economic research all the time he could
spare from administration, founded the Burma Research
Society. Its Journal has done good service for Burmese
history and archæology, not least by uniting scholars,
Burmese and European, in the quest of Burma's past.
Of the former, perhaps the chief contributor has been
U Pe Maung Tin, Principal of University College, and
General Editor of the Society's Text Publication Series.
The Society has published many Burmese classics, and
a translation of part of the Glass Palace Chronicle.
On the side of archæology, its Journal records excavations
made in 1914 by Mr. J.A. Stewart at the old city of Pegu.

When Rangoon University was founded at the end
of the last war, Dr. P.J.Hartog, Vice-Chancellor of Dacca,
advised us not to attempt to build up a universal library,
but rather to focus our attention on Burma itself. This
wise policy has in the main been followed: I would men-
tion in particular Principal D.J.Sloss (who later became
Vice-Chancellor of Hongkong University) as having done
his utmost to build up Burmese studies. The University
Library, under U Khin Zaw's direction, soon became the
finest library in Burma: its Chinese section alone con-
tained 20,000 volumes. If I were here concerned with the
more modern periods of Burmese history, I could mention
a fine series of books and original research by our Pro-
fessors of History, Dr. D. G. E. Hall (a great scholar and
an inspiring leader, now Head Master of Caterham),
Prof. Pearn and Prof. Desai. A Readership in Burmese
History was created, which I had the honour to hold.
Apart from Chinese texts, my main concern-and that of
the Professor of Oriental Studies, U Pe Maung Tin,
who has all along collaborated with me-was the mass of
Burmese inscriptions. The first task was to find them,
then rub them, then safeguard them by publishing
collotype reproductions. Journeys all over Burma were
required, from Kengtung to Arakan. Maung Sein, my
servant, was sent on many a hunt by the University, and
discovered over a hundred new inscriptions. He became
so expert at taking rubbings, that a number of inscrip-
tions deemed illegible can now partially be read, for
instance those of king Makuta (misread Manuha) at
Thaton, and the Shitthaung Sanskrit inscriptions at .
Mrohaung, brilliantly studied by Prof. Johnston of
Oxford. The Oxford University Press has published, for
Rangoon University, three large portfolios of the
Inscriptions of Burma, chronologically arranged. Two
more are now in the press, completing the literature of
Burma down to the founding of Ava in 1365. It remains
to edit the inscriptions published. The University's
collection of rubbings was probably even better than that
of the Archæological Department, though perhaps not
quite so complete. It is sad to think that both of these
great collections have now fallen into enemy hands.

When the India Society did me the great honour
of asking me to write a book on "Ruined Cities of Burma",
I suggested U Lu Pe Win as my collaborator, since
the Archæological Department has the best collection of
photographs and drawings of our antiquities. With the
kind help of our Chairman of Council, Sir Francis
Younghusband, the then Governor of Burma Sir
Archibald Cochrane, and the Premier U Saw, this was
arranged. Lu Pe Win was to provide the illustrations,
and I the text. We were both well advanced in the work
when the Japanese began invading Burma. Passing
through Mandalay last Feb., I hoped to collect most, if
not all, of the plates. But bombing was expected at any
moment; and Lu Pe Win was prudently engaged in
moving the more precious parts of his collections,
including all his negatives, to the comparative safety of
Pagan. As soon as this was done, he promised to
forward his contribution by air-mail direct to the India
Society. I regret to say that I have had no further
news of him: and the plates have not been reached.
But I have not yet given up hope.
Finally I would call your attention to a greater
work, of fundamental importance, to which Rangoon
University, the Burma Government, and the Burma
Research Society have all contributed-the new Burmese-
English Dictionary compiled by Dr. J.A. Stewart and
Mr. C.W. Dunn. The first part is now on the point of
publication. Apart from its immense value as a work
of learning, it is a fine instance of cooperation between
the Burmans and the British, being based on half a
million slips collected by 150 Burmese scholars, including
many monks, and about 25 non-Burmans. I sincerely
trust that this great work will be continued without
delay; for not only are educated Burmans and orientalists
eagerly awaiting it, but also the compilers are no longer
young and it will be long, I fear, before we find their
equals as scholars of Burmese.

by briefly showing you, through the epidiascope, a few
