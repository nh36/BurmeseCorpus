MAYAN-LAJUNG, CHANGING LAND USE AND CAPITAL

by
ROBERT E. HUKE✶
The two villages studied in this paper show surprising contrasts in their rates of
development and their adaptability to more intensive resource utilization. The one village
has apparently profited, at least in the eyes of Western oriented researcher, from a series
of fortunate geographic, economic, and cultural phenomena while, over the same eight
year period, the second village has made little progress.
Mayan and Lajung are two Kachin villages located roughtly 25 miles west of the
town of Myitkyina in the Kachin State of Northern Burma. Mayan is located at the
junction of the Burma Railways Mandalay-Myitkyina line and the Ledo Road of World
War II fame. Lajung lies 5 miles south and west of Mayan and is connected to Mayan
by a trail, passable over part of its length by jeep.
From Mayan the movement of both people and goods east via the rail line or the
road, west and south via the railway, or northwest via the Ledo Road is relatively simple.
Movement of people or goods to or from Lajung on the other hand involves a difficult
haul over 5 miles of jungle trail to reach the lines of easier transport.
Mayan lies in a constricted valley structure which varies in width from one to two
and a half miles and is about four miles long. The surface of this basin-like area is
moderately smooth with only minor irregularities. Soils on these bottom lands are, at
least in part, fluvial in origin, they are deep, moderately well drained and rich. By
contrast, Lajung lies in the narrow valley of a tiny tributary of the Mogaung River. Level
or moderately sloping land is restricted to a narrow belt along the river northwest of the
village. On three sides of the village steeply sloping mountains rise abruptly to heights
from 1500 to 2000 feet above the settlement. Soils along the Mogaung River are alluvial
and very poorly drained, soils on the mountain slopes are thin and sometimes subject to
slump and moderate erosion.
Mayan is a relatively young village whose first settler moved here in 1930 from
Mankrin, a village seven miles north of Myitkyina. Mayan grew only slowly and at the
time of the Japanese invasion of Burma in 1942 there were only seven houses in the village

Land Use Patterns in 1952
In 1952 two distinct types of farming were being practiced by the people of Mayan;
taungya and wet rice. Of the two, taungya was by far the most important. It was the taungya
that produced the food, and the materials needed for the manufacture of clothing by the
villagers. The paddy lands produced a moderate surplus of rice for several families. This
surplus was bartered within the village or sold for cash in Myitkyina. The cash income, in
turn, provided a large portion of the luxuries enjoyed by the villagers. Of the forty
families in Mayan, 37 operated taungya plots while nine supplemented their taungya with
wet rice farming.
The 1952 taungya plots were centered on the southeast slopes of a rugged 1600 feet
hill two and one half miles northeast of the village. In all cases the plots were well above
the base of the hill at the 500' contour and most were grouped between the 750 and 1000
foot contours. Almost all of the taungyas in use were concentrated in this area to facilitate

The location of the taungya groups and of the individual plots within the groups
appeared to have great significance. Much thought and planning, some of it pseudo.
scientific and much of it superstitious was given to the choice of each area to be farmed.
These methods as well as the techniques used in taungya cultivation have been covered in
previous papers and will not be discussed here.
The largest taungya plot observed in Mayan measured approximately 30 acres. This
plot occupied the lowest slopes of two hills and the narrow valley between them. Slopes
varied from 10 per cent in the valley bottom to 60 per cent on the hillsides. These 30
acres were shared by eight Mayan families. There were no fences or stakes to demarcate
one plot from the next, but each family had some steep slope and some are of gentle slope
close to the stream.
The eight families sharing this plot represented 46 people. Thus an average of
two-thirds of an acre per person. However, one of the families sharing this plot also
operated an area of wet rice and operated a correspondingly smaller taungya than did
the remaining seven families who worked not wet rice. Thus, an area of somewhat more
than .66 of an acre was planted for each member of those families operating no wet rice.
The second largest taungya in Mayan was roughly square, measuring some 220 yards
on a side or about ten acres. This plot was similar to the above plot in that it included
the sides of two hills together with the tiny valley between them. This plot was farmed
by a single large family of fourteen persons; again slightly over 2/3 of an acre per person.
These two plots were separated from each other by 500 yards of uncut timber.
To the east, south and west of these two plots were other of the 1952 taungyas, each
separated from its neighbor by anywhere from 50 to 500 yards of forest. The abandoned
1951 taungyas were on the east slopes of another small mountain two miles south east of
the village, four miles from the 1952 site.
Normally the taungyas were no larger than was sufficient to feed the family planting
them. However, in several cases, families were found farming about one and one-half
acres for each member. These were families with no wet rice. When questioned about
the size of their holdings, they reported using the excess production to the barter with
other villages for such necessities as sault, or transporting the surplus to Myitkyina to sell
for cash.
Two-thirds of an acre of taungya, in the Mayan-Lajung area, produced about 22
baskets of unhusked rice or 800 lbs. of cleaned grain in a normal year.
Four hundred fifty lbs. were required to feed one person for one year. An additional
seventyfive lbs. per person was used for feasts, one hundred fifty lbs. per person used for
feeding the chickens and pigs, and the remainder, about one hundred twentyfive lbs. was

The farmers in Mayan reported little erosion on their hill plots. The steepsloped
taungya at the southeast edge of Mayan provided an excellent opportunity to observe the
degree of erosion after the first season of farming. On this slope few signs of gullying
were found. Sheet erosion, however, had been active. In places where fallen logs were
lying along the contour of the hill, soil dams were found on the upslope side of the log.
These dams measured three to four inches in depth. Where large tree roots were exposed
along the contours of the hill, the level of the soil on the upslope and downslope sides of
the roots differed by two to three inches. At the ends of the fallen logs, tiny alluvial fans
were noted. It is estimated that between one and two inches of topsoil were removed
during the first year of operation of this particular field.
Of the nine families with wet rice holdings, only one farmed paddies in the vicinity
of the village. This holding of 18 acres was located one half mile northeast of the village
center and occupied one of the few areas in the vicinity of the village considered level
enough for wet rice production. Most other areas would have required terracing with
level differences of a foot or more between fields. The villagers did not consider such
hard work worth the effort. Eight holdings, averaging nine acres each were operated in a
valley bottom 11 miles east of the village. The men operating these plots were away from
the village for several days at a time during periods of heavy work. In all cases, families
operating wet rice holdings also had taungya plots and the yield from the paddy fields was
used mainly for sale or barter.

Each home in the village had, somewhere nearby, a small garden producing spices
and often one or more fruit trees. Several of the village families supplemented their
income by hauling the best of the timber from the surrounding hills to the railway line
with the help of elephants. Such timber was then sold through appointed government
agents.
Family incomes in Mayan village ranged from a high of Kyats 2,000, about U.S.
$420 to a low of Kyats 3, U.S. $0.63. Several families reported incomes between Kyats 40
and 60 ($8.40 to $12.60). Each family had minimum expenses of Kyats 3 to pay the
government tax assessed on every house. Other money available was normally spent for
such items as salt, cloth, tobacco, fish paste, kerosene, or steel blades.
Certainly life in Mayan during 1952 was close to the subsistence level. Most people
had ample food, adequate clothing, and relatively good housing. Few had any luxuries.
The situation in Lajung was similar. Agriculture was mostly of the taungya
variety with less emphasis on paddy fields. Area farmed per capita was slightly less than
in Mayan. There was no selling of timber. Income per family was less than at Mayan
with several families reporting only Kyats 3 per year, used for tax purposes. Any excess
cash available was often used for the purchase of additional opium. Diets were less varied
than at Mayan, clothing was not as good, and the longhouses provided cramped and
uncomfortable housing.

Mayan-1960
Between 1952 and 1960 Mayan showed a surprisingly rapid rate of population
increase. During this eight year period the original Baptist settlement of 40 homes had
grown to include 133 homes. In addition, the settlement had expanded in two new
directions. At the northeast corner of the village, where there had been no homes in 1951,
there were 31 homes in 1960. These were nucleated around a Catholic Church built in
1958. At the northern edge of the original settlement, where there had been no homes in
1952, there were 17 longhouses in 1960. This shoestring anamist appendage to the village
was oriented along a foot path between the Mayan railway station and the Ledo Road.
The total population of Mayan had shown the phenominal growth of 350% in eight years;
from 250 persons in 1952 to 1,154 persons in 1960.
Land Use Patterns-Mayan 1960
The most remarkable change in land use was the shift from an almost putely
subsistence agriculture based on taungya to a heavy emphasis on cash cropping
based on extensive plantings of sugar cane. In 1960 there were 226 individual
operators of sugar holdings, representing one or more producers from over 90% of the
village families. In 1952, by contrast, there had not been a single cane producer. Together
these 226 producers were cultivating 1018.58 acres of cane, almost 4 times the entire
cultivated acreage of all crops in 1952. The average size of the holdings was 4.51 acres
with a range of from 1.20 to 30.44 acres. The great majority of operations fell in the
3.50 to 6.00 acre range.
Yields per acre in Mayan average 17.8 tons of cane and range from 13 to 31 tons.
The average is slightly below the average for all of Burma, it is roughly the same as that
in Cuba or the Philippines but only 1/3 that of the Java average.
The gently rolling land to the north, east and southeast of the village has been
brought under cultivation, land which in 1952 was covered with tropical grasses, bamboo,
and scattered trees, land which was not then used by the people of Mayan. In 1960 about
50% of this land had been reclaimed and was productive. The remaining land in this
direction will prove more difficult to cultivate as it is either swampy or steeply sloping.
However, north and northwest of the settlement there appears to be adequate land avail-
able for an approximate doubling of the area under sugar cane.
A few Mayan residents had had some experience with the production of sugar cane
in the immediate pre-World War II period when nine farmers in the settlement produced
cane to be shipped to the small mill at Sahmaw, 30 miles southwest of Mayan. This was

In 1956 pineapples were brought from Maymyo, a hill town some 300 miles to the

Between 1952 and 1960 the importance of taungya at Mayan had decreased consider-
ably. Whereas in 1952 93% of the families had depended upon taungya for a major
portion of their income, by 1960 only 80 families or 43% had any taungya holdings and
most of these used their hill gardens only to supplement other sources of food and income.
In almost every case the taungya plots measured considerably less than the 2/3 of an acre
per person necessary to provide for all of the needs. The variety of plants in each taungya
was also far less than was noted in 1952. Fibers, oil seeds and tobacco were among the
most obvious missing items.
It appears that with the passing of time taungya cultivation will become less and
less important in Mayan. Two farmers stated that after the present season (1960) they
would no longer cultivate taungya because it is very hard work, exceeded in difficulty only
by production of pineapples.
The 1960 season found about 200 acres of taungya under cultivation. The average
hölding for each of the 80 families involved was only 2.5 acres. The plots involved were
in three distinct locations; northeast of the village near the 1952 sites, and on two steep
hills some 3 miles southeast of the village, hills which in 1952 hsd been covered with
virgin forest. No longer was the work of cutting, clearing, burning and weeding shared by
the majority of the families in the village but now was shared only by those families,
usually five or six, sharing a single clearing.
Wet rice, too, had declined in importance in Mayan. Only 21 families or 12% had
such fields in 1960 compared to 9 families or 23% in 1952. Some 210 acres were so farmed,
about 80 acres of which were located in a level low-lying area 2½ miles west of the village
while the other 130 acres were located 11 miles east of the settlement in the same place as
the majority of the 1952 paddy farms.

Sugar and Pineapple-1960
The cycle of work on the sugar fields starts almost immediately upon completion of
the harvest, sometime between December and April and continues for 11 months. During
the height of the growing season the plantations must be weeded twice or even three times.
Families with holdings of two or three acres usually do all of the work themselves with
the possible exception of the cutting and tieing at harvest. At this time one or more
laborers are often hired to speed the process and assure meeting the delivery schedule
demanded by the mill.
After the land has been originally cleared it appears to take about 75 man days of
labor per acre to trench, plant, weed, cut and bind the cane. At the going wage of Kyats
2.50 per day this represents a cost of Kyats 187.5. Further costs for transportation of the
cane to the rail siding, land tax, and the purchase and cutting of plant canes and binding
material add Kyats 130 per acre. With the average yield per acre being 17.8 tons and the
price at the rail siding set by the government at Kyats 30 per ton it can be seen that the
income per acre averages Kyats 534. The apparent profit per acre per year is somewhere
in the vicinity of Kyats 217 or U.S. $46.
Mayan has been fortunate to have conditions of physical geography favorable for
the production of cane. The soils of the valley are deep and rich, having developed on
quarternary alluvial deposits. To date the yield of cane have shown no decline year by
year despite the complete lack of chemical fertilizers. The soils are also well drained and
have not had to be ditched to improve the drainage.
Sugar cane is quite demanding in terms of ideal climatic conditions for its growth.
It is very susceptable to low temperatures; frost at any season but particularly at the
harvest is very damaging; little growth takes place when the temperature is below 50°F.
Optimum temperatures for vegetative growth lie in the vicinity of 90°F. But considerable
lower temperatures are desirable during the ripening season. Moderate moisture is
needed by the young plant; considerable water is necessary during the period of rapid
vegetative growth; the period of ripening should be relatively dry to promote a high
sucrose content and to ease the transport of the heavy cane from the fields. Conditions in
Mayan appear admirably suited to the requirements of cane. Temperatures from Novem-
ber through February average 66°F. with the mean low at 54°. By May the average
temperature has risen to the high 80's only to drop to the low 80's and High 70's from
June through October. Rainfall, as recorded at Mogaung 12 miles west of Mayan shows

growth and is in need of moderate moisture. In Central and Southern Burma irrigation

Rainfall for the months from November through early May results from the
passage over the Kachin State of a series of disturbances moving from west to east. The
origin of many of these disturbances can be traced to the North Atlantic and the Mediter-
ranean. Their paths can be followed across the Middle East, south of the Himalaya and,
after picking up additional moisture in the northern portion of the Bay of Bengal, into
Northern Burma. Rainfall produced by such disturbances is of great importance to the
sugar producers of Mayan.
Pineapple is a newer crop than sugar in Mayan, raised by fewer people on a much
smaller acreage.
Data on labor demand and costs per acre are less precise. It is obvious
that labor demand is very high and that the cost of cultivation is considerably above that
for sugar. In addition, much work is demanded during the hot season and, to the farmers
at Mayan, the work is less agreeable than that needed for taungya, wet rice, or sugar.
Pineapples are planted near the end or after the rainy season, sometime between the
end of August and the middle of November. They are harvested 22 months later between
July and September. They must be hoed and weeded several times, both during the cool
season and during the hottest months. This is difficult and unpleasant work according to
every farmer who has done it.
A compilation of estimates from several farmers indicate that the cost of cultivation
averages about Kyats 300 per acre per year for this two year crop. Roughly 120 man
days per acre per year are put into the production of pineapples. Most farmers report a
yield of 3,500 fruits per acre and these sell at a price of 2 pineapples per Kyat. The
apparent profit is thus Kyats 575 per acre per year, about 2½ times the profit from
sugar cane.
Taungya, too, is difficult and time consuming work but, perhaps because of tradition
and long experience, is far more enjoyable than pineapple cultivation to the farmers of
Mayan. Work in the taungya starts in February or March with the cutting of the larger
trees and continues intermittently until the harvest is completed, usually in December.
Approximately 120 days of labor are required for each acre so farmed. Forty days are
taken to cut, burn, and clear the fields as well as to build the ubiquitous taungya hut.
Planting, weeding and harvesting together account for about 80 days of labor. Not all of
this labor by any means is done by the husband. He does all of the felling of large trees

Income-Mayan 1960
Cash income in Mayan has shown even more remarkable gains than has the
population. In 1960 almost every family reported earning of Kyats 1000 or more and
at least two families had incomes of something over Kyats 10,000. The shops carried
many goods not found in 1952. Items found in every shop included such things as:
clothing, woolen blankets, pots, china, a variety of foods including canned goods and
bread, cookies, coffee, and tea, pressure lanterns and metal chests.
Most villagers were wearing Burmese clothing of machine made cloth in contrast
to the home woven cloth and War surplus clothing of 1952. Many farmers wore wrist
watches and carried flashlights. Most homes were lighted by pressure driven mantle
lamps rather than the cloth wick in a condensed milk can so common in 1952.
While precise figures concerning cost of living, costs of cultivation, profit, savings
or investments were difficult to define, it is quite obvious that there had been a marked
increase in both the real income and in the level of living at Mayan. Income figures are
perhaps more impressive when it is realized that, even in Rangoon, the Consumer Price
Index had actually moved downward a fraction of a percent during this eight year period.'
Lajung-1961
By 1960 Lajung had shown far less change than had Mayan. On first approaching
It, the settlement could easily be recognized by photographs taken eight years earlier. In
1952 there had been 13 longhouses in the village and by 1960 this number had grown to
only 14. The village site had not been shifted in the interval nor had the houses changed
in appearance except for a new cover of thatch here and there. The village had one new
family which had migrated from the far north and the total population had increased from
roughly 104 to about 120.
Nor had land use shown the profound changes apparent in Mayan. taungya was
still by far the most important form of farming. Two distinct groupings of taungya plots
were observed. Together these plots measured 90 acres or almost exactly 3/4 of an acre
for each person in the settlement. This represented a far larger per capita acreage than
found in Mayan where the per capita holding in 1960 was less than 1/5 acre.

of the village between the 750 and the 1,000 foot contours of a rugged mt. In 1960 about
of the plots were lower on the slopes of the same mountain and centered only mile

Neither pineaples nor household vegetable gardens were observed at Lajung. No
other agriculture was being carried on.
Per capita income in Lajung had increased markedly since 1952 but the rise was far
from as spectacular as the rise in Mayan. Several families reported incomes on the order
of Kyats 300 but it must be remembered that families in Lajung average over 8 members
per household, two more than at Mayan.
Lajung supported no shops and the air of prosperity, so evident at Mayan, was not
present in Lajung. Clothing was somewhat better than it had been in 1952 but in other
respects the village had made very little progress and few changes were obvious.
Conclusions
The fortunate association of several geographic, social, and economic conditions at
Mayan have made it possible for that village to show marked development between 1952
and 1960. Easy transportation was available. Education was available and in demand.
Soils, topography, and climate were favorable for sugar cane production. The newly
built government sugar mill at Namti was easily accessable. It appears that a combination
of these factors made possible the impressive changes at Mayan.
Lajung on the other hand shared these advantages only to the extent of having the
same climate as Mayan. Transportation was more difficult and education was not in
demand. Soils are thinner and topography not as favorable for cane as at Mayan. This
combination of circumstances has apparently prevented Lajung from developing as rapidly
as its more fortunate neighbor.
