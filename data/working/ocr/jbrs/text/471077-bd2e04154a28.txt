THE BURMESE MODIFICATION OF THE BERNREUTER
PERSONALITY INVENTORY
U Sein Tu*
Introduction
The present research was designed to develop a paper-and-pencil personality test
applicable in the Burmese socio-cultural context and standardized on a Burmese population.
The paradigm and concepts utilised in this study are in the psychometric and psychologi-
cal test tradition. The basic conviction of the test-author is that the development of
objective psychological test instruments which are properly designed and validated consti-
tutes an important and necessary step in the application of universalistic and impartial
principles to the problem of selection and placement in universities, armed forces,
government agencies, industry, etc. (7,8,9). In the absence of a long-range plan to fulfil
this need, a modest grant was obtained from the Burma Research Society! to do prelimi.
nary work on one of the more important psychological tests currently being used in the
West, namely the Burnreuter Personality Inventory.(5)
The Bernreuter Personality Inventory
The Bernreuter Personality Inventory is a paper-and-pencil personality test intended
for use on populations ranging from children of Grade IX to adults. The original
Bernreuter consisted of 125 items organized in the form of a questionnaire which could be
self-administered by a normal adult subject in a period of between ten and twenty-five
minutes. Each of the 125 items could be responded to in one of three ways, namely:
YES, NO, or? These 125 items were selected from an item-pool which drew upon four
other personality measures, namely the Allport Ascendance-Submission Reaction Study,
the Bernreuter Self-Sufficiency Test, the Laird C₂ Introversion Scale, and the Thurstone
Neurotic Inventory. (4,9)
From inspection of the item-content Bernreuter devised, on an apriori basis, scoring
keys for 4 personality traits for his test, namely, Neurotic Tendency (B,N), Self-Sufficiency
(B2S), Introversion-Extroversion (B,I) and Dominance-Submission (B4D). The Bernreuter
scales were then correlated against the original scales developed by Thurstone et.al, and
intercorrelations ranging from .67 to .94 were obtained. These intercorrelations, however,
are spurious measures of validity in view of the fact that subjects will tend to answer the
same questions in the same way regardless of the context in which they may be presented.
They may be more appropriately regarded as indices of reliability.

Within the Inventory itself too, it turns out that the individual traits do not possess
the status of independent aspects of personality. Appreciable interscale correlations were
obtained between Introversion (BN) and Submission (B4D); and between Self-Sufficiency
(B2S) on the one hand and Extroversion (BI) and Dominance (BD) on the other; while
Introversion (BI) and Neurotic Tendency (B₁N) were found to be practically identical
(r = .95). (3,6,9,10)
Flanagan (6) subjected the 125 original items of the Bernreuter Personality Inventory
to factor-analysis and came out with two factors which accounted for 96 percent of the
variance in the original items. Factor I was identified tentatively as Self-Confidence, and
Factor II as Self-Sufficiency or Independence. Persons who score high on Factor I tend
to be sure of themselves, reliant on their own ability, and possess a high level of tolerance
for criticism; while low scores on this trait tend to be self-deprecatory, to feel timid and
inferior, and to possess little capacity to withstand criticism. Factor II measures the
degree to which a person tends to do things by himself rather than seek solace in sympathy
of friends and companions, and to solve one's problems by oneself instead of seeking
advice or help.
Each of the four original Bernreuter traits, (B₁N), (B₂S), (BI), and (B,D) were
found to consist of these 2 basic factors Self-Sufficiency and Confidence in varying propor-
tions, together with some minor unidentified factors. Thus "Extroversion" was found to
be made up of Self-Confidence with the addition of some Self-Sufficiency items.
Flanagan's two factors have the added advantage of being orthogonal of factorially
independent of each other.
The Burmese Bernreuter
The psychologist who intends to adapt a standard psychological test to his own
cultural milieu has the choice of following either of two courses of action. First, he may
take a test, translate the individual items, and publish the result as the completed test.
This verbalistic method is employed by persons who have no basic understanding of the
procedures and underlying rationale of psychometrics, possesses little merit, and need not
concern us further. The other course is to study the form and spirit in which the original
test was built, and to practically construct a new test, beginning with the creation of an
item-pool of test questions applicable to one's own socio-cultural setting, and following
the original test construction procedures step by step through item-analysis, reliability
testing, validation, and final standardization on a new population. The latter is the standard
order of procedure and is greatly to be desired provided the time and the facilities for
such thoroughgoing test reconstruction are available.
In a modest test-program such as the present one, however, such facilities are usually
wanting, and such time-consuming work is for the most part entirely out of the question.
For the psychologist in this situation, a third strategy is available. This is to take
a reasonably long original test, translate the test items, and, using the translated list as the

modified test, we shall be left with a short form of the original test which has been

The value of such a procedure is enhanced when scales representing more or less
factorially pure traits are translated and adapted, since the derived test items in each scale
may themselves be subjected to further factorial analysis to determine whether they do or
do not hang together in the modified scale and in the new culture. This is essentially the
method that has been followed in the development of the Burmese version of the Burn-
reuter Personality Inventory.
Flanagan's factor-analysis version of the Bernreuter Personality Inventory, consisting
of 50 items comprising two factors, Self-Confidence and Self-Sufficiency or Sociality, were
first translated into terms meaningful to Burmese subjects. The translated items were
then administered to a group of 125 Intermediate Part (B) college students. Item-scale
correlations were then computed for each factor taken separately, and items were selected
on the basis of the extent to which each individual item correlated against the whole scale.
This analysis left us with twenty items on the Self-Confidence Factor (Factor I) and
fourteen items on the Sociality or Self-Sufficiency Factor (Factor II). These two scales
were then incorporated into a single test format as shown in Fig. I and designated the
Burmese Bernreuter Personality Inventory.

Reliability
Internal consistency reliability for the Burmese Bernreuter Personality Inventory
was computed from the average inter-item correlation, and found to be .87 for Factor I
(Self-Confidence) and .79 for Factor II (Self-Sufficiency). This indicates that the items in
each translated scale are in fact measuring the same single trait, even when applied to a
Burmese population.
That the scales are measuring meaningful factors which are independent of each
other is also indicated by the negligible correlation of .0682 between Factor I and
Factor II.
Reliability coefficients were also computed for the two factors. Retest reliability
calculated from readministration of the Sociability scale after an interval of 12 days to
115 B.A./B.Sc. (Part A) college students was .85. The Confidence scale, when given to
109 B.A./B.Sc. Psychology students on two occasions, again with an interval of 12 days in
between, yielded a retest reliability coefficient of .74.

Summary
The present paper reports the results of an attempt to modify the Bernreuter
Personality Inventory to suit Burmese conditions. Flanagan's factor-analysed version of
the Personality Inventory was translated, and two scales representing two orthogonal
factors, Confidence and Self-Sufficiency, were prepared. Of the original 50 items in
the Flanagan Bernreuter, an item analysis based on item-scale correlations eliminated 16
items, leaving 14 in the Self-Sufficiency scale and 20 in the Confidence scale. These
remaining items were then subjected to reliability and validation analysis.
Internal reliability coefficients of .87 and .79 were obtained for Factors I
(Confidence) and II (Self-Sufficiency) respectively, while retest reliability coefficients were
.74 for Factor I and .85 for Factor II.
Validation of the Burmese Bernreuter Personality Inventory against externa]
criteria (self ratings and classmates' ratings in a group of high school students) yielded
moderate positive validity coefficients ranging from .31 to .37, indicating the need for
caution in applying the test to individual cases. Validation with use of sophisticated
raters, however, raised the validity coefficient on Factor I to .81.
The two factors, Confidence and Self-Sufficiency, turn out to be consistently
independent of each other in the Burmese samples tested, one study revealing a negligible
positive correlation of .0682 and the other a negative correlation of -.0496 between the
two traits.
Norms for use of the Burmese Bernreuter Personality Inventory with college
students have been prepared on a University of Mandalay student sample and are presented
in the text. Separate norms exist for the Rangoon University college population, having
been developed on a sample of 618 Rangoon University students, and may be
obtained by writing to Professor U Hla Thwin, Department of Psychology, Rangoon
University.
