1.1. The meaning of 'malapropism'.

3. The importance of criticizing
U Lu Pe Win's article
It gives me considerable pain to be so
critical of U Lu Pe Win's article. He was
one of my teachers at the University of
Rangoon, and he has been regarded by
many Burmese people as a leading scholar.
I have been compelled to take this course
for two reasons: (1) because his article
has appeared in the Journal of the Burma
Research Society and (2) because he is
intending to contribute more articles to
the Journal (p. 23).
States of America
-
(1) The Journal is looked upon as an
organ of Burmese scholars' views by other
scholars in many parts of the world. These
scholars in China, Russia, Germany,
Czechoslovakia, France, and the United
are taking a more than
passing interest in Burmese Studies. In
London, Burmese language, literature and
culture are being studied by scholars at an
advanced level in the University. All these
scholars are hard to convince. Their
knowledge of Burmese many not be as wide
as that of U Lu Pe Win; they not however
gullible. They find it most difficult to
accept many of his statements, theories and
conclusions.
Reactions of some of these scholars to
his article so far has been extremely un-
favourable. Some treat it as a joke, others
shrug their shoulders or shake their heads,
and the rest wonder why such an article
ever appeared in the Journal. I hope the
points I have made will have a salutary
effect on U Lu Pe Win's future contribution.
(2) His thinking as revealed in his article
is still redolent of the 19th century. It
smacks of the flavour of the Vohāra-linattha
Dipani 21 (Etymology of Words) by Hlethin
Atwin-wun in 1830.
27:
The Atwin-wun's treatment of his sub-
ject matter was at any rate less dogmatic,
and perhaps comparatively more reasona-
ble. Admittedly many of his theories are
not sound, since they were based upon the
false assumption that the Burmese language
was an off-shoot of Sanskrit or Pali and
accordingly he derived almost all the
words from one or both of these classical
languages. But he was one of the pioneers
in this branch of linguistics, which was then
still in its infancy. He was educated in the
monastery where competency in religious
knowledge naturally took precedence over
secular learning. He lived in a 19th century
Burma. We can expect his horizon to be
rather limited.
U Lu Pe Win has had a different
training and back-ground. (See JBRS, vol.
XLVIII, pt. 1, 17). These are also days in
which the subject of linguistics has made
such progress that it is quite difficult to keep
abreast of it, and knowledge at one end of
the world is now accessible to any one at
the other end. There is no excuse for him
in the nineteen sixties, as there was for
Hle-thin Atwin-wun in 1830, to be so paro-
chial and outdated in his views.
Every scholar is conscious of his res-
ponsibility to work for advancement of
knowledge. The purpose of contributing
a learned article or writing a serious book
is either to re-interpret old knowledge or
discover new knowledge with its added
values. Whatever message he wishes to
give, a scholar is expected to convey it in a
style that is simple, clear and brief. Usually
he should also give reasoned statements,
relevant examples and close arguments for
conclusion or conclusions he arrives at. In
