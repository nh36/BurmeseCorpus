warp. It was only in about 1938 that people began to use Kanazo for
building purposes on an increasing scale as a result of encouragements
given by the Forest Department, though in Bengal this timber has been
freely used for general construction for many years past. Almost all
the sailing boats use Kanazo posts as masts and booms. Madama and

(i) Inland sandy level forest type consists usually of areas a few
acres in extent. Fresh drinking water is available here. The principal
species are characteristic of an ordinary hill forest comprising Kanyin
(Dipterocapus Sp.), Thingan (Hopea Sp.), Thabye (Eugenia Sp.),
Thabyu (Dillenia Sp.) as the principal tree species. Salu (Licuala
Sp.), Minbaw (Caryota Sp.), Kazaung (Oncosperma Sp.) are to be
found as undergrowth.
(ii) Sea face sandy level forests are prevalent along the sea
coast. They extend in width from a few yards to several hundred
yards, and are mainly covered by grass. On older beaches tree growth,
in small patches, is made up of Sit (Albizzia procera) Myaya
(Grewia Sp.) and Thabye (Eugenia Sp)

(iii) Clay high levels are lands which have risen above the
ordinary Kanazo level and are flooded only during the rains. On
lower ground big kanazo trees with with little or no regeneration are to
be found while elsewhere Sinnin thayet (Mangifera Sp.), Bamwa
(Litsaea Sp.), Thabye (Eugenia Sp.), Pantaga (Meliaceae), Te
(Diospyros Sp.) typical of ever-green type of forest which either form
an understorey or have taken the place of Kanazo. Among the
undergrowth Thin (Clinogyne Sp.), Danon (Calamus Sp.), Thinban
(Hibiscus Sp.) and creepers may be mentioned.
(iv) High Intermediate type are the highest of the true mangrove
swamp forest.
Here aerial roots of kanazo are only a few inches
high depending on depth of flood water. Other species are Bamaw
and Pantaga with Myinga (Cynometra Sp.) as an understorey.
Thabaw (Pandanus Sp.) is also fairly common. Where the water is
more salt, as in the east of Pyindaye reserve, Thayaw (Excoecaria
Sp.) and Madama with poor scattered Kanazo are to be seen. The
ground is often covered with Hngetkyidaung (Acrostichum Sp.)
(v) Low Intermediate levels form the optimum level for growth
of Kanazo. In the north and the centre of the block of reserves,
trees growing up to 100/110 feet, with a girth of 6 feet at 6' 6" are
to be seen while in the south the height would average about 70 feet.
Kanazo grows almost pure. Its associates are Pantaga, Sagalun, Kyi
and Sauk. Occasional growth of Byu-u-talon, Kyana and Madama
seen in the north become profuse in more salt areas in the south.
(vi) Fresh water low level type is found on mud banks along
the islands in the north with Laba (Sonneratia Sp.) as the principal
species.
(vii) Low level salt water type is found further south and alon
the sea face with Byuchidauk (Rhizophora Sp.) and Kanbala
making up the main tree crop. They skirt along banks (of streams
and island) and are only a couple of hundred yards wide.

8. Since 1924 the method of treatment has been the Burma
selection system with a general girth limit of 4' 6" at 8'6” above
ground. Annually certain compartments are opened to working and
trees which have reached the exploitable girth limit are marked and
removed.
The annual area yield laid down in the working plan is arranged
in annual coupes totalling about 10 square miles. Before the war about
60 firewood extraction camps with a labour force varying between
2,500 and 3,500 men and women were distributed over the extraction
area. Each camp was in charge of a trader who was known as a
firewood broker and who was responsible to the forest officer in charge
for working any sub-coupe allotted to him. All the 60 brokers joined
the Broker's Association. At the association meetings grievances were
ventilated and differences among brokers, workmen and other local
problems were discussed and settled under guidance of the forest
officer in charge. To keep this labour force in hand, boat races and
football tournaments were organised with the approval of the civil
authorities. The organisation had worked so well that in about 1938
the Commissioner appointed a special headman to look after the
extraction camp and the Government also provided a doctor to look
after the health of a population which totalled about 7,000 souls.
9. I have briefly described the general description of the area and
the organisation employed to exploit these tidal forests. I would like
to touch now briefly on our problems in the management of these
forests. Our knowledge of the growth of the principal tree-Kanazo-
is very limited. It is estimated that it takes a kanazo tree about
310 years to reach a girth of 5 feet at 8' 6" above ground. Experimental
sample plots were first formed in 1928. Systematic measurements
could be taken, as far as I remember, only in 1934 and again in 1938.
We require more information on the Silviculture of Kanazo.
I do not think we know enough as to how to treat the kanazo
forests. Kanazo in early life appears to like shade and comes up in
groups. The present method of selection some time resulted in large
gaps being left.
Where optimum conditions prevail regeneration is abundant and
seedlings about 5/7 feet high and totalling 1,200/1,500 per acre could
be counted but elsewhere the picture is different.

To my mind the poor type of kanazo forest a few miles north of
the sea face will, with the gradual extension of the land southward,
one day improve in quality, reach optimum growth and then when the
land has risen high enough the Kanazo type will in turn be succeeded
by an evergreen type. I am inclined to think that there should be
variations in method of treatement of the kanazo sub-types.
Research is no doubt required.
10. Prewar the pressure on the reserved forests from landless
cultivators was considerable, especially in case of areas which have
risen high above ordinary flood level.
Such high areas are decidedly suitable for cultivation and during
1934 some 17 square miles in the northern part of the Pyindaye
reserve were in process of disforestation. Further disforestation of
20 square miles in the Labutkwe reserve has been in progress. The
present forest area was estimated in 1923 to be insufficient to produce
the quantity of firewood required for local supply. If firewood has to
be brought from hill forests the rise in cost will disturb the economy of
the rural population. As cheap firewood is under existing circum-
stances a necessity, I do not think it prudent to disforest any more areas
for several decades to come. For the future I think all new islands
should be reserved.
11. This brings us to the question of treatment for high level
areas which have been or are about to be succeeded by an evergreen
type. It may be that artificial regeneration with sugar cane or other
vegetable crop is the answer if we wish to accelerate production of
valuable tree species. Here too further experiment is necessary.
12. There is also the question of elimination of waste in exploita-
tion. Rejected branch pieces and butt ends amount to about 40-50 per
cent of the timber a tree could produce. When the Irrawaddy steamers
were run with wood fuel some 15 lakhs cubic feet of firewood were
supplied annually from wood waste.
Small experiments were carried out in 1937 to see whether the lop,
top and butts could be converted into charcoal. The results were not
satisfactory. Further research is indicated in order to find uses for
timber now left behind.

13. I have mentioned the encouragement given by the Forest
Department for using kanazo as a general construction timber. The
timber is hard and strong, but is liable to wrap. It is probably more
durable than Kanyin but people prefer to use Kanyin because it is
easier to convert and process. Research into the right type of saw for
conversion and cheaper method of seasoning will go a long way to
meet the needs of the people in the delta towns and villages.
