THE EXCAVATIONS AT HALIN *
Myint Aung**
The main problem of Burmese archaeology is to fill
up the gaps in the curly chapters of Burmese history and
to correlate its pre- and proto-historic periods with its
known periods. The solution of this twofold problem has
been the aim of Burmese archaeology since the inception
of the Survey. With this aim in view, therefore, the
excavations at Beikthano, a Pyu site in Central Burma,
were undertaken. These excavations established for it a
chronology ranging from the first to the fifth centuries
A.D. This date is some-what anterior to the known date
of another Pyu site with almost the same cultural
assemblage as Beikthano. This is Thare-khittara also
located in Central Burma and its known date ranges
from the fifth to the eighth centuries A.D. 2 Hence it
gives rise to a problem that there should be a link which
may correlate the two aspects of Pyu culture found at
Beikthano on the one hand and Tharekhittara on the
other.
It seemed that Halin, a site in Shwebo district, would
provide the missing link. For Halin was known to have
yielded some artifacts similar to those found in the
excavations at Beikthano and Thare-khittara. Besides
these artifacts of archaeological interest, Halin also
possesses, like both Beikthano and Thare-khittara, the
remains of city walls. Thus it was chosen for detailed
investigations after proper explorations in it in 1961-62.
The site and its environments
Halin (lat. 22°28' N. and long. 95°49' E.) lies in the
administrative jurisdiction of Wetlet township in
Shwebo district in Upper Burma. It is situated some 10
miles 5 furlongs to the south-east of Shwebo and 6 miles
to the north-east of Wetlet. Lying within the Dry Zone,
Halin enjoys only a scanty rainfall, less than 30 inches
per annum. The soils in its environs are heavy clay and
marshy. The upper surface of the soil is saturated with
sodium chloride and on this account Halin is one of the
centres of local salt industry. Besides salt fields Halin
has rice-lands mostly watered by the Nagayon tank on
the south-east of the city and Halin lake on the west.
The ancient site enclosed by the fortifications on
four cardinal points and their respective ditches, occupy
a little elevated area immediately to the north of the
present Halin village. The ruinous fortifications form a
rectangle with its longer sides orientated north-south and
shorter sides, east west. Each longer side measures
nearly 2 miles while each shorter side is a mile long. The
average existing height of the fortifications does not
exceed 5 feet from the ground level at several places.
Inside the enclosed area are srewn the low brick mounds
and most of them are too low to recognize as such at a
glance. The lowness of the mounds is owing to the
repeated ploughings of the area for dry crop cultivation.
The fortified area fails to show the existence of any
standing religious structure, modern or ancient, in it. Nor
does it show any modern habitation except one or two
temporary watch-men's hut's amidst the plantations.
Cutting diagonally across the fortified area is the bed of
a dry stream which runs SW-NE. (Map).

Historical background
The two stone inscriptions in Pyu language found at
Halin in 1904 and 1929 may probably contain some
accounts of its historical events. The first stone was
dated to fourth century A.D. by Dr. Blagden¹ and the
second to about seventh-eighth centuries A.D. by U
Mya, 2 the ex-superintendent of the Survey. But the
subject matters of these inscriptions are still shrouded in
obscurity as they could not be deciphered satisfactorily.
The standard Burmese chronicles such as U Kala
and Hman-nan yazawins do not mention Halin as a Pyu
city. But one or two allusions to the site are found in U
Kala's Chronicle in connection with a love episode of
King Naratheinkha of Pagan period. ³ Besides we come
to know that the name of the site was spelt "Han-lin" in
some stone inscriptions of Pagan period.4
Somewhat fuller account of the site is found in a
thought provoking local chronicle recorded in a literary
work of late eighteenth century A.D.5 This is in verse
form written by U Aung Hpyo (ca. 1768-1803 A.D.)б, a
scholar in the reign of King Bodawpava in Konbaung
period. He mentioned in his work that Halin was first
founded by one Karabaw, a prince from India who
migrated to Burma long before the ministry of Lord
Buddha. In order to supply his city with ample water,
The Excavations at Halin
Karabaw attempted to dam the river Irrawaddy. But he
failed in his efforts. Instead he constructed a tank at a
spot on the east of the city and it is known as Nagayon
tank today. After Karabaw Halin was ruled by a line of
799 princes up to the time of Pyu Min and Pyone Min,
the two brothers. The reign of Pyu Min and Pyone Min
first proved to be prosperous and the people under their
rule were happy and contant. But as a result of
misunderstanding between them Pyu Min Killed his own
brother. This caused the city to rise in revolt against the
King who was Killed eventually. In the chaos
conflagrations broke out and the city was reduced to
ashes. Thus came the end of a once flourishing city.
It is difficult to prove the truth of all these statements
by excavations. But there can be little doubt that Halin
was once a big city with its fortifications, gates and
residential areas. These can be seen as brick mounds in
different stages of ruin all over the city. Along with
these structural remains were found Pyu symbolical
coins, potsherds, beads of various materials, gold
ornaments. stone inscriptions and a sculpture all datable
to a period ranging from before the fifth to the ninth
century A.D. Taking together these shreds of evidence, it
becomes obvious that Halin can serve as a link in
correlating the two aspects of Pyu culture brought to
light at Beikthano and Thare-khittara in Central Burma.
Hence the excavations at Halin.

Structures and stratifications
The excavations at Halin wore con-ducted in five-
successive season from 1963 to 1967 and the following
structural remains were laid bare.
Fortifications
Some part of the southern fortifications, were
exposed at site HL-4 and it was found that the wall has a
breadth of 29ft. 6 ins, with 35 courses of bricks in situ in
its northern face and 21 courses in its southern face. The
bricks were laid in mud mortar.

stratifications show that the wail is supported by a thick

The alignment of the southern wall was found to
have a curve at the south-eastern corner when trenches
were laid at HL-18. Here the breadth of the fortification
is only 16 ft. with 8 courses of bricks intact. The inner
face of the curve has a guard room measuring 15 ft. by
14 ft. 6 ins. It may be generalised from the available
evidence that the fortifications assume a rectangle with
its corners rounded and guard rooms attached inside the
curves.
Gateways
According to the local chronicle Halin city had 24
gates originally. But all these could not be verified as
the limited excavations are intended only to solve one or
two main historical problems. Hence the choice for
digging fell on 3 selected gateways in HL-10, 11 and 17,
(Plate-1), Each of these three is found to have a width of
17 ft. flanked by 2 arms bending into the habitation
areas. The first gateway at HL-10 piercing thro-ugh the
southern wall, faces south; the second at HL-11 cutting
the eastern wall, faces east and the third at their corner in
HL-17 faces south-east. Their layouts are similar to each
other.

But the south-eastern gateway is there important

Here is a vindication of the Halin chronicle which states

Residential structures
The area enclosed by a rectangle near the centre of
the city is traditionally taken as the palace site by the
local people. When the excavations were commenced,
this area was given first priority. But the first two
seasons of work at HL-1,2,6 and 7 failed to bring out
any substantial structural evidence except a few
disconnected brick walls of small dimensions. These are
parts of the rectangle which encloses the area, Although
the first attempts at revealing the habitation patterns of
city-life were not much rewarded, they should not be
taken as entirely fruitless when artifacts like potsherds,
beads, personal ornaments, etc. were encountered in the
digs. All these together confirm that this was a habitation
area even if no large-scale building activities took place
here. Hence it became necessary to concentrate on it at
the next attempt.
This attempt was made accordingly in 1965. This
time it was able to unearth a fairly big assembly hail
measuring 120 ft. by 38 ft. at HL-9. The hall takes on a
rectangular plan with its longer walls aligned NE-SW
and its shorter walls, SE-NW deviating some 19° 56'
towards west from the magnetic north.

Pottery
The excavations at Halin brought to light a red-ware
industry with a variety of pottery types. These include
wide-mouthed storage jars, medium-sized pots and
cooking vessels, shallow dishes, small sized pots, basins,
drinking cups, lids, handle, oil-lamps, and so on. All the
pottery is wheel-turned and plain. But some of the sherds
have decorations in simple criss-cross, hatch, punch-
mark, roulettes, human and animal figurines in penals,
srivatsa, baddhapitha and floral motifs. Unlike these
varieties of designs on the domestic pottery, the burial
pottery has few decorations. They are simple urns with
tapering sides and flanges either near the bases or the
mouths. Some of them have lids shaped like typical
Burmese betel box. A few of them are bowls with covex
sides and plain sharpened rims.
The classification of fabrics shows that the potters at
Halin used three kinds of clays: fine lavigated clay for
well decorated wares; medium clay for ordinary wares of
daily use and coarse clay for storage wares and burial
urns (Figs. 4, 5, 6)

Antiquities
Of the antiquities yielded by the excavations, paste
beads assume the majority being found practically in
every site. They have different colours: white, black,
blue, red and green. As they are too minute for
description, detail treatment of them is not possible.
Next in quantity to the paste beads come terra-cotta
beads. Most of them have plano-covex, truncated conical
and round shapes. (Plate-6) There are also beads of
agate, quartz and green stone. They assume a variety of
shapes: round, hexagonal, circular, barrel, etc. Three or
four stone beads are etched. Along with stonebeads are
found a few stone pendants. Some of them are shaped in
stylized elephants and a dog. (Plates 7 and 8)
A seal in agate bearing a Pali title in embossed
letters reading dayãdãnam is the most important find
ever made in the excavations (Plate-9 a). It can be dated
to the fifth century A.D. on paleographical grounds.
Similar seals were reported from Cambodia and Malay
peninsular.1 (Plate-9b). They were also dated to the
same century.
Along with these objects are the Neolith c ground
and polished implements in different kinds of stones.
The common types among them are adze and ring-stone.
There is not enough evidence to decide their place in the
Halin cultural assemblage.
Gold objects in the forms of plain finger-rings, beads
and pendants are also found at some sites in the
excavations. (Plates-10, 11 and 12). They disclose that
the Pyus at ancient Halin were well-to-do to afford to
wear precious personal ornaments. Symbolical coins in
silver or a kind of alloy are also brought to light. They
may be classified in-to three types: type 1 has the
symbol of a couch in srivatsa on the obverse and a
baddhapitha
on the reverse; type 2 bears srivatsa flanked by swastika
and baddhapitha symbols on obverse and the symbol of
rising sun on reverse, and type 3 is represented on the
obverse face by a srivatsa symbol which encloses 9 dots
in 3 rows containing 3 in each and a baddhapitha on its
reverse face. These symbols may have some religious or
ritual significance. (Plate-13). Similar coins were also
reported from Cambodia.1
Iron objects unearthed by the excavations can be
grouped into two categories: domestic tools and
defensive weapons. The domestic tools consist of
knives, chisels, adzes, tweezers, spoke-shave, door-
sockets, spades, linked chain, nails and many other
indeterminate objects. The defensive weapons are
represented by swords, diggers, spear heads, axe heads,
arrow heads, caltrops with four spikes and a missile or
two. (Plates-14, 15 and 16).
The inventory of bronze artifacts includes mirrors
with handles, rattles and daggers. A piece of coarse cloth
is found sticking to the blade of a dagger and it offers
evidence that weaving was in vogue in Halin period.
Human skeletons
The excavations at Halin have laid bare a number of
human skeletons and disarticulated bones in piles buried
at a gateway (HL-17). These are being studied and no
final report is available as yet at the time of writing this
paper. But the former professor of Anatomy at the
Institute of Medicine (1) had reported after his
preliminary studies of these skeletons at the site that the
bones have an average height of between 5 ft, 4 ins. and
5 ft. 6 ins Bone diseases like T.B. and osteo-arthritis
seem to be absent or rare in these bones. Teeth in most
of the skulls are found to be in excellent condition. (Fig.

General observations
On the whole it is observed that the Pyus at Halin
city were a literate people with a high standard
civilisation. They knew architecture, town-planing, art
and some other technologies. A sect of Buddhism with
no idolatry seemed to have flourished among them as
evidenced by the stupa-shaped lid of a vessel, the plans
of the tombs or ritual structures and the seal bearing Pali
title daydãdãnam.
Their inscriptions, pottery types and architectural
styles definitely point out that they had close intercourse
with the Pyus of Beikthano and Thare-khittara in Central
Burma. Their rouletted pottery and structural layouts
indicate that they might have established relations with
south-eastern India. There is also a tangible proof in the

The paleography of some letters inscribed on a few
potsherds, of the stone seal and of the inscriptions in Pyu
language, indicates that the Pyu civilisation flourished in
Halin from the fourth to ninth centuries A.D. This can be
pushed back to about one or two centuries earlier by the
Halin architectural plans bearing a close resemblance to
those of Nagar-junakonda in south-east India which are
dated to third-fourth centuries A.D.1 This dating is again
coroborated by the analogy of the rouletted pottery
found in Halin and Arikamedu its type-site in India. The
rouletted pottery at Arikamedu is in fact dated to about
the second century AD.2
The above archaeological datings are confirmed by
the age determination of charcoal specimens by
radiocarbon analysis which gives the following results:

Charcoal from the site HL-17 1740± 60 years B.P.3

sixth century A.D., HL-10 to second century A.D. and
