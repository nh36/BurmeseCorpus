Section 1. Introduction
A basic economic characteristic of an urban community is that it
is a fairly large population living in a continuous settlement, without
directly producing its own food supplies. This has made the degree
of urbanisation in a country dependent on a number of conditions,
the more important of which may be listed as follows: (a) a
sufficiently advanced agricultural technology: (b) adequate water
supply; (c) an adequate system of communications and transport
with the hinterland which supplies its basic food stuffs and raw
materials; and (d) a social and enonomic organisation to ensure the
stable existence of a large sized population. These are the pre-condi-
tions of urban life. Given these pre-conditions, the actual development
of urban settlements, their location, and their economic and social
character depend further on other conditions of the underlying economy
and the people.
At a period which may be dated over two centuries ago, these
conditions existed in different parts of the world more or less uniformly,
so that urban populations were distributed all over the world roughly
in proportion to total populations. Since that period, important
changes have occurred in the conditions of agricultural and industrial
productivity and in the nature of international commerce. The increase
of agricultural productivity which occurred during that period has
made it possible for a larger proportion of the world's population to
leave their agricultural occupations: particularly in the advanced
countries of the west, the progress of international trade by which
Europe was able to live on the food surpluses of the rest of the world
has permitted increasingly farger numbers to live in cities and engage
in non-agricultural occupations. At the same time, the development

Recently the United Nations Economic, Social and Cultural Organisa-

East on this very subject. 3 This paper is an attempt to present the

and to pose the problems raised by this phenomenon for further study
and research particularly by social scientists. In order to view this
phenomenon in its proper background of world events and world
history, a brief summary is first given of the history of urbanisation in
the world generally, and particularly in South East Asia over the last
one and a half centuries.

Behind this statistical picture of world urbanisation,
important distinctions should be noted. Just as the industrial city is
the typical city of modern times, the importance of cities in medieval
and early modern times both in Europe and in Asia was based on
somewhat different characteristics. They tended to be royal cities,
religious cities, forts or great market cities in the interior or at the
ports. By way of summary, these various characteristics may be
divided into the categories of cities performing "central" functions,
commercial functions or other miscellaneous functions. A typical
example of a central city has been Paris, with its rather slight indus-
trial basis Such cities existed in most parts of Asia as well. These
cities thrived when they did by the frank exploitation of the rest of the
country, by the might of the sovereign or the nobility. On the other
hand, the great Italian cities, for example, flourished on the basis of
their profitable commerce; their locations depended on the extent of
the lines of communication and transport which served their trade.
Such differences in the economic bases of the early cities had an
important consequence on the nature of their cultural patterns. The
culture of the central and royal cities was based on the leisure classes,
with whom cultural fashions originated, but did not spread to the
masses. The commercial cities played a greater part in the spread of
cultural patterns, mainly by providing for the interchange of ideas
between the different countries with which they carried on trade.
It is within the last 150 years that a new type of city began to
dominate the scene, the industrial city, and it is the rise of the
industrial cities which constitutes the main feature and the greater
part of western urbanisation during this period. Often these industrial
cities were new and not developed out of earlier towns, for their
growth depended on availability of new kinds of industrial raw
materials and sources of power. Often, the new cities reached larger
sizes than the older cities, even when these old cities turned to large
scale industry. The culture of these cities was therefore less influenced.
by tradition and although weighted down by the stresses and strains of
a developing industrialism they were at least free to evolve their own
values in the arts and sciences. Even more important, these powerful
new cities evolved their own traditions of self-government and played
a significant part in the modern development of democratic ideals and

Section 3. S. E. Asian Urbanisation: Some Special Features.
It will be one of the purposes of the present paper to show that
urbanisation in Burma has been of a type with urbanisation in the rest
of S.E. Asia, and it is therefore useful to describe first some special
features of urbanisation in S.E. Asia during the last 150 years.
Before the beginning of this modern period, there were indeed
many great cities in this part of the world, but they were cities which
like the medieval cities of Europe performed some central functions
with little, if any, industrial activity. They were for the most part
royal cities and depended for their size on the might of the rulers and
fluctuated with their fortunes. In this respect, therefore, we may say
that there was little difference between East and West on this particular
matter. It is in the intervening period that urbanisation in the East
acquired its special characteristics. In appraising these special features,
we must note first of all certain basic economic conditions that prevailed.
First must be mentioned the existence of either a colonial regime or a
dependent economy in the whole of this region. This fact alone has
influenced almost every aspect of the economic, social and cultural
development of these countries, and incidentally the nature and course
of urbanisation. The result has been that certain fundamental similari-
ties have prevailed in spite of the many important differences in the
relations between these countries and the metropolitan powers.
At the same time, the countries which belong to what might
naturally be called South East Asia, i.e. the countries between India on
the one hand and China on the other, have always been influenced by
these two great countries. For the particular matter in which we are
interested at present, the over-population in these countries has been
an important fact dominating the scene in the smaller and less densely
populated countries between them. While these countries have been
important influences on the trade. culture, and the economics of the

One characteristic of the ensuing development of these countries
in particular the pattern of their urbanisation, has been the emergence
of what have been called "primate cities" in these countries. The
"primate city" is one which predominates over the other cities and
towns of a country; in size it is many times as large as the next largest
city. The concept appears to have been first mentioned by Mark
Jefferson, who deduced as a general principle that all over the
world, it is the law of the primate city that the largest city shall be
supereminent and not merely in size but also in national influence".
He cited particularly the examples of Metropolitan London seven
times as large as the next largest city of the United Kingdom, Copen-
hagen nine times as large as Denmark's second town of Aarhus, and
Mexico City five times as large as the second city of Mexico. When
he noticed exceptions to his general principle in the British dominions,
he suspected that the dominions looked "home" to London as their
primate city. He drew a strong connection between national solidarity
and the growth of primate cities. "Nationalism" he said, "crystalli-
ses in primate cities”.
But the primate cities of South East Asia have been some-what
different from his conception. The special position of the great cities
of S. E. Asia has been considered in articles by D. W. Fryer 6 and
Norton S. Ginsberg. 7 Fryer states that by western standards the
countries of South East Asia had only one city apiece until very recently.

A second characteristic feature of urbanisation in this region has
been the intense westernisation of the great cities of the region. Most
of the great cities were originally laid down according to plan and not
allowed to emerge haphazardly, although it is also true that the great
rate at which these cities expanded has defeated in most cases the hopes
of the original planners. Because of their close contact and constant
interaction with the west, these cities have been great cosmopolitan
cities, nearer to Europe in their economic and cultural aspects and
characteristics than to their own interiors. They have a technology,
a pattern of transport and communication, and a system of trade and
finance far in advance of the rest of the country. They provided in
this way as the gateway for the foreign influences which have led to
the particular development of these countries.
During a period in which economic advancement depended on the
ability to understand and utilise western technology and system of
trade and finance, these aspects of the great cities of South East Asia,
of course, led to their very speedy development. In particular, it is
claimed that these advantages in the primate city inhibited the growth
of lesser cities, which depended only on their rural neighbourhhoods
for their own development. Consequently, these great citics held
substantial proportions of the total urban population of the respective
countries.
A third feature of South East Asian cities is related to the second;
it is the plural character of the cities of Asia. The dominance of foreign
over native influences in these cities is shown not only by the adoption
of western institutions and technolgies alone, but also by a large
foreign population. In this matter, the foreign populations have only
to a small extent been from the west and were mostly from India and
China. The plural character of these city populations is to be measured
not only by the numbers of the foreigners residing in the cities but
even more by their control of the most important economic and
social positions.

The effect of these special features has been that the cities of
South East Asia have had an existence somewhat unrelated to their
hinterlands. Their main functions have been to serve as a link of the
respective countries with the corresponding metropolitan powers and
as the headquarters of their exploitation of the dependent economies.
As such, in spite of their central character in some respects, they have
failed to perform their natural functions for their own countries, in
such ways as providing commercial centres for the internal trade, a
financial centre for internal transactions or even as a cultural clearing
ground. In spite of their greater westernisation, the fundamental
characteristic of the great Asian cities has not been industrial but
commercial, especially related to the countries' foreign trade.
These characteristics of the great cities led in turn to peculiarities
in the economic, social and political environment in these countries.
Because of the foreign dominance in the urban centres, native industrial
enterprise was unable to get a start, and the main part of the economic
development in many of these countries has consisted in the exploita-
tion of the extractive industries for the export of primary products
from these countries in exchange for consumer goods. Economists
have been aware of the importance of these factors for some time.
In applying their theory of international trade, they have had to
modify the classical postulates of immobility of factors of production
between countries, and to treat the foreign commerce of these countries
as if the great cities were parts of metropolitan countries rather than as
belonging to the respective countries, so as to take account of the fact
that communications were simpler between the cities and foreign
countries than with their own hinterlands. " Studies have also been
made of South East Asian countries in terms of the concept of "dua!
economies. 9
Further, the social structure of the cities has been wholly divorced
from the rest of the country. Even in advanced countries, village
people have been despised as rustics. The contrast between rural and
urban ways of life has been even greater in Asia. While foreign

Section 4.
Urbanisation in Burma (1891 to 1953 )
It is against this background of urbanisation in the world in
general and in South East Asia in particular that the Burmese experience
has to be considered. Source materials for the study of Burmese
urbanisation, apart from the basic statistics of urban populations, are
not plentiful as there have been few sociological studies. The main
basis of the following discussion is the statistical record given in
the Census Reports of Burma from 1872. The Decennial Census
reports are available from 1872 to 1931, and a few figures from the
1941 Census Reports relating to populations of some large towns, the
rest of the 1941 Census information having been lost.
For the post-
war period, the 1953 Census gives us a great deal of fairly detailed
information about the urban population of Burma. as it was mostly
confined to the towns of Burma. However, very little information is
available about the rest of the country. Therefore it is not possible to

to total population in post-war Burma. However, some advance data

Section 6. Conclusion
Urbanisation in Burma has been influenced by a number of basic
economic factors. In the early phase, the rate of urbanisation was
decided by the balance between the growth of commerce and industry
on the one hand and the agricultural expansion on the other. At one
time, the pull of agricultural expansion dominated in the influence on
urban development and the rate of urbanisation actually declined.
Throughout this period, urbanisation in Burma as in the rest of South
East Asia showed the effect of a colonial and a dependent economy.
The greatest cities and most rapidly growing cities were seacoast cities.
concerned with the relation with foreign countries rather than with the
development of the country itself. Under this influence, the urban

Since the war, a great change has taken place. Firstly, the rate of
urbanisation has been accelerated. The E. C. A. F. E. seminar observed
this phenomenon in other countries of the region and reports that this
urbanisation has out-distanced the rate of economic development. In
many countries of the region, one of the factors influencing this has
been a state of insecurity and civil unrest outside the main cities. If
true, this calls for a re-organisation of the economy of the country, so
that the towns may be able to absorb the newly urbanised population.
Another feature to be mentioned is the almost complete transformation
of the plural character of the Burmese cities, into which the indigenous
population appears to have entered in large numbers and from which
the foreign element has tended to disappear. This appears to be
exceptional for towns in the whole of this region.
We are living during a period of great changes in the nature of
our urban populations and a number of social, political and economic
problems are being thrown up by these changes. I hope that this paper
provides a basis for assessing the magnitude of the change and to
stimulate further research on the new problems. The administration
has also to take account of these changes in planning its programmes
in such fields as housing, sanitation, transport, education and so on.
Only then can we say that a greater degree of urbanisation has
been successfully achieved.
