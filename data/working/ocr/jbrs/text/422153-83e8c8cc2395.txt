DEVELOPMENT OF THE INDUSTRY
Rubber was first grown experimentally in Mergui District around the year
1876 at the Taw-yat-kyaung area lying near the entrance to the Mergui
aerodrome site. The experiment was conducted by the government. The
rubber plants used for the experiment came from the Royal Botanic Gardens,
Kew, and later, probably, also from Ceylon.2 The experiment was successful.
The first rubber plantations were set up by the British companies, the Crown
Rubber Company being the pioneer. Seeing the success of the European
rubber companies, the Chinese and native peoples also started establishing
rubber plantations. With the beginning of the 20th. Century, the rubber
acreage of the district grew rapidy.
In the year 1920, of the total area of Mergui District, i.e. 6,260,000 acres
or 9789 sq. miles, 422,886 acres, (6.8%) were settled and 141,789 acres
(2.3%) were under cultivation.³ As early as 1920-21, rubber occupied the
largest acreage after rice, the acreage under rubber being 23,884 acres
and that under rice being 80,062 acreas. After rubber, orchards occupied the
next largest acreage, i.e. 21,380 acres. Rubber occupied 0.38% of the total area
and 17% of the cultivated area of the district. In 1920-21, Rs. 28,40,793 worth
of rubber was exported to Penang and Rangoon. The amount exported to
Penang was about 3.9 times that exported to Rangoon. As exports, the items,
which were next in importance to rubber, were minerals (about K. 3,78,000's
worth i.e. nearly 4th. that of rubber) and fish products (about K. 3,60,000's
worth i.e. nearly that of rubber).

According to popular opinion, the per acre rubber yield of Mergui
district is higher than that of any other district in Burma. It is believed to
be due to the fact that Mergui district is more favoured climatically for the
growth of rubber than any other district in Burma, being located in the most
southern part of the country and nearest rubber habitat-the Equatorial
Climates. In Equatorial Malaya, the rubber yield is higher than in Burma.

In 1922, the British Parliament enacted the Stevenson Rubber Act to
keep the production of rubber at around 60% of the out-put of rubber for
the year ended 31 October 1920. The export quota was lowered or raised
around the 60% figure in accordance with the fall or rise in rubber price.
In 1925, partly because of the Stevenson Scheme and partly because of
the greater demand for automobiles in the United States and the rapid
post-war economic recovery in Europe, the price of rubber once again rose
to a height of two and a half kyats (3 shillings 4 pence) per pound causing
a rubber boom in Mergui District. The increase in the demand for rubber
was to some extent due to the fact that man had found many new uses for
rubber in the manufacture of many articles. The rubber boom made
people, living in those parts of the world which are suitable for the growth of
rubber trees, extend the rubber acreage and out-put.
In 1928, the Stevenson Scheme was dropped, because it failed to lower
the rubber out-put proportionately with the fall in rubber price and
hold the rubber price above the unprofitable level. The failure of the
Stevenson Scheme was partly because its restrictive aim was thwarted by the
smuggling of rubber from the British possessions in South-East Asia to non-
British possessions. The scheme unintentionally caused the American rubber-
consuming firms to set up their own rubber plantations. Hence, it is claimed
by some that the ultimate effect of the scheme was an increase, rather than
a decrease, in the rubber acreage and out-put.
The increase in the world natural rubber out-put became coupled by
the increase in the out-put of synthetic rubber, made from coal, coke and
petroleum and the introduction of a new industrial technique that enabled
the use of reclaimed rubber in manufacturing.
By 1930-31, the supply of rubber became much greater than the demand
for rubber. Moreover, in that year, there was a general depression in the
U.S.A., which was the largest buyer of rubber in the world. The general
depression in the U.S.A., that later spread to the whole of the capitalist world,
reduced the demand for things which contained rubber. This in turn drasti-
cally reduced the demand for rubber. The rubber price plunged down to
3 pices or 3 pyas per pound, i. e. ½½ of a pence per pound. That made
the production of rubber unprofitable to almost all the rubber plantation
owners in Burma. The few rubber planters who still managed to make
both ends meet or make a little profit were Europeans, whose plantations
were located on more or less ideal locations and were efficiently run.

RUBBER INDUSTRY DURING THE WAR-TIME
After the British had left Mergui district and before the Japanese could
set up an effective administrative machinery in the district, some workers
and others, who lived near the rubber estates came to get the idea that who-
ever could physically control any rubber estate or any section of it would
come to be recognized as the lawful owner of the estate or its section. Some of
such people came to divide the estates between themselves and became self-
styled “estate owners" over night. A few of them abused some rubber trees
and used them as firewood. But, their days of glory were numbered, and soon
with the setting up of an administrative machinery by the Japanese, they came
to realize that the new government still recognized the original estate owners
as the real owners.
Due to the abnormal conditions, most estate owners dared not go to
their estates for inpsection now and then as in normal times. Many of their
managers, who lived in their estates, took advantage of the above condition
by secretly misappropriating the estate money and also by secretly selling
parts of the estates' products to black marketeers.
Though some rubber trees were abused by unruly people, some destroyed
by aerial bombings and strafings and still others by other military action,
the extent of damage, in comparison with the total rubber acreage in the
district, was slight.
During the second world war period, partly because of disturbed political
situation, partly because rubber could no longer be sold to the traditional
western market, and, also partly because it could not be sent in any
appreciable quantity to Japan, the only possible buyer, as a result of
the Allied attacks on the Japanese shipping, the out-put of rubber once again
dwindled almost to nothing. At that time, some of the rubber produced was
used for the manufacture of fuel for cars, as it had been found that cars could
be run on rubber fuel. But, due to the primitive way of distillation, the fuel
clogged up the carburettor and produced too much somke in the car cylinders.
Hence, the carburettor needed cleaning and the cylinders needed decarboni-
zation often. Inspite of the short-comings, rubber fuel did succeed in keeping

POST WAR CHANGES
During the post-World War II period, Mergui district effected slight
extensions of rubber areas. By 1951-2, with the recovery from war effects in
many parts of the world, and with the appearance of two important new
buyers in the world rubber maket, i.e. the Soviet Union and Peoples' China,
the price of rubber once again rose to about K. 3.00 per pound causing
another rubber boom in Mergui District.
Noticing the boom, the Government wanted to take a share in the profits
of the boom and made itself the sole buyer of rubber in the country. After
1956, two rubber buying firms' were established in Mergui as the buying
agents for the Government. They were supposed to have based their price
quotations on Singapore price quotations less 12 pyas ( 3 ½ ½ pence ) per
pound for expenses and freight. Due to the lack of competition in buying
the local rubber price came to decline appreciably. Hence, the boom ended
and as rubber earned little profit, rubber estates were no longer maintained as
well as before. If rubber estates are not well maintained, particularly if the
old rubber trees are not replaced by young ones, the rubber plantations in the
district and in other parts of the country can disappear. But, rubber is a good
earner of foreign exchange and Burma's foreign exchange position has plenty
of room for improvement. Realizing the above situation, the Government
decided to give better incentives to the people engaged in the production of
rubber. Accordingly, the Government took steps for giving up its monopoly
in the buying of rubber by allowing non-government buyers to buy rubber
from the rubber producers at competitive prices and to export it themselves
to foreign countries. One of the two rubber buying firms in the district,
representing the Government, is supposed to continue buying rubber for the
Government; but it will do so in a freely competitive manner with non-
government buyers.8 The Government expects to recover at least a part of the
decline in profit, caused by the sacrifice of its monopolistic position, from
the export duty charged on rubber exported by private exporters. It is hoped
that with the reintroduction of the free market, the out-put and export of
rubber from the district will increase and that with the improvement in the
position of the rubber industry, the economic main-stay of the district, the
general economic condition in the district will improve..

managers and other employees. Under these circumstances, the standard of
efficiency in the operation of the estates naturally has been lower and dishonest
practices on the part of employees have been more frequent than at nornal
times. Hence, much rubber reached the black market and much had been
smuggled out of the country.
The rubber estates in out-lying areas are sometimes visited by armed law-
less elements. When the latter leave, they often forcibly take away some rubber
with them. According to newspaper reports, the smuggling of rubber out
of the country was done partly by the armed insurgents to get foreign exchange
for buying arms. It is said that some of the out-of-the-way estates were once
directly operated by insurgents.
The law-less elements sometimes try to extort protection money from
the estate owners. The estate owners, then, are placed in an unenviable
position. If they pay the protection money, they risk prosecution by the
Government. If they do not, they risk persecution by the lawless elements.
However, with the establishment of General Ne Win's government, law and
order has increased in Mergui district, and the chances of rubber industry
becoming more normalized have become better.

Big company estates on Mergui island are partially underlain by red
sandstone and conglomerates. So are some of the rubber areas on Pataw
island, across the Mergui harbour. Rubber trees are also found to be growing
on the same kind of rocks to the north-east of Kyaukphya village.
Rubber plantations are mostly underlain by granite at Kabinchaung
and Tibu on King island as well as at Kawthaung. The same rock partially
underlies the rubber areas at Maliwun and on Sellore island.
Rubber is grown mostly on moderately sloping grounds near the sea and
rivers, particularly around the towns of Mergui and Palaw, the largest and
second largest town of the district respectively. The distribution pattern
clearly reflects the importance of accessibility as a locative factor. Appreciable
numbers of rubber estates are found on the Mergui-Palaw motor road and
on the Palaw - Palauk motor road. Many rubber plantations are also found
on or near the Tenasserim river up to a distance of about 50 miles from the
mouth of the river. Some rubber plantations are found on the Tenasserim-
Thabawlaik motor road and the Kawthaung (Victoria Point)-Maliwun road.
Setting aside a tiny rubber area near the town of Bok-pyin, there is a vast gap
between the rubber estates found on the Kawthaung-Maliwun road and those
found further north- a distance of 92 miles or nearly 38% of the length of the
district.

The King island-Kalagun island culster is the third largest in the district,
the largest, the second largest and fourth largest being the Mergui cluster, the
Palaw cluster and Tenasserim cluster respectively.
On King island, rubber is grown mostly on the eastern side whereas
on Kalagyun island, it is grown mostly on the northern part.
From Mergui, one main branch of the cluster stretches out towards
northeast for a distance of about 20 miles and another branch towards east-
southeast for a distance of 16 miles . Then this branch turns west-south-
west and continues into the inner islands, i.e, those which are separated from
the main land only by streams or stream-like straits, covering a distance of
about 20 miles.
The Palaw cluster of rubber estates is most extensive around Palaw.
Its nain branch extends along the Mergui-Palaw road and its second
largest branch extends northwards along the Palaw-Palauk road.
The focus of the Tenasserim cluster lies on the Tenasserim-Thabawlaik
road, i.e. about 6 miles to the east-south-east of Tenasserim. From the focus,
one branch runs west-north-west for a distance of about 10 miles and then
turns north-north-west and continues for a distance of 16 miles. Another
branch extends northwards for a distance of about 20 miles. Both the branches
more or less follow the course of the Tenasserim river.
Elevation-wise, the rubber plantations of Mergui district range from
a few feet above sea-level or roughly sea-level to over 1000', In the Mergui
cluster, they generally range from sea-level to over 300'. Between the Minthan
taung (mountain) and the confluence of the Kyaukphya chaung (creek) and
the Tenasserim river, however, they rise up to an elevation of over 1000'
The rubber plantations of the Palaw cluster generally range between sea-level
and an elevation of over 400'. On Kalagyun island, rubber plantations extend
up to 1000' elevation and on King island up over 500' elevation. On Kisseraing
island rubber plantations are found between sea-level and 200' elevation.
Plantations of the Tenasserim cluster range between 50' and 250' elevations.
In the Kawthaung area, they are found between sea-level and -250'
elevation.

In the year 1949-50, Mergui district had 3236 rubber holdings covering
an area of 43,438 acres, of which 24,079 acres (55%) were held by Burmese
nationals and 19,359 acres (45%) by non-nationals or foreigners. Of the still
British-owned rubber acreage of 7710, King Island Rubber Estate Ltd.
owns 5630 atres and Consolidated Plantation Ltd. owns 2080 acres. King
Island Rubber Estate Ltd. consists of King Island Estate, Mergui Estate and
River Estate. 13
The largest extent of European-owned rubber area was and is found on
the south-eastern sector of King Island (See Map 2.). Since the end of the
Second World War, about one-third of this area had been sold to a Burmese
national. In the prewar time, there was a small European-owned rubber
estate in the northern part of Kalagyun island. The most important European-
owned estate on Mergui island was the Mergui Crown Rubber Estate. It was
divided into two parts, one lying near Mergui and the other about 2 or more
miles in the direction of Kywegu ferry, about 9 miles away from Mergui on the
Mergui-Tavoy road. The rubber area along the Shwedu road, that branches
off south-wards from the Mergui-Kywegu road, still largely belongs to the
British. So do the estates at Milaungchanug (near Tamok ferry), Kapa and
around four miles to the north-north-east of Pala. Some British-owned rubber
areas at Tamok had been sold to the Burmese nationals after the war. These
estates also lie on the Mergui-Tavoy road. The rubber area at Mayanbinchaug
(on the same road, about 7 miles from Tamok ferry) was European-owned
before the war. Rubber areas at Nyaungbinkwin (on the Tenasserim
Thabawlaik road) and at about 12 miles upstream (on eastern bank) from
Tenasserin are also still owned by the British. Fairly big rubber estates
at Maliwun and on Maliwun-Kawthaung road belonged to British planters
during pre-war days. Nowadays. they belong to Burmese nationals.
If we consider the holdings with less than 100 acres as small holdings
and those with more than 100 acres as large holdings, then the district had
3173 small holdings and 63 large holdings. Small holdings covered a total
area of 28.071 acres and large holdings covered a total area of 15,367 acres.
Hence, in terms of simple arithmatics, an average small holding had approxi-
mately 8.9 acres and an average large holding had 244 acres. The numerical
ratio beteween the large holdings and the small holdings was 1:50. That
between the total acreage of the large holdings and the total acreage of
the small holdings was 1:1.8.

OPERATION OF THE RUBBER PLANTATION
The bigger estates are generally run more efficiently than the smaller
ones. Owners and managers of bigger estates are generally better educated
and better trained than those of smaller estates. In bigger estates, more
preventive measures are taken against attacks on rubber trees by insects
and beasts as well as against the danger of forest fire. 13a The trees which are
under attack by insects are generally quarantined with the help of trenches in
which insecticide powder has been put. If only certain branches of the rubber
tree are found to be under attack by insects, the afflicted branches are cut
down and used as fire-wood. Barbed wires are used for keeping wild beasts
away. Fire-lines or fire-breaks, i.c. lines of tree-less space, about 15' wide
are kept around estates to prevent the spread of forest fire.
In small rubber plantations, established by local people, the rubber
trees tend to be spaced too close to each other and the rubber trees are also
often tapped too early.
Rubber plantations are generally self-sufficient in firewood. The rubber
trees and branches, which have been cut down because of diseases, or which
have fallen down because of old age or high wind, are used mostly for smoking
the rubber. Smoking is generally done in enclosed sheds into which smoke
and heat are let in from below. In most estates, rubber sheets are put on racks
before smoking and taken off the racks after smoking right inside the smoke-
shed. But, in the largest estates, rubber sheets are put on racks, placed on
carriages, outside the smoke sheds and then the loaded carriages are wheeled
into the smoke sheds. Similarly, after the rubber sheets have been fully

Before rubber sheets are smoked they are first sunned. Rubber sheets
are obtained by coagulating the latex of rubber in pans with the help of acid.
During the pre-war days, acetic acid was almost the only acid used for the
said purpose. In those days, it was imported from Penang. But, after the
war, formic acid has become more popular than acetic acid. It is because
the former is stronger and more economical than the latter. Some large
estates are now using hydrofluoric acid as a rubber coagulant. Some rubber
estates use sulphuric acid. Some of the sulphuric acid for the estates comes
from Rangoon, where it is being manufactured.
In smaller estates, latex is put in separate small pans and later acid is
mixed with latex in each small pan. Each small pan contains just enough
latex for producing one sheet of rubber. In bigger estates, where hundreds
or thousands of rubber sheets are produced every day, this method of process-
ing will require hundreds and thousands of small pans and will also require
much time for mixing acid with latex in each small pan. So, in such estates,
latex is drained into tanks measuring about 2½' by 7' with a depth of about 2.
Acid is mixed with latex in these large tanks minimizing the time required for
mixing. The inside of the tank, together with the body of the latex, is divided
into small compartments at intervals of about 14" before the latex coagulates.
The division of the inside of the tank is done by placing aluminium sheets in
vertical grooves. The coagulated latex in each compartment is enough.
to make one sheet of rubber. When the latex has coagulated, the aluminium
sheets are taken out of the grooves, and slabs of coagulated latex, formed in
the small compartments, are run through roller presses to produce raw rubber
sheets. In small and medium-sized estates, the roller presses are manpowered,
but in big estates, they are machine-powered.
Cups for collecting latex are traditionally made of earthen ware and are
imported from Twante. During the pre-war days, some European plantations
used glass cups for collecting latex. Once, some of such glass cups were
manufactured in Mergui with the help of Japanese craftsmen. Sands from
the sea beaches near Mergui were used as the raw material. It was found that
glass cups, even locally made ones, were more expensive than the traditional
earthen ware cups and also that glass cups were stolen more often than the less
presentable earthen ware cups. Hence, the use of glass cups for latex collection
came to cease.
Latex collection cups are placed on metal rings, which are held on to trees
by means of spikes driven into the barks of the trees. Latex is drained into
the cup by means of a metal groove, which is also ariven into the back of the
tree. The metal rings, the metal grooves as well as the pans used for coagula-
ting latex are manufactured in Mergui from imported raw materials, i.e.

All tapping knives used to come from England. But, nowadays, they are

Tapping generally starts at the eighth year of growth of the tree. It usually
· takes about six years to finish tapping one side of the trunk. Hence by around
the twelfth year of tapping, both sides of the trunk have been covered. In
other words. by then all the virgin bark in the tappable portion of the tree
has been removed and replaced by new growths. With the disappearance of
the virgin bark, the yield of the rubber tree decreases gradually, Thus, it is
supposed to be advisable to replace the old rubber tree with a young one after
the old rubber tree has attained its 40th year.
The bigger estates, especially the European owned ones, carry out the
replacement programme as a matter of course. But, smaller estates as a rule
fail to do so partly because of financial stringency and partly because of the
lack of foresight. Hence, the margin of profit of the smaller estates is generally
low.
Some estates, especially larger ones, tap their trees every alternate day
instead of every day. Alternate day tapping minimizes the wearing of the
bark and at the same time it gives more time to the tapped portion of the
bark to heal before it is tapped again. Thus, it is believed that in the long run,
a tree, tapped every alternate day, will yield more latex for more years than
a tree tapped every day. The alternate day tapping also minimizes the labour
expenses as well as the wear and tear of equipment. Hower, in the short-run,
every day tapping is of course more profitable than alternate day tapping.
As many of the estate owners are not rich enough or wise enough to forego
the short run benefit for the greater long run benefit, hardly 10% of the
estates of the district are being operated on the alternate day tapping basis. 14
During the past few years, most estates had to resort to every-day tapping
to make both ends meet. Making both ends meet was a problem with many
estates, because of the low rubber price, caused by the monopolistic buying.
In the earlier days, rubber estate owners had to pay only land revenue
but no income tax. Nowadays, they have to pay both land revenue and
income-tax. The increase in the tax-burden, borne by the estate owners
partly accounts for their inability to invest in alternate day tapping for their
long-run benefit. Nowadays, the estate owners have to pay land revenue
on the basis of K. 3.30 per acre.

Another reason that accounted for the preference on the part of the estate

During the pre-war days, the majority of the labourers in rubber
plantations were Indians, the Burmese labourers forming a minority. After
the war, many Indian labourers returned to India and the entry of Indian
labourers into Burma declined sharply. Hence, the ratio between the Indian
labourers and Burmese labourers in the rubber estates of Mergui district
became roughly even. Many rubber planters prefer Indian labour to
Burmese labour, because the former generally is more disciplined and less
demanding than the latter. The Indian labourers are supposed to excel in tapp-
ing whereas the Burmese labourers are supposed to excel in clearing jungles.
MARKETING
All the rubber produced in Mergui district is traditionally sent out of
the district through the port of Mergui. Most of the rubber is exported in
the form of sheets. It is also exported in the form of raw rubber strings or
scraps. Scraps are a bi-product of the rubber industry. For tapping latex from
the rubber tree, an inclined groove has to be cut in the bark of the tree. After
some latex has come out of the groove and drained away, the latex left in the
groove coagulates by itself. The following day, the coagulated latex is pulled
out from the above mentioned groove before a new groove is cut just below
the old one. The coagulated latex, when pulled comes out as a string. The
raw rubber strings easily stick to each other. So, they can be turned into
bundles just by being thrown into a can and be left there for sometime.
Rubber scraps fetch only about half the price fetched by rubber sheets.
Rubber sheets used to be bundled with the help of vegetable fibres. But,
nowadays, they are being turned into bundles merely by means of pressure.
To make bundles with the pressure method, a few rubber sheets are used as
cover sheets for other rubber sheets. The cover sheets are pressed on to other
sheets, which have been neatly piled. To make the cover sheets attach more
securely to the main pile of sheets, a sharp iron rod is sometimes run through
them into the main pile of rubber sheets.
As the road system of the district is very skeletal, (see map 2) most of the
rubber reaches Mergui town by water. In the thirties, much of the rubber from
the outlying district reached Mergui in small steamers, run by an Indian
firm. As the second world war approached, motor boats, built and run largely
by the Chinese and Sino-Burmans, came to compete fairly successfully with the
small steamers as means of transportation for rubber. With the outbreak of the

During the pre-war days, rubber was bought up from the estate owners

After the war, the pre-war status quo came back in the rubber marketing
field. But, after the 1951-52 rubber boom, all the rubber bought by the two
firms, representing the state, was sent to Rangoon by motor schooners,
mostly owned and operated by the people of Mergui.
In the year 1956-57, 4279.9 tons of rubber were exported. 15 At the then
current price of K.1.10 per pound for moderate quality rubber, the rubber
produced in that year was worth about K. 10,545,920. The total export
tonnage of Mergui for the above year was 23,285 tons.16 Hence, weight-wise,
rubber constituted 18.4% of the export tonnage of Mergui. The remaining
tonnage was made up of fish products, mineral products and vegetable
products.
For marketing, rubber is graded into five classes. Only a few well managed
big estates manage to produce grade 1 rubber. Most estates produce grade
2 or 3 rubber. A few badly managed small estates produce grade 4 or grade
5 rubber. Higher grade rubber fetches slightly higher prices than lower grade
rubber. Though the difference in price per pound between higher grade rubber
and lower grade rubber may be small-about 3.75 pyas (I penny) per pound-,
as rubber is generally sold in hundreds and thousands of pounds, the actual
difference in payments received is often appreciable.
According to the new state policy as regards rubber marketing, exporters
can now export rubber to any foreign country. But in cases of sales on
consignment basis, sales can be effected through agents, recognized by the
Agricultural and Rural Development Corporation (A.R.D.C.) 17. This new
policy has already introduced a spirit of optimism among the rubber producers
of Mergui district. It is likely that much rubber from Mergui will once again
be sent to Penang and Singapore, the latter being one of the world's largest
rubber marketing centres.

unable to give loans to needy rubbers planters despite its original expressed

At present, grade 1 rubber is being produced almost solely by large
estates. As the main requirement for the production of grade 1 rubber
is cleanliness, there is no reason why small estate owners cannot produce
grade 1 rubber, provided they are given a little more technical knowledge.
So, attempts should be made to impart the necessary technical knowledge
to small estate owners. Lloyd wonders if it will be beneficial for small
estate owners if they form co-operatives for the production of rubber
so that they can reap the benefits, which normally can be enjoyed only
by large rubber estates, with large financial and managerial capacities.
To counter the bad effects of the boom--slump trend of rubber market,
people should be encouraged not to depend solely upon the production
of rubber for their livelihood. 22 With that aim in view, inter crops,
like coffee, pine apples and perenials should be allowed to be grown
between rubber trees. The rubber Grant Act and revenue rules that
prohibit the growth of inter crops between rubber trees should be
amended.
The revenue law did not exempt revenue on replanted rubber areas
for eight years as it does with newly planted rubber areas. So that
estate owners may be tempted to replant the areas under trees, which are too
old, the revenue law has been accordingly amended. Hence, the revenue
law nowadays exempts revenue on replanted rubber areas for eight years.
Abolishment of income tax levied on production of rubber can also
induce the rubber planters to do replanting. If need be, income tax can be
substituted by export duty on rubber. Such substitution will affect,
somewhat, small estate owners. However, we may note that true small
holders, i.e. those who own estates with less than 25 acres, are seldom
solely dependent on the production of rubber for their livelihood.
Still another possible alternative to get money for financing the
replanting programme is to levy a cess tax on rubber production, to be
used for granting loans to rubber planters, who undertake to replant.
