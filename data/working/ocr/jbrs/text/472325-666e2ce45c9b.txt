TEXTURAL CHARACTERISTICS OF CHANNEL SEDIMENTS
OF THE CHINDWIN RIVER
Abstract
As the grain size distribution parameters of the channel sediments
of the Chindwin River do not exhibit any progressive change downstream,
it appears that the hydraulic environment remains uniform over the
distance of 520 river miles. Negative skewness of the channel sediments
is due to presence of gravel and coarse sand fraction. Abrasion
appears to be an operative process in the Chindwin River because majority
of sand-sized minerals (0.125 mm) show an increase in roundness
downstream. Lack of progressive change in values of sphericity and
shape factor is due to lower limit of value change, lower size limit
and mixing of spherical and non-spherical grains due to annual floods
in the Chindwin River. The scarcity of material in 2-1 mm class
is primary deficiency in this size class.
Introduction
The presence of rounded pebbles in
streams is a common feature and it is
obvious that rounding is due to abrasion.
To study the effects of abrasion on sedimen-
tary particles laboratory studies have been
made with an abrasion mill or with rolling
barrels. The results obtained from these
experiments are of considerable importance
no doubt, but it may be argued that the
abrasion mill studies and the like are poor
imitations of river action and as such are
poor substitutes.
Another common observation is that
the grain size of sediments carried by
streams decreases downstream. Since peb-
bles are rounded it has been presumed that
the decline in size downstream is also due
to abrasion. This deduction may be true
to some extent, but it has also been postu-
lated that decrease in size downstream is
due to progressive sorting. What then is
the truth?
Much attention of late has been paid
to the study of compositional and textural
changes produced in clastics due to stream
transport. Most of these studies (Krumbein,
1940, 1942; Sneed and Folk, 1958; Plumley,
1948) have been directed at pebble-sized
particles. Sand-sized particles were studied
by Russell (1937), Russell and Taylor (1937),
Van Andel (1951) and Pollack (1961).
Russell (1939, p. 33) summed up the then
existing state of our knowledge on the
effects of stream transport on sediments as

Description of the Chindwin River: The
Chindwin and its tributaries drain an area
of approximately 44,000 square miles. It
rises in Hukawng valley on the Indo-Burma
border and flows for aproximately 800
miles on a southerly course before it enters
into the Irrawaddy River. During the high-
water season (June to November) the river
327
is navigable up to Singkaling Hkamti
(henceforth called Hkamti). During the
remainder of the year the water at level at
places in the middle and upper Chindwin
is only two and half feet and this makes the
upper reaches of the river inaccesible by
steamer. The river is not navigable north
of Hkamti in any season.

For the greater part of its course the
Chindwin River follows the trend of the
Tertiary ranges along the western side of
the Chindwin syncline. Often it cuts across
from one strike valley to another (Fig. 2),
returning again lower downstream. Conspi-
cous cliffs occur where the river banks are
formed of massive, well-cemented, resistant
sandstones. Numerous oblique potholes
(Fig. 3), a few inches in diameter and up to
20 inches deep, occur in some of the cliffs.
Fig. 3. Oblique potholes on one of the cliffs
Above Homalin it is possible to observe
the mode of transport of the river bed load
because the water is so clear. Sand general-
ly moves by rolling; movement by saltation
takes place only where the surface of bed is
irregular and increases turbulence. In the
narrow channels, where the current is
strong, granules and small pebbles also
move by rolling. Otherwise they remain

Earth movements in the Chindwin valley: The
upper Chindwin district between Hkamti
and Mingin is essentially a broad syncline
the middle of which is occupied by Irra-
waddian sediments and Quatenary alluvial
deposits. Strike ridges composed of Lower
Tertiary sandstones extend for many miles
along the western side of the Chindwin
valley, with steep dip slopes to the east. On
the east the valley is bounded by the
Mettaung and Zibindaung hill ranges of
western Shwebo and Katha districts. The
syncline narrows rapidly south of Mingin
as its axis rises up the Patolon valley.

Stamp (1940, p. 335), discussing the
origin of the Chindwin valley, states: "The

trough before the advent of the folding

the ease with which these streams cut across
the strike of the Tertiary ridges.

For sampling a dredge-like sampler
was designed. It consisted of a cylindrical
tube, 11 inches long and three inches in
diameter, which was sealed at one end. To
the open end of the tube was attached a
beveled lip-like device, which projected
downwards to scrap the top one inch of the
bed load. Suitable screws were attached to
the external wall of the sampler through
which ropes were fastened, so that it could
be pulled along the river bed from a boat.
To prevent the sampler from overturning
the screws were attached near its open end.
At sampling points, the sampler was
thrown into the channel from the boat.

interval were used. Each sieved fraction
was weighed to the nearest 0.01 gram. The
sieved fractions were later examined with a
binocular microscope to determine the
presence of aggregrates, if any. Most sands
were without aggregrates, but when an
exception did occur, the percentage of aggre-
grates was deducted from the weight of the
sieved fraction. Presenc of aggregrates is
undesirable, as it adversely influences

kurtosis.

Statistical Parameters Used in the
Grain Size Analysis
Many parameters have been introduced
for describing the grain size distribution of

sediments. The parameters of Trask (1932)
and Krumbein (1936) are quartile measures
based on 25th., 50th. and 75th. percentiles
of cumulative curve. Some investigators
object to the use of these parameters on
theoretical grounds. Inman (1952) stated
that since size-frequency curve approaches
a normal distribution, parameters based on
quartiles have no particular significance.
He advocated the use of 16th. and 84th.
percentiles instead of 25th. and 75th. per-
centiles. Folk and Ward (1957) suggest the
use of 5th., 16th., 25th., 50th., 84th. and
95th percentiles because the parameters
obtained from Inman's suggested percentiles
ignore one-third of the distribution, parti-
cularly the 'tails' which according to them,
offer the most valuable information. Fried-
man (1962, p. 724) discussing the relative
merits of moment and graphical measures,
states: “Strictly speaking, graphical mea-
sures, such as the parameters of Inman
and Folk and Ward, as simple descriptive
measures are just as valid as moment mea-
sures, and their 'validity' does not depend
upon the distribution being log normal."

those defined by Folk and Ward (1957).

The position of the modal classes for
the sand and gravel fractions is shown in
Fig. 6. It is noteworthy that material of
-1 to 0 (2-1 mm) never forms a modal
class. Pettijohn (1940) stated that there was
a deficiency in the 2-4 mm (granule), the
2-1 mm (very coarse sand) and the 1/8 to
1/16 mm (very fine sand) classes. His
observation was based on the fact that in
his study of 2000 published mechanical
analyses the modal class rarely fell in the
grades mentioned above. Commenting on
the scarcity of certain grade sizes Pettijohn
(1957, p. 48-49) states: "One may assume
that materials in the grades under considera-
tion were produced by weathering and rock
disintegration, but that these materials were
either never deposited as a modal class for
certain hydraulic reasons or because of
mechanical instability they disappear during
the transport process, or we may suppose
that there is a primary deficiency of certain
size classes. It may be that all sizes of
335
materials are not produced in equal quantity
by breakdown of the source rocks."
It is not possible to conceive of a situa-
tion, whereby hydraulic reasons would be
responsible for certain sizes not being
deposited as modal classes, because there is
no reason why these grades should not be
concentrated in some hydraulic environ-
ment, if they were available in sufficient
quantity. It appears that the deficiency of
these size grades is either due to (a)
mechanical instability of certain size grades
or (b) primary deficiency of these grades.
Pettijohn (1957) pointed out that the
particles in the 2 to 8 mm range would be
structurally weak and would be unable to
survive vigorous stream action. Kuenen
(1959) supports this view but with the
qualification that this hypothesis is applic-
able only to granular rock particles. As
fragments of granular rocks in this size
range are very rare in the Chindwin gravels
their absence is possibly due to structural
weakness.
Granular rocks like granites, grano-
diorites and gneisses by disintegration will
yield materials of sand size. Should chemi-
cal decay be the mode of destruction, only
quartz of sand size will survive, along with
the minor accesories. From his study of
Plutonic rocks Dake (1921) concluded that
such rocks would rarely yield particles
coarser than 1.00 mm by decomposition.
According to his estimate only nine per
cent of the quartz would exceed 1.00 mm
in diameter and only 20 per cent would
exceed 0.6 mm in diameter. It is apparent
that a large percentage of material that
would be obtained from decomposition of
granites would be below 1.00 mm in dia-
meter. What is true for granites should
also hold for other granular rocks. Mono-
mineralic grains coarser than 1.00 mm

The decline in roundness of zircon
may be explained as due to fracturig because
20 to 30 percent of the zircon grains were
broken. However, this mechanism fails to
expalin the decline in roundness of haema-
tite and sphene because no fractured grains
of these minerals were encountered. Their
decline may be due to chemical corrosion,
or due to reasons which cannot at the mo-
ment be explained.

Sphericity of minerals was computed
from the grain count analysis data. The

Haematite, amphiboles, zircon, pyrox-

sphericity downstream. According to Rus-

downstream, and if by suspension, it dec-

exhibit any trend, it is siginificant that
those which exhibit such a trend show an
increase in sphericity downstream, thus
indicating that traction is the dominant
mode of transport in sand-sized sediments.
Earlier it has been stated that observations
in the Chindwin River showed that most
sands move downstream by rolling.
The coarser grades (0.125 mm) com-

In conclusion, whereas there is a distinct
increase in roundness downstream, no such
relationship is visible in the case of spheri-
city and shape in the majority of the
minerals. Also no relationship between
roundness and sphericity has been observed.
Similar observations were made by Pollack
(1961, p. 33) and according to him the lack
of progressive change in roundness, spheri-
city and shape of mineral grains downstream
is probably due to “lack of abrasional rigor,
the possibility of a lower limit of value
changes, and the possibility of a lower size
limit below which abrasional forces are
non-operative”, According to Pettijohn
(1957) sphericity is a function of the
original grain shape and as such is little
modified.
It may well be that the causes listed
above are responsible for lack of pro-
gressive change in sphericity and shape
factor values. Perhaps there is another
factor involved; that is flooding. In regions
of the Chindwin basin type, where the
annual rainfall is over 200 inches and
is confined to a limited period (4-5 months),
floods must greatly increase the capacity
and competency of the streams, thus
enabling them to carry in suspension
part of the bed load hitherto carried by
traction. This would cause mixing of
spherical and non-spherical grains. To
what extent this mixing takes place is not
known, because studies of this nature
have not been undertaken; but it is
probable that it occurs. Should this
deduction be correct it will make it
extremely difficult, if not impossible, to
study the effects of stream transportation
on the sphericity and the shape of the
mineral grains.
SUMMARY AND CONCLUSIONS
363
1. The grain size distribution para-
meters of the channel sands of the Chin-
dwin River exhibit no distinct downstream
trend; though there is a tendency for the
mean size and the kurtosis values to
decline downstream. This lack of trend
indicates that the hydraulic conditions of
the river remain uniform over the distance
of 520 miles studied. This is also indicated
by the bimodality of majority of samples.
2. The mean size of the gravel frac-
tion only and the long diameter of the
largest pebble in the distribution show a
progressive decline downstream. Therefore,
in the study of ancient channel sediments,
the downslope direction may be determined
by the measurement of these two para-
meters, but not by the variation in the
mean size of the sands.
3. There is a marked deficiency in
the 2-1 mm size class. It is due to
primary deficiency of material in this
size range. Lack of granular rock pebbles
in the 8-2 mm range is attributed to the
structural weakness of these pebbles.
4. The effect of tributary dilution on
the textural characteristics of the river
sediments is negligible. This is indicated
by the presence of sand or gravel banks,
mouths of the tributaries,
present at
composed of material much coarser than
the material present in the river channel;
and also by the fact that sorting does
not improve downstream.
5. The size distribution of the channel
sands is generally positively skewed (Pol-
lack, 1961 and Friedman, 1961). This is
not true, however, in the case of the
channel sediments of the Chindwin River.
Their negative skewness is due to (a) the
