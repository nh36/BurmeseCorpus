1. The Three Assumptions
The three assumptions are: (i) that
the most useful unit for tonal analysis
is the syllable; (ii) that such different
types of phonetic feature as pitch (high
versus low), voice quality (clear, or breathy,
versus constricted, ‘creaky' or glottal-trill, 2
vowel duration (short versus long), and
syllable-final consonantal and vocalic fea-
tures (occlusion alternating with friction and
with nasality, etc., e. g. [-], [-p], [-0],
[-s], [-m], [-n], versus nasality, e.g. [-m],
[-n], including nasality of vowel versus
vowel-ness, or vocality, e.g. -j], [~u], [-o]
can be associated with each other in a
tonal statement; and (iii) that the tonal
(or tonemic) analysis can be applied
regardless of differences in grammatical
function, and therefore in the grammatical
classification, of the lexical items to which
it is applied.³

B. Basic Forms
It is clear from Cornyn's and Mc-
David's phraseology that in all such cases
they prefer the tone-I or tone-II form to
the tone-III: it is Cornyn's tone I that is
said to 'change', not the other way about;
it is McDavid's tone-I and tone-II forms
that are 'replaced', not the tone-III; and it
is Cornyn's tone I that is indicated for the
particles -té and -mé when these are cited
in isolation.2 That is to say, Cornyn and
McDavid treat one of their alternative tonal
classifications as basic for these lexical
items, and subordinate the other classifica-
tion to it.
23
The grounds for their choice are not
stated; but a more serious objection is not
to the absence of explicit criteria governing
the choice but rather to the very concept
of "basic form. What makes this concept
419
difficult to justify is the fact that it is just as
un-Burmese, or, rather, anti-Burmese, to
use the tone-I or the tone-II form of a
lexical item in the grammatical conditions
appropriate to the alternative tone-III form
as it is to use the tone-III form in the
grammatical conditions appropriate to the
tone-I or the tone-II. It is just as un-
Burmese to use the tone-II form lin and
the tone-I forms -té and /ou in the gram-
matical conditions appropriate to the tone-
Ill forms lin, –tè, and /u/, for example,
as it would be if one used lin, -tè, and /u/
where grammatical conditions required lin
té, or /Qu/. That is to say, each of the
alternative tones is, as it were, basic in the
grammatical or phonological conditions
appropriate to that alternative; and this fact
must necessarily give each alternative equal
status with the other alternative. This
means that the phonetic form appropriate
to the conditions in which a particular
lexical item is named or cited, the citation
form of that lexical item, is not to be
regarded as in any way superior to other
phonetic forms of that item: the citation
phonetic form has a monopoly of the cita-
tation context, but is inappropriate in any
other context. To disregard the unique.
status of each alternative phonetic form in
the environment appropriate to it by choos-
ing one phonetic form in preference to the
other, or to the others, and treating
it as basic, whether or not it is so
designated, cannot be other than
arbitrary; and, furthermore, this disregard
leads to the use of process terms such
as 'change' and 'replacement', which
suggest processes that do not, and can-
not, take place. It is now recognized
that the terms 'replacement' and 'change'
do not refer to articulatory processes and
the actual replacement of one phoneme
by another, the superceding, in e.g. the
