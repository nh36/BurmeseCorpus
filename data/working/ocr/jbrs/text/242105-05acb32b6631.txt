3. MYINBYUSHIN
(1) ELSEWHERE IN BURMA
The worship of the Nat Myinbyushin is common in Upper Burma, at
Myedu, Meiktila, Kyauksè, Kalemyo, and elsewhere. At Myedu he was the
guardian of the south gate. There are two shrines at Meiktila, where the
nattein paid no taxes and enjoyed a piece of land rent free. When the
sluice-gates of the lake were opened each year, an offering was (and per-
haps still is) made to the spirit of the horseman of Nawrata. Visitors had
to pay him homage on reaching Meiktila, doffing all except the most neces-
sary clothing. The legend ran that Nawrata sent a man on a white horse to
see if Meiktila lake extended as far as Mount Popa. The horseman rode so
fast that on reaching the king again he could only gasp out that it did not
before he and his horse expired and became Nats. There is another
Myinbyushin in Meiktila district who is apparently quite a different person,
for this is the spirit of a villager who was killed by a kick from a pony.
Kyauksè also reveres Myinbyushin, but its legend is a variant. King
Naratheinga fell in love with Welu Dewi, wife of his brother Narapati, and
followed the stratagem whereby King David acquired the wife of Uriah the
Hittite. He sent Narapati to quell a rebellion in Tagaung, and began to
make love to his sister-in-law. Narapati's faithful groom Maung Pyi at
once sprang to horse and rode with all speed to tell the prince his master.
Narapati was returning towards Pagan when Maung Pyi galloped upon his
white horse, and unjustly incensed at what he believed to be the groom's
slothfulness, at once slew him. When he proceeded on his journey, he was
confronted by his henchman's ghost and became duly sorry for his misdeed.
A somewhat similar story will be noticed in the description of the Komyo-
shin Nat. Numerous shrines in prominent positions around Ava commemor-
ate a Myinbyushin who is said to have been a faithful servant of Anawrata.
In Shwebo town Bodawgyi is the chief Nat, and there are eight nats
subordinate to him, of whom one is Myinbyushin.
Number a in Sir R. C. Temple's list of the thirty seven Nats is named
Shwe Nawrata. He was of royal descent and was thrown into the Irraw-
addy and drowned. He is pictured as a Manipuri, and may well be one of
the princes referred to in the Kani legends narrated below.
Two legends prevail at Kani, one of which may be at once disposed of.
It relates that Kaye-yaza-gyi and Kaye-yaza-nge, sons of King Monda,
crossed over from Manipur and built a fortress and city at Kani. Hostile
neighbours, who may have been Mon princelings, attacked and defeated
them, and the brothers rode over Kani cliff into the Chindwin with some of
their followers and were drowned. The unsatisfactory features of this tale
are the unexplained reference to Mons, the absence of any mention of a
white horse, and the fact that Myinbyushin is commonly regarded as one
Nat only and not two brothers. According to the more widely held version,
King Min Kyaw Zwa ruled in Pagan and his son Prince Diparit was Myosa
of Kani. He built a fort on the hill above the riverine village; on one side
flowed the Thanlawaddy river, and he dug a moat on the other three. The

(2) THE SHRINE OF MYINBYUSHIN
Behind the bungalow on the water-front is an ornamental archway,
whence a path winds up the cliff. About half-way up are the outlines of
what is called the Mindwet Taga. There are quantities of loose bricks on
either side, and this may well be the site of one of the city gates. On the
plateau a turn to the right leads to the southernmost building, the Myin-
byushin pagoda, which looks down on modern Kani. It is a squat old-
fashioned fane, the lower part a white-washed 'bu,' though the upper stages
are the usual slender gilt erection of the present day. The people of Kani
believe that it was built long ago by Indians. When it was rebuilt in 1929,
it was found to be solid except for a compartment about half-way up, in
which were ten brass statues of a deity who was said to resemble the pictures
in Indian religious books. They had Indian features and on them were
inscriptions which none in Kani could read, though an Indian postman
alleged that they were in an Indian character. As the figures were re-placed
in the pagoda, the facts cannot be verified. King Nawrata or Prince Diparit
is said to have been the founder. Inscriptions on the upper portion show
that, it was re-built in B. E. 1290 and re-gilded in 1292.
Nearer the cliff edge is the Natsin, a small square white-washed stone
building, unfortunately covered by a shed of corrugated iron. In the shrine
are seven gilt statues of teakwood, of undoubted antiquity. Some of the
figures, especially the two smallest, are in poor condition, and much of the
gilt is missing from all seven. They represent Myinbyushin and six of his
men who spared his death. He and four others are on horses, mule-like
beasts with long pointed ears and long tails, and braided maines, that are
clearly not thoroughbreds. They hold the reins in their left hands, and
swords in the right, though their present weapons are modern ones that re-
placed the originals. The bridles, bits, reins, and saddles (and especially
Myinbyushin's) are elaborately ornamented, the saddle-cloths running to a
point. All seven figures are in court dress and most have long helmets
whose pointed tips curve backwards. In their ears are large nadaungs.

The largest figure is that of Myinbyushin. Rather smaller are those of
Taung Min Gyi and Taung Min Ngè. The former is a Shan Sawbwa, with
a long pointed beard, and his helmet alone has a much carved base. Taung
Min Ngè is his brother. He too is a Sawbwa, and there is a little carving
on the base of his helmet. Taung Ma Gyi and Maung Min Shin, Temple's
numbers 13 and 14, are also brothers (sons of Maung Tin Dè by the
nagama) and are portrayed as soldiers with dahs or spears. They are
usually shown with six arms. The brothers at Kani never had six arms, I
think, though another figure, described below, had. The other two horse-
men are of lesser rank and are represented by smaller figures. They are
said to be Shin Nyo and his younger brother Shin Byu. These appear to
be the same as the brothers Maung Pyu and Maung Nyo (of whom Maung
Pyu is the elder) who will be referred to in the legend of Komyoshin,
Maung Pyu, in fact, becoming the Komyoshin Nat. One small figure,
with a round helmet, stands and shikoes. He is the sit-thugyi 'Let-kyauk-
pet.' He used to have six arms, four of which sprouted from the elbows,
but only the more orthodox pair remain. The analogy with the many.
armed Indian gods is obvious. The last of the statues is that of a clerk
who is seated and holds a parabaik and a pen. Worshippers keep the figures
swathed in pink scarves, pink being the Nats' colour as elsewhere in Burma.
Some offerings, of flowers are usually to be seen on the threshold of the
shrine.
The Nan-u pagoda stands a little inland from the shrine and is suppos-
ed to have been at the entrance to the palace. Though it is rather dilapid-
ated and some of the guardian chinthes are tumbling to ruin, it does not
appear to be of great age, and the design is the conventional one of the
present day. It is alleged to have been built by Diparit's descendants.
There are no trustees for any of these buildings, responsibility for whose up-
keep rests upon the town elders.
In Burmese times there was a festival in July, but it has been dis-
continued of late years. There are no natteins to preserve the shrine of
Myinbyushin, but there is a natgadaw. King Thibaw appointed a certain
U Dwe to be Kani Wun, and his henchmen included a man whose grand-
daughter used to go to the Taungbyon festival as a natgadaw. With the
consent of the other Taungbyon natgadaws she made her permanent abode
at Kani. She tells fortunes at her house in Kani town and receives a few
offerings, but her income is small. Myinbyushin remains the guardian nat
of Kani and its neighbourhood. He protects those who pay their respects
to him with gaungbaungs, pasoes, fruit, tea, and other offerings. It has
been noted above that at Meiktila clothes are doffed at the shrine of
Myinbyushin, but I do not know if they are offered to the nat or resumed
by their owners.

4. KOMYOSHIN
(1) HIS ORIGIN
There are several fables of the origin of Shan Komyoshin or Komyoyin.
The story prevalent in the Kani township confuses him with the Mahagiri
Nats of Popa. Maung Tin De of Tagaung could do his work as a black-
smith with a hammer weighing twenty-five viss in his left hand and one of

fifty viss in his right. The king feared that he would rebel and tried to
capture him, but he took refuge on the Manle stream in the modern Katha
district. There he lived with a female dragon, who presently laid two eggs,
but the eggs were carried down stream while he and she were crossing it.
They were caught on a rock and hatched out into human children, who were
suckled by a thamin and went about the forests with her. Two brahmins
of King Duttabaung told him of these strange events, and the children were
brought to his court at Tharekettara. When they grew up, Maung Pyu
was sent to collect revenue from the Shans and his younger brother Maung
Nyo on a similar errand to the Mon country. They gained prestige and, as
usual in these stories, the king became afraid that they would depose him.
He therefore urged them to a boxing match, promising the winner a paso
worth a lakh, but so stern was the contest that both died of their injuries
and became Nats. Maung Nyo was called Talaing Komyoshin Nat and was
the patron of the Mons, while Mg Pyu was revered in the kingdoms of the
ninety nine Shan Sawbwas, though "kosè" seems to have dropped out of
his title. The unjust death of two brothers, faithful servants of their king,
is parallelled in the better known story of the Shwebyin brothers of Taung-
byon. They, too, were by some accounts the sons of unusual parentage,
their mother being a biluma.
Another version of Komyoshin's legend is given at page 324 of part 2
volume 3 of the Upper Burma Gazetteer. In 692 A. D. King Narapati
Sithu unjustly executed his old servant Maung Aung Zwa, who became a
nat and appeared before the monarch, protesting his innocence. Narapati
ordered the nine sawbwas of the Kanbawsa Shan States to build shrines
and render annual homage to the Nat. Komyoshin showed his gratitude by
his material assistance to a later king of Burma. At page 220 of his
History of Burma, Harvey tells that this Nat fought for Alompra in the
third battle of Moksobo, which preluded the advance on Ava. Alompra had
some allies among the Gwe Shans, which may explain the Nat's activities.
And in 1756 when the sawbwas accompanied Alompra on his Siamese
campaign, the Nat was seen mounted on a white horse which he rode to
victory at the head of the attackers, just as the Great Twin Brethren had
led the Romans at the battle of Lake Regillus more than two thousand
years before. At the end of the Siamese War many shrines were built in
honour of Komyoshin in Upper Burma, including those at Maukkadaw and
Mingin in Upper Chindwin, and Kanè and Alon in the Lower Chindwin.
Aung Swa Ma Gyi is nat number 10 in Temple's list and is described as
dying as a result of the treachery of King Alaungsithu. He is portrayed on
horseback, with a dah in his hand.
Some dwellers in Kani township deem that he is a brother of Ame

clearly confusing him with Taungu Bayin, whom I described in the previous

(2) HIS SHRINES
Komyoshin used to abide on the east bank of the Chindwin at Mayodon
below Kani, till he moved with his nattein further down stream to a village
which was named Natkyi in his honour. There a still existing shrine was
built in a fenced compound outside the village and close to the river bank.
In it is his gilded wooden statue on horseback, about one and a half feet
high.
He has a less pretentious shrine at Kanè, opposite to Kani. On the
landing-stage near the east gate of the village is a wooden platform fasten-
ed to the trunk of a large tree, whereon are two statues of the Nat. One is
of padauk wood, nearly two feet high, and is said to be about sixty years
old. The other is a small dark figure of sit wood, of greater age, and about
a foot and a half in height. Both are mounted and are very similiar to
Myinbyushin's figure in the temple on the cliffs of Kani. Both were gilded
but the older statue has lost nearly all its gilt. Some hold that the larger
figure represents Komyoshin and that the smaller is merely a follower, but
the majority are agreed that the larger was a second and more elaborate
version made at the instance of one U Kauk when the old small figure was
becoming decrepit. This U Kauk was King Mindon's thittaw-ok for the
forests above Alon, charged with the duty of bringing teak logs down the
Chindwin and levying dues. His son is the present headman of Kanè. The
shrine is one of the three kundauks in Kani township, places under trees
where offerings of provisions are made, the other two being one in the
Mayodon jungle in Sulegon village tract between Natkyi and Kanè, and the
other between Ywatha and Wetchaung above Natkyi. There is a similar
figure on a similar platform on the water-front at Mingin.
The Nat has a shrine of wagat and in-wood on the river bank in the
Mondaing quarter of Alon, a smaller edition of Bodawgyi's palace in the
same town. There are natteins at Alon who possess statues of him.
Komyoshin accompanies Bodawgyi in the processions before the Tabaung
festival. There is also a nattein at Kanè, an old woman who tells fortunes
at the shrine or in her house in the village. Offerings of bananas and
cocoanuts and less often of rice or pink scarves are made to the nat and
kept by the nattein. Sometimes they are redeemed for a small money
payment, by permission of the nat received through the nattein.

formerly from about the tenth waxing to the fifth waning of Tagu, but the
people are now sophisticated and also poorer, and the feast lasted only two
days in 1933, namely the second and third waning. About twelve hundred
devotees used to attend in more prosperous times. A pandal of bamboo
and thatch with cloth screens is erected in front of the temple. It is meant
for the natgadaws, who come from Mandalay, Monywa, and elsewhere, but
is used also by spectators. The natgadaws here are all women, though
some wear men's clothing in the dances, dressing in different styles to
portray the various nats. Most of them renew their energies with draughts
of toddy. The curious ceremony of bidding the nats occupies much of the
two days. Most of the thirty-seven chief nats of Burma are called upon, in
their turn, to come and receive respects and offerings and to enjoy the pwè.
First, the Mahagiri nats are invited, then usually the patron himself. When
his turn comes, the nattein chooses a natgadaw of superior merit and asks
her to invite the nat. Nine dahs are tied round her waist with a rope or a
scarf, and two are held in her hands, while she dances and sings and calls
upon Komyoshin. The other nats are bidden by whoever feels the urge.
Ame Yeyin, whose cult in Yinmabin township I have described, has a
tigerish disposition, and her statues at Alon and Powundaung show her
riding on a tiger. The natgadaw who invites her has to simulate the actions.
of a tiger. A bowl of water and bunches of bananas are placed on a tray,
and the natgadaw crawls on the ground and roars, laps up the water and
takes the bananas off the tray with her teeth. The "kya seik pauk” and
kya ye thauk" are two of the most popular features in the festival.

When U Min Gyaw is invoked, his love of liquor is not forgotten and
toddy is offered. Another popular nat is Taung Mingyi who, as we have
seen, has a statue in Myinbyushin's temple above Kani. When his turn comes,
the nattein hands two dahs to a natgadaw, who pretends to sharpen them.
The nattein has to pay her four annas and a sixteenth of a basket of rice
for her services. In the closing stages, the nattein gives two baskets of rice
to the natgadaws, some of whom make offerings to the Nat Yahan, while
others accept the offerings on his behalf. Some of the rice is kept apart for
those who make the offering, so all receive a share. I understand that Nat
Yahan is a pious priestly nat, and wonder if his placation is a sop to
Buddhism similar to the offerings to pongyis which are made at the end of
Bodawgyi's festival at Alon. The natpwè ends with the natgadaws in full
cry round the pandal, imitating the riding of ponies, and calling out that
Komyoshin is riding. They hold thabye leaves in their hands and fans round
which scarves are tied, the fans being held out in front to represent the
ponies. Myinbyushin is-strangely enough-not invited to this pwè, though
his natgadaw is usually present and sometimes takes the part of one of the
other nats.
