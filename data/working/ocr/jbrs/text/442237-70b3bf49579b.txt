GRADING COMMERCIAL VALUES OF SHRIMP PASTE (HMYIN
NGAPI) IN BURMA AND DETECTING ADULTERATIONS WITH
FISH AND SHRIMP PEELS IN IT*
By
H. Wittfogel
The commercial value of any goods is generally defined by a variety of its
different characteristics and special attributes. Even rarity can influence value and price.
The commercial value of food usually depends on its nature, substance and quality, its
nutritive value not being neglected. The nutritive value, however does not always deter-
mine the price as for example delicacies sometimes of low nutritive value, often fetch high
prices which do not correlate with their real nutritive value.
In Burma as well as in other countries of South East and / or Far East Asia
fermented fish products are thought to be very popular in the diet of rice-eating nations.
One of the most flavouring agents of Burma's rather monotonous rice dishes is the so-
called "ngapi" which literally means “pressed fish". In Burma different kinds of ngapi
are being processed using various raw materials.
In general there are two main kinds of ngapi: (a) shrimp paste, hmyin ngapi and (b)
fish paste, nga ngapi. They mostly differ in origin, colour, consistency, smell, taste and
particularly in commercial value. The nutritive value of fish paste is not considered to
be essentially lower than that of shrimp paste, but the price ratio is 5:7, the price of fish
paste made out of whole fishes being compared to that of pure shrimp paste.
In coastal shrimp fisheries the so-called “middlings" represent by-products of
the regular total shrimp catch. These middlings consist of small fishes of various species
(about 12 or more) up to 6 "in length. These middlings are estimated to be about 20%
of the shrimp catch. In order to produce a high-quality shrimp paste it is essential that
these kinds of middlings, being more than 2" in length, are to be selected out of the total
shrimp catch.
It had been proved rather difficult when processing a shrimp paste to pick out
of smallest middlings of 2 in length or less. Even if this selection is done carefully the
work should be supervised by the producer himself. It happens therefore that these
smallest middlings usually remain in the shrimp paste raw materials. They should, how-
ever, in any case not exceed 10% of the total shrimp raw material. This means that

Middlings are too small to be consumed unprocessed or even salted or dried,
their commercial value thereby being extremely low i. e. not more than 1/20 1/15 of the

In Burma these middlings are often included in the paste to become part of it.
But this paste can no more be considered a pure shrimp paste (hmyin ngapi) on account
of its high fish content of 20% or more. The buyer who wants to buy a shrimp paste
will expect an article of nature, substance and quality which it purports to be i.e. shrimp
paste (hmyin ngapi). The fact that the fish content represents of the total weight
of the manufactured shrimp paste offered for sale should be declared and correctly
labelled by the producer or dealer so that buyers will not be deceived and/or over-
charged.
The correct labelling of such a product could be done by branding: “Shrimp
paste with..% of fish". Producers and dealers mostly do not know how much fish the
shrimp paste contains. Therefore, it could be recommended (in accordance with rules
and regulations of other countries concerning similar subjects) to label: "Consumers'
quality shrimp paste", "sa-thone hmyin ngapi". This declaration makes clear that the
“consumers quality" labelled shrimp paste is of second grade quality but quite acceptable
without any objection to being consumed by persons who want to buy a reasonably low-
priced product.
The legal meaning of "adulteration' of food and drugs in general is complied
with (i) if any substance has been mixed or packed with it so as to reduce or lower or
affect injuriously its quality, or (ii) if any substance has been substituted wholly or in
part for the article, or (iii) if any valuable constituent of the article has been wholly or
in part abstracted.
The additional incorporation of fish or shrimp peels in shrimp paste or its raw
materials is to be considered an adulteration of food which can be subject to prosecution
according to the present relevant Food and Drugs Act (1928) in Burma with special view
to Section 3 (1-4) of this Act.
The commercial value of shrimp paste (hmyin ngapi) as well as any adulteration
of it can be detected by separating the digestible flesh components from undigestible,
particles or debris. The greater the volume of undigestible, revealed components in the
sediment, the greater the probability of the commercial value of the paste being low.
The separation can be done by simple floatation in a heavy liquid Carbon Tetrachloride.
The sediment consists mainly of fish bones, fins, scales and shrimp peels.
A microscopic examination will reveal the percentage of fish components amongst
the undigestible sediment by statistical counting. Fish and shrimp particles are identified

according to their histological structure. This can be determined by making a count
of the fish and shrimp particles in a sample under examination. The percentage can
then be very easily determined.
Fish bones, fins and scales have very characteristic, symmetrical structures whereas
the shrimp peels have almost no structure. To be able to differentiate fish head bones
from shrimp peels some histological experience is required. Personnel without any
experience in such work should be trained and advised to do separate examinations of
defatted, dried and minced shrimp peels, fish bones, fins and scales.
Pure shrimp paste will neither have a high sediment of undigestible components
nor a higher amount of incorporated fish than 10%. To relieve undue hardness and to
equalize possible inaccuracy in analysing an allowance limit of 5% should be granted in
such food inspection routine work. Only shrimp paste with a small amount of sediment
and a percentage not exceeding 15% of incorporated fish (10% plus 5% allowance limit)
should be labelled: "pure shrimp paste hmyin ngapi a-cit" or simply "shrimp paste
hmyin ngapi".
Shrimp paste with a considerable volume of sediment and a percentage of incor-
porated fish of not more than 25% (20% plus 5%) should be labelled as a product of
inferior quality and priced accordingly.
Any shrimp paste offered for sale and exceeding 25% of fish content should be
sufficiently declared in an unequivocal and distinct manner and if the price of such a
paste is not accordingly lowered, producers and/or de alers should be prosecuted for having
sold, offered or exposed for sale, or manufactured for sale adulterated food which is not
of the nature, substance or quality which it purports to be.
To grade commercial values of shrimp paste (hmyin ngapi) and detect adultera-
tion of it with fish and/or shrimp paste the following physico-chemical test method
has been worked out:
20g of shrimp paste is taken and washed by being stirred in 150 ml H₂0 for
5 minutes.
The slurry is centrifuged at about 3000 r. p.m. for 5 minutes. The supernatent
liquid is then thrown away.
This process of washing, centrifuging and separating is repeated.
The sediment is recovered and dried in an oven at about 105°C and ground
into powder.
This powder is depleted of fat by being shaken with about 100 ml Ethyl or Pet-
roleum Ether for 3 minutes.

2g of this moisture and fat free shrimp paste is shaken in Carbon Tetrachloride
which is taken and filled up to 15 ml mark in a graduated, conical, centrifugal tube.
However, no centrifuging should be done and the heavy particles are simply allowed to
settle down.
In this case the lighter particles namely the digestible shrimp and fish flesh will
float up and the heavier ones, the undigestible fish bones, fins, scales and shrimp peels
will settle down. The greater the volume which is road directly from the graduated tube,
the greater the amount of undesired undigestible components thus revealing the low
quality or adulteration of the paste.
To identify shrimp and fish components the supernatent liquid with the flesh
particles is thrown away and the sediment cleared up by at least 3 repeated washings in
20% Potassium Hydroxide (KOH) and rinsing 2 or 3 times in slightly acidified distilled
water till the supernatent liquid is free of foam.
The sediment is collected on a petri-disc, dried and examined under a microscope
using low power. Statistical countings are made on a randomly taken 100 particles of a
sample, each count showing the frequency of fish and shrimp. It is recommended that 3
hundred or more particles should be counted. The frequency ratio revealed under mic-
roscope is the ratio of fish and shrimp mutually incorporated in the paste.
To test the above method pastes are prepared with known ratios of fish and
shrimps and these are made to undergo the above mentioned microscopical examination.
To have all preconditions of the experiments as similar as possible to those of the regular
shrimp paste processing conditions, shrimps were collected in a coastal shrimp paste
processing place of the Irrawaddy Delta area after the middlings had been separated.
On the other hand the same middlings of 2 - 6" of length were used experimentally with
the shrimps after they had been minced. It was inevitable that some shrimp debris
remained adherent to the middlings.
We determinad (a) the sediment volume of undigestible components of the
material, (b) the shrimp: fish percentage revealed by microscopical examination and (c)
the average percentage of fish incorporated in 3 hundred particles of every sample.

The above table of results gives you at a first glance the feeling that the ml sediment
findings appear confusing. High sediment volumes are found together with low fish ratios
and low sediments with high ones of incorporated fish. But all samples of "lintote ngapi"
which is made of a particular species of very small pinkish-coloured shrimps and also
those from Mergui and Tavoy area had a low sediment volume of 0.7 ml. Sediment
volumes of under 0.7 ml are positive characteristics of a good quality of shrimp paste.
Samples with high fish ratio have often but not always high sediment volume too.
This seems to be logical because high sediment volume means high amount of undigestible
components in the paste. The small and lean middlings in the paste consist mainly of
undigestible bones, fins and scales, their flesh portion being relatively unimportant.
An explanation for little sediment amount and high fish ratio could be given after
having experimentally minced middlings at different grades (1/8 and 1/32"). It was found
that extremely fine minced fish after having been shaken with Carbon Tetrachloride show-
ed a smaller volume (0.45 ml) of sediment than the same amount of fish consisting of
larger perticles (0.75 ml). Therefore, it has to be concluded that the sediment volume
depends on the mincing grade on the raw material too.
If the sediment amount of any paste is high but the fish ratio low it can be conclud-
ed that big shrimps had been processed or prawns with strong and voluminous peels.
On the other hand a surplus of shrimp peels in the sediment means that they had been
fraudulently included into the paste to increase its bulk and weight. Shrimp and prawn
peels are available in abundance in all shrimp paste processing places where the bigger
shrimps and smaller prawns of the total shrimp catch are dried and after drying peeled.
In any case the sediment volume findings are multiform and heterogenous,
evaluation and interpretation of the findings not being unequivocal. Sediment volumes
0.7 ml are positive characteristics of pure shrimp paste, high sediments 0.7 ml indicate an
inferior quality or adulteration.
Therefore it becomes necessary when grading commercial values of shrimp pastes
and detecting adulteration with fish and shrimp peels, to determine precisely the
percentage of fish components existing amongst the shrimp peels by the more accurate
histological examination and statistical counting.
By this microscopical counting it is possible to find out exactly the percentage of
fish contained in the shrimp paste. The above mentioned experiments made on shrimp
pastes with known ratios of shrimp fish demonstrate quite obviously the effectiveness and
usefulness of the new test method.
"Lintote ngapi" and the pastes from Mergui and Tavoy are considered good quality
shrimp pastes and also those which are processed from good raw material free from
middlings greater than 2". These good quality pastes all show an average percentage of
fish incorporation under 15%. "Lintote ngapi" has an extremely low fish ratio of only
1.3%-
Out of 17 examined, unlabelled samples of shrimp pastes from processing or blend-
ing plants and local markets, 14 had fish ratio higher than 15%, a typical sign of inferior
quality. The fish ratios of 5 samples of the mentioned 17 exceeded 25%, 2 samples having
the extremely high percentage of 31.0% and 27. 3%. In these cases obvious suspicion of

The alarming results of these tests give clear proof that 82.4% of 17 samples which

The described experiments are not only evidence that the new physico-chemical test

Only shrimp pastes with a modest sediment volume and a percentage of incorporated
fish not higher than 15% (10% plus an allowance limit of 5%) should be labelled "pure
shrimp paste", "hmyin ngapi a-cit" or simply "shrimppaste", "hmyin ngapi".
Shrimp paste with a considerable volume of sediment and a percentage of incor-
porated fish up to 25% (20% plus 5%) should be labelled as "consumers quality shrimp
paste", "Sa-thone hmyin ngapi" which means a product of lower quality i.e. of second
grade but quite acceptable for human consumption.
If the fish content of a shrimp paste exceeds 25% and if this fact is not sufficiently
declared and if the price is not accordingly lowered, producers or dealers should be
prosecuted for having sold, offered or exposed for sale adulterated food which is not of
the nature, substance or quality which it purports to be.
Shrimp paste (hmyin ngapi) is an essential part of the every day diet of the Burmese
people, its total annual production being estimated in 1959 as 18-20000 tons. Its nutri-
tive value as a protein to supplement the poor Burmese diet is of great importance. The
Union of Burma Government should therefore make every effort to guarantee the quality
of shrimp paste offered for sale.
SUMMARY
A new physico-chemical test method for grading commercial values of shrimp paste
and for detecting adulteration of it with fish and shrimp peels has been worked out and
checked in numerous tests. The method reveals the determination of the percentage of
fish incorporated in the shrimp paste. By this method the digestible fish components
of the paste are separated from undigestible particles or debris. The greater the volume
of undigestible components in the sediment the lower the commercial value of the paste.
The separation is done by simple floatation in a heavy liquid as Carbon Tetrachloride.
The sediment consists mainly of fish bones, fins, scales and shrimp pects.
The percentage of fish components among the heavy undigestible sediment is
determined by ordinary statistical counting using a microscope, fish and shrimp particles
being identified according to their histological structure.
Sediment volumes 0.7 ml are positive characteristics of pure shrimp paste, high
sediments 0.7 indicate inferior quality or adulteration.
