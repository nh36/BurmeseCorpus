On behalf of the Burma Research Society I thank everybody present at this inaugural meeting
of the Fiftieth Anniversary Celebration of the Burma Research Society. I also extend a very
warm welcome to our friends from foreign countries who are attending this Fiftieth
Anniversary Conference either as participants or observers. The presence of our friends from
foreign countries will surely encourage the Burma Research Society to greater achievements
in the years to come. The Burma Research Society was founded in 1910 by Mr. J. S.
Furnival who was then a member of the Indian Civil Service stationed in Burma which
was then a province of India until 1938 when it was separated from India. Mr. J. S.
Furnival retired from service in 1923. Ever since he set foot on Burmese soil he has been
toiling untiringly and selflessly up to now for the welfare of Burma. Though he is eighty
two years old, I am extremely pleased to mention that he is with us to-day and is still
helping the Burma Research Soeiety very actively to the best of his ability. For his
meritorious service to Burma, the Government of the Union of Burma conferred the title
of Thado Maha Thirithudhama on him in 1950 and the Univesity of Rangoon also
honoured him with the honorary degree of Doctor of Literature in 1957. All members
of the Burma Research Society wish him good health, long life and great happiness in
the years to come. When the younger members of the Society review the activities of
the past years there are two of the oldest members who have been like strong pillars to
the Society since 1914 in the person of Professor G. H. Luce and Professor Pe Maung Tin.
I am extremely pleased to mention that both of them are still quite hale and hearty
inspite of their old age and they are with us here this morning. For their outsanding
services to the University of Rangoon the honorary degree of Doctor of Literature was
conferred on them in 1957. On behalf of the members of the Burma Research Society
I wish Dr. G. H. Luce and Dr. Pe Maung Tin good health, long life and great happiness
in the years to come.
Since the Burma Research Society was founded on 29 March 1910 the members
have been mostly Englishmen and Burmans. The objects of the Society are the study and
encouragement of science and "humanities” in relation to Burma and the promotion
of intellectual and cultural intercourse among members of different communities, especially
between Englishmen and Burmans with a common interest in such objects. The Burma
Research Society is still one of the strongest cultural links between England and the
Union of Burma. I sincerely believe that this link will grow stronger in the years to

was then Superintendent of the Burma Archaeological Department which was started in

During the fifty years of its existence the Burma Research Society has served Burma
very well indeed culturally. Because of the excellent articles in its Journal, it is very well
known and highly respected not only in the West but also in South East Asia. The first
number of the Society's Journal appeared in 1911. With the exception of the years 1942
to 1947 when the hardships of the Second World War and its aftermaths were much felt
in Burma, it appeared regularly twice a year at first until 1914 when it became a quarterly
Journal and again a half-yearly Journal since 1948. The series has now come up to
volume 42. The Journal contains excellent articles on anthropology, archaeology, botany,
Buddhist philosophy, economics, geology, history, linguistics, literature, meteorology and
sociology. Monsieur C. Duroiselle, the Society's first Honorary Secretary and the first
Editor of the Journal said "Such a Journal will, after a few years be a mine of
information, a Burmese Encyclopaedia which is worth much more than the subscription
paid by the members of the Society". What he then said about the Journal was proved
true soon after its appearance. Twenty five years afterwards when the Society held its
Silver Jubilee Meeting on 2 August 1935, the Chairman Sir Thomas Couper spoke
highly in praise of the Journal. He said "The Society's life is its Journal wherein is
enshrined the outcome of its activities” As a matter of fact the Journal has become
indispensable to those who want to know more about Burma. As there has been a great
demand for some time for reprints of the excellent articles in the old Journals, the first
volume of the reprints of the articles is now obtainable from the Honorary Secretary of the
Society at a very moderate price and the second and third volumes of the reprints will
be available in due course. The three volumes will cover about one thousand pages.
Besides publishing the Journal regularly the Society has also undertaken to collect and
edit some works on classical Burmese literature which are on palm-leaf and also on
“parabike" manuscripts. These are historical treatises, local legends, stories, sermons,
belles-lettres, plays, songs, verses and historical records. The Society has already published
forty four books on the subjects mentioned above and has a programme to publish more.
Three Mon texts, one on history, one on prose and one on verse have also been edited
and published. The Society has also published a translation from Burmsese into English
some part of the Glass Palace Chronicle.

From the time of the appearance of the Journal in 1911 up to the time of the outbreak
of the Second World War in 1941 original articles for the Journal were contributed mostly
by Englishmen who were in service either under the Government of Burma or in the
University of Rangoon and also by Indians who were members of the teaching staff of the
many departments of the University of Rangoon. Very few Burmese scholars contributed
articles to the Journal and of these the late U Shwe Zan Aung and Professor Pe Maung Tin
were the most prominent. But since the revival of the Journal in 1948 articles for it
have been contributed mostly by Burma nationals. It is very desirable that an attempt
should be made in the very near future to publish the Journal four times a year and
most of the articles should be contributed by the various departments of the University
of Rangoon and of the University of Mandalay. One of the most important functions
of a University is to encourage research in the faculty of arts and in the faculty of science.
To achieve this object professors should have sufficient leisure which is to be devoted
to research. This would be possible only if the professors in our universities are relieved
of a big share of their responsibilities by increasing the number of competent lecturers
and assistant lecturers. Our universities will not be able to exercise their beneficial
influence to the full until the standard of teaching in our State High Schools improves
considerably. At present much of the time of the teachers in our universities is spent
on teaching which ought to have been done at the school stage. This not only delays
progress but also prevents our professor from doing the full amount of research which is
so important to the studies and the tone for our universities. Therefore I would urge
upon our Vice-Chancellors the necessity for securing for professors adequate leisure for
research and also the importance of providing lecturerships and assistant lecturerships
for younger men in sufficient numbers to relieve the professors of monotonous routine
of daily instruction. Another very important function of our universities is to turn out
level-headed, diligent and well disciplined graduates who would be a credit to the Union of
This objective can be achieved only if sufficient number of teachers are appointed
for the student population of well over ten thousand. The ratio of the number of teachers
to the number of students in our universities is about 1 to 14. By teachers I mean professors,
lecturers, assistant lecturers, demonstrators and tutors. During my recent tour in the
People's Republic of China and North Vietnam, I visited the universities of Peking,
Hsian, Shanghai, Hofei, Hangchow, Wuhan, Kunming and Hanot. In these universities
I found that the ratio of the number of teachers to the number of students is 1 to 8.
Hence teachers in these universities are in a position to give individual attention to the
students. Therefore it is extremely desirable to increase the number of teachers in our
universities. I shall not hesitate to say that the future of the Union of Burma depends
on our universities.
Burma.
Now I would like to talk about subjects for which there is tremendous scope for
research in this country. The subjects which I shall touch upon are archaeology, history and
languages. In 1901 Lord Curzon, the then Viceroy of India visited Pagan and Mandalay
and roused the Government of Burma to a sense of its responsibility to protect for
posterity its great heritage. The Burma Archaeological Department was found in 1902.
The first Superintendent of the Burma Archaeological Department was Mr. Taw Sein Ko who
was at one time the assistant of the roving German archaeologist, Dr. Emil Forchhammer
who, if he had lived longer, would have achieved much for archaeological research in

Burma. Mr. Taw Sein Ko was suceeded by Monsieur Charles Duroiselle, a Frenchman
who accomplished much for the Burma Archaeological Department. The next Superin-
tendent was U Mya who was a patient, tireless and self-effacing scholar of great ability.
Long after his retirement from service he has been re-employed by the Government of

the Archaeological Department. Though over eighty years old he is still discharging his
duties with great zeal and devotion. On U Mya's retirement U Lu Pe Win, a very able
energetic and skillful scholar, became the Superintendent. On U Lu Pe Win's retirement
in 1957 U Po Lat, a very outsanding scholar succeeded him. From 1902 to 1958 the
only important excavation made was by Mr. J. A. Stewart, a member of the Indian Civil
Service and a very able scholar in 1914 at the old city of Pegu. After a lapse of over
forty five years, in June 1959 a very important excavation of an old Pyu city near
Taungdwingyi in Central Burma was started by the Burma Archaeological Department.
The results of the excavation so far are very promising. The work is now continuing.
With sufficient number of trained men and also sufficient funds at the disposal of the Bur-
ma Archaeological Department, I think, it would take quite a few years to excavate the whole
city. A good museum located at Taungdwingyi is now an imperative necessity to conserve
and exhibit all the materials which would be unearthed during the long excavation. There
are many other important historical sites where excavations should start in the very near
future. The sites which need urgent attention are Myohaung in Akyab District, the old city
of Vesali which is about twelve miles north of Myohaung, Prome (Sriksetra) which was
at one time the capital of the Pyu kingdom, Thaton, Pegu, Martaban and Pagan. The ex-
cavations at these sites should unearth materials which would bring to light much of Bur-
ma's past culture.
I shall now talk briefly about the history of Burma. The history of Burma written by
Burmese historians before the regime of King Bodawpaya who died in 1819 is full of inac-
curacies and conjectures. To rewrite the correct history of Burma the Government of the
Union of Burma has set up the Burma Historical Commission in 1956. The Burma Historical
Commission is now collecting authentic material and facts from all reliable sources. During
my recent visit to the Peoples' Republic of China I learnt that the number of national minori-
ties in Yunnan Province which is adjacent to the north eastean part of Burma is twenty. Of
these the Pais are the natives of Tali-fu area which is very close to the Burma border in the
neighbourhood of Bhamo. I learnt from the Professor of History of the University of Kun-
ming, the capital of Yunnan Province that the Pyus whose capital, Prome (Srikşetra)
flourished in the 7th century A.D. were the descendants of the Pais of Yunnan Province.
After the Pyus the Burmans came into Burma from the north between Tibet and China in
the first millenuim A.D. The first capital of the Burmans was Pagan. Burma has had very
close cultural and political contacts with the Chinese for well over a thousand years and
Chinese historical records are very correct and reliable. While I was in Peking I visited the
museum of Chinese history and also the museum of national minorities. There are fifty two
national minorities in People's Republic of China and of these, twenty are the inhabitants
of Yunnan Province. I also visited the archaeological museum at Kunming. In the archaeo-
logical museum at Kunming the names of seventeen kings beginning with King Hsi-nu-lo
and ending with King Yang Kan-Chen who ruled the Kingdom of Yunnan from A.D. 649 to
A.D. 937 during the Tang dynasty of China and also the names of twenty two kings beginning

Before I conclude I would like to say something about the scope of scientific
research in Burma. There are unlimited facilities for research in botany, zoology and
geology in the Union of Burma. In the years 1957 and 1958 the Asia Foundation placed
,,
at the disposal of the Burma Research Society generous grants of which a considerable
portion was utilised to encourage research in science and humanities in many depart-
ments of the University of Rangoon. The Burma Research Society is extremely grateful
to the Asia Foundation for these magnificent grants.
The Burma Research Society is now run mainly by some of the most outstanding
professors of the University of Rangoon. In serving the Burma Research Society these
professors perform their respective duties with great zeal and devotion. In the hands
of these very capable teachers of the University of Rangoon, the Society will surely
gain more strength from year to year.
Ladies and gentleman I thank you very much for listening to my inaugural address
very patiently. I am quite sure that all those who attend the various meetings during
the next few days will benefit much from the learned papers which will be read in these
meetings. On behalf of the Burma Research Society I thank our honoured guests from
foreign countries for gracing this occasion with their presence. I also hope they will
enjoy their stay in Rangoon and also the visit to Pagan next week. In conclusion
I wish this Fiftieth Anniversary Conference of the Burma Research Society a great
success.
