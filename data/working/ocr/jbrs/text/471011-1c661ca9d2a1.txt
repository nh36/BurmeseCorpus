THE DEVELOPMENT OF UNIVERSITY EDUCATION IN BURMA*
Nyi Nyi**
The Universities of modern Burma owe very little to the ancient or medieval centres
of higher learning. Yet the idea of a "Tekkatho" was not new to Burma. With the
assimilation of Buddhist and pre-Buddhist thoughts and ideas, Tekkatho, a word probably
derived from Taksasila became a Burmese household word through the legends and the
Jataka stories.
As related in a Jataka story, it was well known that a sixteen year-old son of a
King of Bayanathi (Benares) went to the distant Tekkatho with a thousand pieces of gold
as a fee to the teacher who was to take him through the various branches of learning.
The Tekkatho was supposed to offer eighteen arts including medicine and surgery,
astronomy and astrology, agriculture and accountancy, archery and snake-charming.
The proper Universities imparting higher learning in early days on the Burmese
soil were the famous monasteries especially in Upper Burma. Some of them were with
traditions dating back several centuries. Buddhist philosophy with Pali in which it is
written affords a life-long study, while astrology and medicine are popular.
Beginnings of Modern University Education
Rangoon College
The earliest dissemination of higher education in Burma was traced to 1878 when
a few pupils of the Rangoon High School were given instruction to enable them to appear
for the First Arts Examination of the University of Calcutta. In 1884-85 a College
Department of the Rangoon High School was affiliated in Arts to the Calcutta University
under the name of Rangoon College.
It
This College came in 1885-86 under the control of the Educational Syndicate.
remained under Syndicate control till 1904 when Government assumed direct management
and was then known as Government College, Rangoon. For the first fifteen years of its
existence, it was housed in the school building. The College building was built only in
1900-01 and the main additions, Chemical and Physical Laboratories, were opened in
1913 and 1915 respectively.

the Calcutta University offering B.A. in English (Pass and Honours), Mathematics (Pass

Judson College
The other constituent college of the University at its founding was Judson College,
known until 1918 as Baptist College. It was a product of the American Baptist Mission's
educational work in Burma, dating back to 1843, and the immediate outgrowth of
Cushing High School which was founded in 1872.
Collegiate instruction began when affiliation for 'First Arts' was obtained from the
University of Calcutta in 1894. Affiliation for the Bachelor of Arts Degree followed in
1909 and for Honours in English and Philosophy a year or two later.
Founding of the University
The arrangement of affiliation to the Calcutta University had never been satisfactory
and voices were raised from time to time for the establishment of a separate University
in Rangoon.
The first proposal to found a University in Burma was made in 1892. The desire
for an institution of University standard free from the control of Calcutta University was
fostered by the press, by the Educational Syndicate and by groups of leaders of Burmese
opinion and grew steadily in strength. It was believed that a separate University in
Burma would encourage higher education in the province by stimulating the opening of
colleges to provide degree courses not only in Arts and Science subjects, but also in
Medicine and Engineering.
Although the affiliation to Calcutta had long been regarded as inconvenient, it had
been accepted largely because of the trouble and expense of making the change. However,
when the Calcutta University came to be regarded as the hot-bed of sedition and anti-
government agitation, the argument in favour of a separate university gained force.
It was not till 1907 that concrete proposals were made for founding a University in
Burma and in 1910, representations of Burma's needs drew an announcement in the
British House of Commons from the Under Secretary of State for India that the Govern-
ment of India hoped to establish a separate University for Burma as part of their general
policy in higher education. This was followed in 1913 by the announcement of the
Government of India in an Education Resolution that the establishment of a University

In June 1918, a Committee of Experts with the newly appointed Director of Public
Instruction Mr. Mark Hunter (later Sir Mark Hunter) as its head was constituted to draw

The Committee had the advantage of studying the Report of the Calcutta University

(i) that the University should be of a centralized residential type;
(ii) that provision be made to enable academic matters to be dealt with by
purely academic bodies;
(iii) thai there should be provision for co-ordination of the academic and
administrative bodies, and
(iv) that University affairs be regulated, under Government protection, on a
self-governing basis.
The University of Rangoon Act was passed by the Burma Legislative Council in Sep-
tember 1920 and received the assent of the Governor-General in Council on 24th
October 1920.
The Act empowers the University to provide for instruction in various branches
of learning; to hold examinations; to appoint Professors, Readers, Lecturers, etc. to
institute and grant scholarships, Prizes, etc.; to found, erect, equip and maintain libraries,
laboratories, museums, etc.; to supervise and control the residence and discipline of
students, etc. It also defines the authority of the University as the Chancellor, the Vice-
Chancellor, the Council and the Senate.
It was first conceived to found the University as a ‘unitary' institution. The attempt
was however unsuccessful due to the insistence of the American Baptist Mission authori-
ties that they should be constituted a separate college within the University. It is under-
stood that the authorities in favour of the unitary organisation were overruled by Whitehall
where pressure was exerted from Washington.2 Thus Rangoon College and Judson
College became the first two Constituent Colleges at the founding of the University on 1st
December 1920. However in Article 18(2) of the Act, a proviso was left open for possible
amalgamation of the two colleges, if it could be agreed upon within five years of the Act
coming into force.

The New University
The First Decade
The New University had a total of 829 students comprising 692 in University
College and 137 in Judson College. The University's first Chancellor was Sir Reginald
Henry Craddock, and Sir Charles Morgan Webb was appointed the first Vice-Chancellor.
The College Principals were Prof. M. Hunter for U.C. and Rev. E.W. Kelly for J.C. and
Mr. C.W. Ainley served as the first Registrar.
The first Professors of the University were D.J. Sloss (English), C. Duroiselle
(Oriental Studies), L.G. Owen (Mathematics), M. Hunter (Chemistry), K.M. Ward
(Physics), G.R.T. Ross (Philosophy), D.G.E. Hall (History) and U May Oung (Law).
Except Professor K.M. Ward (6.4.1921) and Professor D.G.E. Hall (16.5.1921), all other
Professors were appointed on the date of incorporation of the University. Although
provisions existed for chairs in Biology, Education and Economics, no appointments were
made in the year of its establishment.
The University received from the Director of Public Instruction the residue of the
Burma University Fund consisting at the moment of transfer of Rs.1,40,027-2-4 of which
Rs. 1,20,000 was at fixed deposit and Rs.20,027-2-4 in current account. The balance of the
Imprest Fund amounting to Rs. 49-14 was also taken over and Government securities
aggregating Rs. 3,43,200 were also made over to the University.3
Before 1920, the Colleges were able to offer only English, Mathematics, Pali, Logic,
Chemistry and Physics to the Intermediates and English, Mathematics, Pali, Chemistry,
Physics and Philosophy to the Bachelor students. In the new University, Oriental Studies
(later Burmese Language and Literature), History, Biology, Economics, Geography, Geo-
logy, Forestry, Education and Engineering were introduced in addition to the subjects taught.
First Convocation
The first Convocation of the University was held on the 28th November 1921 at
which the new Chancellor Sir Reginald Craddock presided. Two Honorary Degrees were
conferred LL.D. (honoris causa) to Aggamahapandita Nyana Maha Thera, Ledi Sayadaw
and M.A. (honoris causa) to Mr. Chas. Duroiselle, Superintendent of Archaeological
Survey, Burma.

First Students' Strike
15
There was however much dissatisfaction among the student body of the two colleges
with the Act. It was generally felt that the new Act imposed undue restraints in order to
limit the size and products of the University. From the dawn of modern nationalism
Burmans had looked to education as an instrument of national advancement and the
various restraints were therefore keenly felt.
When the University was thus opened on 1st December 1920 despite the protests of
the country and the plea to postpone a measure of such great national importance for
consideration by the new reformed legislature about to be inaugurated, this was immedia-
tely followed by a boycott (on 5th December 1920) which soon spread rapidly to the
schools over the country.
On 19th December 1920, the strikers submitted a memorial to the Chancellor
through the Registrar listing the following demands: (see also Appendix A):-
1. The University being residential and unitary and not federal will result in hardship
on the students in the matter of expenses. It will make higher education prohibitive.
2. Memorialists do not see the necessity of insisting on proficiency in English and in one
other subject in qualifying for Matriculation.
3. The adding of the Preliminary course will only add to the difficulty of the students by
needlessly prolonging the period of study and adding to the already heavy expenses.
4. Submit that Oral Examinations are not necessary in the College Department.
5. Do not see any valid reason why an Ordinary Pass Graduate should be disqualified
from trying the Master's Degree.
6. The rules operate still more harshly in view of the rule that Honours students are not
allowed to attempt more than once.
7. Submit that candidates for Master's Degree should be ranked in classes if they succeed
in their second or subsequent attempts.
8. Submit that the three years' maximum time limit for the students who have passed
their Intermediate examinations to sit for their Honours Degree is too drastic.
9. Likewise the time limit for the Master's Degree should be removed.
10. Do not see the necessity of English Composition Paper for Science Degree (Ordinary
and Honours).
11. Submit that the composition of the Council and of the Senate should be reconsidered
so that they may represent all classes of people and all shades of opinion.
12. Submit that in these days of democratic institutions the Vice-Chancellor, the Pro-
Chancellor and the University Professors should be elected by the Senate, subject to
the Chancellor's veto, and not appointed by the Chancellor directly.
13. Provisions should be made for examinations by compartment.
14. Provision should be made for Law students to attend morning sessions.

The strike was started as an educational one ostensibly to repeal the University Act
to enable the newly-founded institution to serve the wider national needs. Yet its main
significance undoubtedly was political. It was the first organized attempt by the intelli-
gentsia in expressing disobedience to the foreign rule. This strike served as a landmark
in Burma's struggle for freedom and was indeed a turning point in the nation's history.
1924 Amendment Act
A Bill for amending the University of Rangoon Act (1920) was introduced in the
Burma Legislative Council in November 1923. The Amending Bill was referred to a
Select Committee whose report was discussed in March 1924, and the Bill, as amended,
passed, the Governor-General's assent being obtained in April 1924. The University of
Rangoon Act as amended in 1924 provided, among other things, for the affiliation of
colleges in other cities of Burma, the representation of popular interests and public bodies
on an enlarged Council, and a definition of the powers and functions of this body.
The Act, as amended, appeared to satisfy the demands of the public, and as a
concrete result the Mandalay Intermediate College was established in June 1925.
It may be noted that, while the 1920 Act was passed by the Lieutenant-Governor's
Legislative Council, the Amendment Act of 1924 came before a Council, constituted under
the Montague-Chelmsford Reforms, which made ‘Education' a Transferred subject.

The end of the First Decade
When the University was first incorporated in 1920, there were only six professors
and 829 students. With the occurrence of the First Students' Strike, the number on the
rolls fell to 331 on 31st March 1921. However, by 1923-24 the number of students had
risen again to 1026.
At the end of the first decade, the student number rose to 1550 and the Professors
totalled 21, viz. W. G. Fraser (English), D.G.E. Hall (History), U Pe Maung Tin (Oriental
Studies), E. H. Solomon (Economics) (Offg.), W. St. John (Philosophy), L. G. Owen
(Mathematics), N. C. K. Aiyar (Physics), D. H. Peacock (Chemistry), F. J. Meggitt
(Biology), C. G. Beasley (Geology and Geography) (filled only from 5-2-1931), G. F.
Clark (Education), H. C. E. Cherry (Civil Engineering) (Offg.), R.A. Cochrane (Forestry),
A. Eggar (Law), Lt. Col. L. A. H. Lack (Anatomy), Major G. R. McRobert (Physiology),
Major R. V. Morrison (Medicine), Lt. Col. H. B. Scott (Surgery), Major C. de C. Martin
(Pathology), Lt. Col. S. T. Crump (Midwifery and Gynaecology), and Lt. Col. T. F.
Owens (Forensic Medicine).
The two notable events in 1930 were the institution of Teachers' Training College
and Medical College by University Statutes as Constituent Colleges of the University.
Prof. T. F. Owens was appointed Principal of the Medical College on 24th September
1930 and Prof. G. F. Clark as Principal of the T.T.C. on 1st October 1930.

R.U.S.U.
The desirability and necessity of a Students' Union were recognized even in the
early days of the establishment of the University but however it was not materialized
until 1926-27, when Dr. U Nyo, K.S.M., LL.D., very generously donated a sum of
Rs. 1,70,000 for a University Students' Union in response to the appeal of the then
Chancellor Sir Harcourt Butler for an Endowment Fund.
The building construction began in 1929 and the Union building was completed by
the middle of 1930. With the permission of Mr. Justice U Ba, then Pro-Vice-Chancellor,
a mass meeting of the students was held on the 20th September 1930 in the new Gymna-
sium Hall with U Kyaw Khin in the chair. The meeting after thanking Dr. U Nyo for
his munificient gift, elected a committee comprising U Kyaw Khin (Chairman), U Ba
Gyan (Secretary), U Kyaw Thein, U Tint Swe, U Tun Tin, U Ba Thein, Mr. M. Yasin,
U Ohn, Mr. Parakasan, U San Phaw Aung, U Ba Pe and U Nyun to draw up the consti-
tution of the Union.
After several modifications the draft constitution was adopted by the students on
9th January 1931, when the first Executive Committee of the Union was also elected.
However, in view of the conflicting views of the University authorities and the Union as
regards membership of the Union, further negotiations took place and the constitution
finally emended was adopted by the Union on 27th February 1931.
The Rangoon University Students' Union was modelled after the Oxford and
Cambridge Unions with debates as a cardinal feature. The objects of the Union were
defined as promotion of a healthy corporate social life, inculcating a spirit of independence,
and creation of a sense of duty and responsibility among the students of the University.
However, in countries where freedom is yet to be achieved, it is inevitable that
students' union soon became part of the freedom movement.

The University Act Enquiry Committee
However, on the 4th March 1936, a discussion took place in the Burma Legislative
Council on the motion of one of its Members for the appointment of a Committee
composed of Members of the Legislative Council to enquire into the causes of the Univer-
sity strikes. As a result of this discussion, the Government of Burma (Ministry of
Education) in its Resolution No. 9QA35, dated the 1st May 1936, appointed a Committee
to examine the Rangoon University Act and to advise the Local Government what
changes, if any, were desirable in the Act. The Committee comprised Mr. Justice
Mya Bu (Chairman), Dr. R. P. Paranjpye, Vice-Chancellor, University of Lucknow,
Mr. B. W. Swithin-bank, Dr. G. S. Jury, Professor Pe Maung Tin, Dr. Ba Han, U Thein
Maung, U Paw Tun, Dr. Daw Yin May, Dr. M. A. Rauf and Mr. L. Htin Si (Secretary).
U Thein Maung however resigned his membership before the Report was being drafted
on being appointed Education Minister.
The Report was submitted to the Government in March 1937. (The main propo-
sals of it are summarized in Appendix C). Government afterwards invited persons and
institutions interested to communicate their views on the several proposals of the Report.
The University also expressed its views after holding several meetings of the Senate and
the Council. (The University's comments are summarized in Appendix D). After
examination of all the opinions, Government proceeded to draft the University of
Rangoon Act (Amendment) Bill resulting in the Amendment Acts of 1939.

The 1938 University Strike

However, before the University could be settled down to its work, yet another
upheaval took place on the campus-a situation undoubtedly reflecting the generally
unsettled political and socio-economic conditions of the country at the time.
The year 1938 was an unsettled year. The Burmese-Muslim riots took place about
the end of July to September and on 8th September 1938 the Day and Hostel students of
the University College presented the Principal (U Pe Maung Tin) with a request to close
the College early for the October holidays on account of the unsettled state in Rangoon.
The Governing Body of the College ceded to the request and the College was closed from
13th September 1938 to 8th November 1938 except Honours and professional students
who continued till the end of the usual term, i.e. 5th October 1938.
There was a lull of about a month early in the second term as U Ba Hein and
U Ba Swe (the President and Secretary of the RUSU respectively) left the College for
Magwe to join the oilfield strike on the 12th December and were promptly arrested
there.7 This incident aroused the feelings of the University students and mass protest
meetings were held in various parts of Rangoon and street demonstrations were also
conducted.
On the 20th December, a group of students led by the RUSU picketed the Secre-
tariat Buildings and as a result of the clash between the students and the police, a number
of students were injured and one student of the Judson College (Ko Aung Gyaw)
succumbed to the injuries a few days later. The following evening (i.e. 21st December
1938) after a meeting held at the Union, the students declared a boycott against the
University. There was strenuous and heavy picketing of the University and Judson
Colleges resulting in the entire dislocation of academic and administrative work.
This strike really had political reasons for its raison de etre and "the boycotters
declared that they had no grievance against the College or the University and that they
were boycotting the College (U.C.) by way of showing their displeasure against the
Governments. The strike was later called off as the result of the agreement reached
between the students' leaders and the new Government, two of the terms of agreement
being the general amnesty to all students in the country who participated in the School or
College strikes and the holding of a Supplementary Examination in June.

Suspension of the University
The War Interlude
With the outbreak of war with Japan on 8th December 1941 and due to the State
of emergency prevailing at the time in Burma, the University of Rangoon Act, 1920, as
subsequently amended was suspended from the 15th January 1942 by the University of
Rangoon Act (Temporary Suspension) Ordinance, 1942 and later by the University of
Rangoon (Suspension) Act, 1943. Under the Suspension Act, a Board of Trustees was
constituted with all the "rights, privileges, duties, powers and functions" previously
vested in the Authorities of the University.
Before the evacuation, the powers and duties of the Authorities of the University
were vested in certain administrative officers--those acting at the time of evacuation being
U Kyaw Din, Education Secretary and Professor C. G. Beasley. On the arrival of the
University office in India Mr. Payton, Chief Secretary was appointed in U Kyaw Din's
place; later, on being released from the Army, Mr. L. Htin Si was appointed one of the
Joint Administrative Officers. Subsequently Sir John Wise took Mr. Payton's place.
These Administrative Officers functioned as the Trustees of the University under the
University (Suspension) Act. In September 1945, two further Trustees viz. Professor
B. R. Pearn and U Tharawaddy Maung Maung were appointed.

The period from 1949 to 1962 may perhaps be best termed the period of expansion

Growth in Numbers
a. Student numbers
During war-time planning in India, the Interim University Committee envisaged a
student body of 500 in the first year of its re-opening after the war and plans were made
accordingly with that figure in mind.
However, when the Interim University was opened in 1946, the demand
for admission was far in excess of the most liberal estimates of the planners. It
was 2003, four times the size of the original stipulated figure. It was composed of
pre-evacuation students whose studies were interrupted by the war, students who studied
in the State University during the Japanese regime, students who studied in the Emergency
University Classes and those who had matriculated since liberation. One notable feature
was that out of the 2003 students, 1349 (67.3%) were in the Intermediate classes and the
Junior Intermediate classes alone accounted for 1090 (54.4%) of the total number.
Since 1946 the student numbers have increased year by year, passing the 10,000
mark for the Rangoon University alone in 1956. The general trend of increase was
broken only three times, firstly in 1949 when the insurrection was at its peak, secondly in
1957 when the results of the Matriculation and High School Final Examination were
declared for the first time in two lists, i.e., List A of Matriculates and List B of High
School Finalists, and thirdly in 1959 when the standard of Matriculation was somewhat
strictly maintained.
This great increase in the number of students seeking higher education in this
country may be attributed to many causes. The prime cause seems to be the national
urge for higher education due to the awakening by the war and the new opportunities
and venues opened up by independence. Other causes may perhaps be summarized as
due to the over-emphasis of academic learning in the schools, unsatisfactory examination
system, prevalence of the spirit of procrastination due to the existence of supplementary
examinations thus causing excessive failures and also probably the grant of free tuition by
Government since 1951.

Before the outbreak of the Second World War, there were as many as 18 different
categories of staff appointments which included members of the Indian and Burma Edu-

(c) Staff-Student Ratios
In 1920 when the University was first founded it had 829 students and a staff of
only 48, thus having a staff-student ratio of 1:17. This situation was much improved by
1930 when the staff number increased to 170 for a student population of 1550, thus having
a ratio of 1:9. The situation was improved much further just before the outbreak of the
Second World War.
The ratios for the post-war years, however, have been a constant bleak picture
fluctuating between 1:15 to 1:20 for total staff members, while the ratios for lecturing
members never descend below 1:20 except in the exceptional years of 1949-50. It is
indeed rather sad to compare this figure with the gradually improving situation in univer-
sities abroad. For instance, in the British Universities the staff-student ratio for the
pre-war years was about 1:10 (1938-39) and for 1956-57 and later years being 1:7; and
even in the People's Republic of China, the general ratio is about 1:6.

Academic Changes
(a) Changes in University Organisation
As stated above, the University of Rangoon was reorganised on unitary pattern in
1946 and the first three faculties established with effect from 26th September 1946 were
Medical, Education and Engineering Faculties. They were followed by Faculties of Law,
Arts and Science on 1st June 1947, Agriculture in 1948, Social Science on 1st April
1958, and Forestry on 1st June 1958. However, the Faculty of Forestry was reorganised
as a department in 1959.
The Intermediate College of Mandalay, founded in 1925 was reopened in 1947 with
U Ko Lay, Lecturer in Chemistry as its Principal. It was raised to degree status and thus
became a University College in 1948 affiliating to the Rangoon University. In 1958, the
College became a separate University on unitary pattern, through a Parliamentary
enactment. With the establishment of the new Mandalay University, the Faculty of
Agriculture was transferred to the new University at Mandalay.
In order to accommodate the growing student body, several Indermediate Colleges
were opened during the post-war years, viz. Moulmein (1953), Kyaukpyu (1954),
Yankin (1954), Hteedan (1954), Magwe (1955), and Bassein (1958)—Yankin and Hteedan
being Branch Faculties teaching only the First Year Intermediates. Magwe College was
transferred to the Mandalay University in 1958 on the latter's establishment and the
teaching of Intermediate of Arts and Science in Yankin and Hteedan was transferred to
the Intermediate Colleges of Science & Arts in Rangoon in 1961.13
Another affiliated college was the Defence Services Academy established in 1953,
offering its own courses but under the general guidance of the Senate of the Rangoon
University.

(c) University Examinations
Just before the outbreak of the Second World War, the University authorities had
generally agreed to reduce the Pass Degree Course to three years and to hold its own
Matriculation Examination. This scheme was further persued and incorporated in the
Simla Educational Plan. Thus according to the Simla Plan, the post-war schools were
to be classified into Primary schools for First to Fifth standards, Post-Primary schools for
Sixth to Ninth standards, Pre-University schools for Tenth and Eleventh standards and
the University would have only 3-year Degree courses.
On return to Burma, the British Military Administration set up in accordance with
its preconceived plan Primary and Post-Primary schools in most areas and also two Pre-
University schools. But the Education Policy Enquiry Committee formed at the time did
nor favour the opening of Pre-University schools, and with the consequent abolishment of
these schools, the University courses reverted to its original duration of four years. Along
with it, the June Supplementary Examinations and Compartmental System introduced just
before the Second World War were revived.
However, as it was felt that the Compartmental System was not educationally sound
and that annual examinations would give students better opportunities of passing their
examinations than the prevalent bi-annual examinations, the Intermediate and Bachelor
courses were divided into Parts A and B from 1951-52 session with a University Examina-
tion at the end of each year and Compartmental privileges were withdrawn for internal
students from that year onwards.
Along with the introduction of Part A and Part B system, the University authorities
also thought of introducing a regulation whereby a student may appear consecutively
only twice in each class as an internal student. It was, however, delayed due to various
circumstances untill 1955 when the well-known “3F” Rule was introduced. According to
this rule, a student may appear only three times consecutively as an internal student
although the number of times he could appear as an external student remains unlimited.
In 1959, the Education Enquiry Committee submitted its Interim Report advocating
the abolishment of the June Supplementary Examinations. The recommendation was
accepted by both the University authorities and Government. It was to have been
implemented in June 1960, but due to circumstances prevailing at the time it was deferred
until June 1961.

(d) Matriculation Examination
Prior to the outbreak of the Second World War, the Education Directorate held
annual High School Final Examinations and results were declared in two lists-"A" List
for matriculates and "B" List for non-matriculates. This practice was, however, found
unsatisfactory by the University authorities as they could not set the standard of matricu-
lation and besides, it was felt that the aims of matriculation and school final examinations
were dissimilar in nature. The University was thus in the process of holding its own
Matriculation Examination when the Second World War reached Burma.
After the war, this ideal was put into practice and the University held its first
Matriculation Examination in 1946. In 1951, the School Leaving Certificate Examination
held by the Directorate of Educaton was combined with the Matriculation Examination
and became known as the High School Final & Matriculation Examination. In 1959, the
Education Enquiry Committee recommended in its interim report the separation of the
two examinations and the recommendation was implemented in 1961 thus reverting to
its original scheme.
In 1946 when the Matriculation Examination was first held, the only requisite
qualification for entry was the age limit-the attainment of 16 years of age and it was soon
found that many unqualified students with little schooling were offering themselves as
candidates thus leading to increasing rates of failures. Therefore, from the 1955 Examina-
tion, another requirement for entry was prescribed, viz., that all candidates must have
passed the Seventh Standard Examination, followed by two-year study prior to the entry
of the Matriculation. According to the latest modification of requirements, candidates must
now have passed the High School Final Examination and studied an extra year before
presenting themselves at the Matriculation Examination.16

Medium of Instruction
The medium of instruction at the University has been English since its incorpor-
ation. In the pre-Second World War years, the trend of events in general education in
the Anglo-Vernacular Schools in Burma was gradually moving towards employment of
Burmese as the medium of instruction and as pointed out in the Report of the Verna-
cular and Vocational Education Reorganisation Committee (the Campbell Report) (p. 274),
"the general introduction of English as a second language in the vernacular curriculum
has become a widespread and persistent demand”.
The Rangoon University Act Enquiry Committee of 1936 examined this question
of vernacularisation of instruction in the University in subjects other than English
itself, and considered that it would be rash on their part “to hazard any view of what
is the proper course for the academic body to adopt". The decision was left to the
University Senate which in the Committee's opinion will have to consider the general
question of desirability or not, the problems of an adequate supply of text-books and
teaching staff, the practical usefulness of the students after their graduation and the
recognition of its degrees in the rest of the academic world.

In Simla during war-time, the Education Reconstruction Committee reconsidered
the question of the medium of instruction and reported that "for the present the main

reconstructed education system is functioning, and Burmese as a medium of instruction
has been developed in the schools, and when an adequate book production in Burmese

The question was further studied by the University Organisation Committee (1947)
who devoted a whole chapter in their report to it. According to the report, the majority
of witnesses were in favour of the principle that Burmese should be the medium of
instruction at the University stage provided the following conditions were fulfilled:-
(1) A sufficient number of text-books and reference books in Burmese in the various
subjects of a sufficiently high standard should first be made available by the
institution of a Translation Bureau.
(2) The text-books and reference books should be kept up-to-date by frequent
revision.
(3) Sufficient time should be allowed to enable the standard of teaching in the
secondary schools, through the new medium, to rise to the pre-war level, before
the proposal is implemented in toto.

Some Attempts At University Reform
Since the revival of the University Act in 1949, many attempts of University Reform
have been made in the course of the past 14 years. There were five Amendment Acts-two
in 1949, one each in 1951, 1953 and 1959, and several committees and sub-committees of
and on the University formed by the Government or by the University Council or Senate.
Amongst these Committees those with real or potential importance are:-University Needs
Committee, 1951; Council Sub-Committee on the Aims of University Education, 1953;
Council Sub-Committee on 3F, Attendance, etc., 1956; Education Enquiry Committee,
1957 and University Education Committee of the Union Planning Seminar, 1958.
1949 Amendment Acts
After the revival of the University Act by the University (Suspension) (Withdrawal)
Act, 1949, the University of Rangoon Act, 1920, as subsequently amended was further
amended by the Amendment Act of 1949 (IX of 1949). This Amendment Act radically
changed the structure and organisation of the University. The constituent colleges dis-
appeared and the University was reorganised in a unitary pattern. The functions which
were previously dispersed between the University and the constituent colleges became
concentrated in the University.
The Executive Committee of the Council was abolished and the Council, greatly
reduced in its membership, was no longer the deliberative and reviewing authority,
meeting twice a year, but became a powerful, authoritative body meeting at least once a
month. The office of Chancellor was also no longer elective - the Prime Minister of the
Union being the ex-officio occupant of the office.

A temporary Amendment Act (XXVIII of 1949) was also passed in 1949 to legally
ensure the representation of the Members of the Constituent Assembly and the three
representatives of the registered graduates already elected under the previous Amendment
Act.

(b) take exception to the principle of ensuring the majority on administrative

(c) recommended that in the conferment of degrees, honoris causa, both the

results and to determine persons on whom ordinary degrees should be
conferred, be transferred from the Executive Committee to the Senate.
In this connection it may be pointed out that in the Act there exists some confusion

The University authorities recommend that these duties be scrutinized and that those
properly belonging to the academic body (such as the appoinment and dismissal of

(24) The University should be developed on “unitary" lines.
(25) Religious and communal bodies may be allowed to establish residential

(26) The University should be controlled by a smaller Council representative of

(27) The constitution of the University should provide and secure that it should

medium of University education parallel to English.
