SIAM IN TRANSITION. By Kenneth Perry Landon. (Chicago,
The University of Chicago Press, and Shanghai, Kelly and Walsh, 1939.
ix + 323 pp. U. S. $2.50.)
One evidence of the widening circle of American scholarship is the ap-
pearance of numerous special studies respecting areas peripheral to the Far
East as that term is generally understood. Within the past decade a succession
of these studies has been produced, and there is room for others. Professor
Landon's book is, so far as this reviewer knows, the first competent survey
of Siam published by an American during the present century.
As indicated by the subtitle, the volume is a "brief survey of cultural
trends in the five years since the revolution of 1932." So many questions
come to mind relative to Siam that one wishes the scope of the investigation
had been widened. Three introductory chapters on politics and economy
during the past decade bring to light the obscure causes of the revolutions of
1932 and 1933. Rather full treatment of economic conditions in what we
should now call Thailand reveal the essentially strong position of the country,
its foreign trade, and its comfortable bank holdings. Siam's favourable
balance of trade and well-filled treasury, with sufficient cash and securities
in the vaults to pay the national debt on demand, are perhaps ample conso-
lation for being considered somewhat backward as compared with countries.
having imposing public buildings and massive annual deficits.
Despite a certain amount of Siamese lassitude and pleasant indolence, a
delightful irresponsibility, and a persistent aversion for soiling the hands in
business, one concludes that Siam in transition to the modern world has ac-
quired a residue of political genius and force which augurs well for the future
of Thailand. It remains a pivotal point in Southeast Asia. From the evi-
dence submitted, Sir George Scott could no longer classify Siam as
country rich enough to inspire cupidity, weak enough to tempt ambition, and
foolish enough to court embroilments.”

In addition to the chapters on political and economy, the sections on
social life and religion appear particularly significant and well done. How-
ever, the number of murders for an average year is given as 1,333 which
seems hardly suitable support for the statement that ".... Siam does not
have a crime problem." Let us assume on the basis of this book that the
bugaboo of alleged foreign plans for political domination of Siam has been
well and truly laid, at any rate for the present. One wishes that place had
been found for adequate treatment of such problems as, for example, the
much-discussed canal at the Isthmus of Kra and reported Siamese ambitions.

Although the index is somewhat sketchy, the bibliography lists the im-
portant materials in English and Siamese, the latter, unfortunately, being
practically unobtainable outside Siam. This meaty book calls attention to the
need for accumulating adequate library materials for research in Southeast
Asia. Appendix III is particularly valuable since it reproduces in full the
unique national economic proposals of Luang Pradist Manudharm; this re-
markable plan for conservative Siam, should its revolutionary ideas become
widely known in Siamese thought and action, may well go down in history
as the San Min Chu I of Thailand.
Printed by Kelly and Walsh, Shanghai, and the careful work of one who
was resident eleven years in Thailand, Siam in Transition is worth more than
cursory study by all who would know Asia. It is available also in a British
edition through the Oxford University Press.
