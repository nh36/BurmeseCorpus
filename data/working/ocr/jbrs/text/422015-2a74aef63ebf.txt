At the beginning I would like to say that it is a great honour for me to
deliver a paper on a rare occassion like this, and to such a distinguished
audience. At the same time I would like to ask you for indulgence for the
limited time, both for preparing and delivering this paper, has not allowed
me to present before you more than several hints on the subject which
has been the theme of my preliminary study; the study--in fact a diploma
work, submitted to the Charles University in Prague can by no means be
considered exhaustive or satisfactory, bearing all the mistakes of a beginner.
In its present form it is no more than a preliminary study, still very far
from perfection
In order to comprehend Collis' novels on Burma it is necessary to be
acquainted with his life and his other works. For this reason I shall give
here some of these facts.
Maurice Collis was born on 10 January 1889, in Dublin, Ireland, as
a son of a lawyer. He received his education at Rugby and Oxford where
he took history, graduating with First Class Honours. Following his earlier
interest in the East, he entered the Indian Civil Service and in 1912 was
sent to Burma. In the years 1912 - 1935 he acted as a British administrator,
occupying numerous posts ranging from that of an assistant commissioner
of a country subdivision to that of the District Magistrate for the Rangoon
Town District. In 1935 he retired and went back to England and since
then, he devoted himself to literature and art criticism.
There were two remarkable features about Collis, the administrator,
distinguishing him sharply from the great majority of other British colonial
officials. One was his friendly attitude to the Burmese and the other his
political liberalism. Collis had many friends among the Burmese and his
interest in their way of life, habits, history and culture made him often prefer
to see a Burmese drama to the attendance of an English club. During his

of the country people's beliefs and mentality that there were moments when he
himself was able to look at the world with their eyes, to feel in the way they did.
Or how else could we explain the strange fact that he met ghosts in Burma?
Among his own countrymen his political liberalism won him a reputation
of "lowering the British prestige” and being “anti-British”. But we must not
forget that though a sharp critic of colonial policy, Collis at the same time
believed that the British must educate and lead the Burmese towards the
establishment of a modern state.
Since 1935 when he retired in England, Collis wrote a great number of
books. Besides novels, romances and drama, which form only a fraction of his
literary production, the main bulk of Collis' work are non-fictional books like
histories, biographies, autobiographies or travel books. With one or two
exceptions these works are of a historical character, dealing with Eastern
lands, namely Burma and the surrounding countries, their general tendency
being to show the penetration of Europeans to the East. Thus, considering
century after century, as described in various Collis' works, a whole gallery
of portraits is presented before us: the 13th century traveller Marco Polo,
Portuguese mercenaries and fillibusters, followed by Chistain missionaries,
the East India Company, English merchants and later administrators like
Collis or Governor Dorman Smith. All these are symptoms of Western
penetration to the East, the development of economic exploitation, succeeded
by political domination of the West over the East. In these works Collis has
challenged the generally accepted opinion that the West brought civilization
to the East by clearly pointing out that though by the 16th century and after
the West was more advanced in the sphere of science and technique which
helped it to politically dominate the East, the Eastern culture and civilization,
namely that of China, were not only much older than the European but also
highly superior to it. For what happened when the Westerners, who claimed
to be bearers of "higher civilization", came to the East? How did they behave
and what was the real purpose of their coming? The author never leaves his
reader doubting; the acts and often base behaviour of all adventures and
pirates, merchants and even administrators of the colonial government
were determined by the sole aim of getting immense sums of money from the
East, of squeezing the highest possible profit. Under the label of "the white
man's burden" the actual moral was the principle of "squeeze and grab".
I think that in this exposition of the theory of the Western world bringing
civilization to the backward Eastern savages lies one of the main contri-
butions of Collis' non-fictional work.
And now to our main theme. Collis has written altogether three novels
on Burma: She Was a Queen, a historical novel, and two novels with a
contemporary setting- Sanda Mala and the The Dark Door.

She Was a Queen published in 1937, is one of Collis' first books and his first
work of fiction. Based on the fifth part of The Glass Palace Chronicle, Collis
relates the story of Ma Saw, a village girl who became Chief Queen of
King Usana and his successor Narathihapate, the last king of the Pagan
Dynasty, an intelligent woman who found herself involved in politics, having
to sacrifice her own personal happiness for the good of the country.
In this book Collis not only delienated an adequate picture of the Pagan
court with figures like Yazathingyan, the clever and intriguing Chief Minister
or the despotic tyrant Narathihapate, but also managed to "catch" very
much of a true Burmese atmosphere which, intermingled with artistic
elements, makes some of the places of this historical novel so effective.
Succeeding in depicting the court, however, Collis has rather failed in an-
other matter. As U Minn Latt has observed in his study "Burma and Her
Reflection in English Letters", in this novel-similarly as in his other
books Collis does not pay enough attention to the life conditions of the
Burmese people. He restricts his attention almost only to the palace; if
he takes us a few times to the village, it is only to show a picture of an
idyllic, peaceful scenery, a contrast to the corrupt court, though, as
U Minn Latt has pointed out "especially in "She Was a Queen" (Collis)
had an excellent opportunity of describing the suffering of the people under
the oppressive king but failed to do so." In this regard Collis seems to be
too closely following his sources which simply do not bother with the life and
conditions of the lower classes.
All events and persons in this novel are not historically true and not
exactly taken from the Glass Palace Chronicle. This fact provoked a highly
indignant critique in : (The Comrade) October 1953, under
the title "A Book Against Which Action Should Be
Taken". Being scandalized by the fact that one of the minor characters
of Collis' book, a monk called Paṁsukū, was described by Collis as
"a drunkard, getting mixed up with women", the critic claimed that the
character was no one else than mo The Venerable Paṁsukū-
worshipped and liked by the people as a rahan in the Pagan period. According
to the critic Collis presented the monk in such a bad light in order to
abuse Buddhist religion and his declaration that the novel was based on
The Glass Palace Chronicle was meant only to shield this abuse. In the end
it is suggested that Collis should be asked to leave out all the abusement
of Paṁsuku or to rewrite the chapters concerned; meanwhile the
government of Burma should take all the old issues of the book and
burn them.
It seems to me that the whole critique is a grave misunderstanding. The
first thing which its author does not seem to realize is the fact that Collis'

even if the author chose a historical person and described him differently from
the historical truth he would only follow a practice common in world litera-
ture. But with Pamsukū the case is quite different for Collis obviously
created an entirely fictitious character, having nothing in common with the
person, mentioned by the critic. In his letter to Dagmar Nováková dated
24 February 1958, Collis stated that Paṁsukū from his book was not a
Hinayana Buddhist at all but a member of a heretical sect of Mahayanists
called the Ari; though he was aware that the sect had died out in Burma
already in the 11th century, Collis "considered that the balance of the book
was improved by having an example of this type of person." And we can
only confirm that this Paṁsukū fits very well into the last days of the Pagan
court. Finally, considering the novel as a whole we observe that in the
conception of this book there can hardly be found any abuse of the Buddhist
Church which is on the contrary represented by the Royal Chaplain in the
most favourable way; Paṁsukū himself was most probably meant as exam-
ple of an ill-adviser who gained undesirable influence over the weak King in
the last few moments of his rule.
Leaving Collis' historical novel for his novels with a contemporary setting
we are struck by a rather paradoxical feature; while Collis' intimate knowledge
of the 20th century Burma enabled him to show so much of the spirit of the
country in his historical novel from the middle of the 13th century (and later
also in his romances), he has almost entirely failed to do so in his novels with
a contemporary setting, namely Sanda Mala. And it is also interesting to
observe that both Sanda Mala and The Dark Door, which undoubtedly
belong among his weaker works, are entirely fictional, being not inspired
by a written source, folk tale or real event as other Collis' books. The histor-
ical novel and the romances, as well as his non-fictional work, prove very
clearly that the author was more successful when he did not invent the
theme himself but borrowed it somewhere else.
In Sanda Mala where Collis presents a love affair of Mangan, a young
English painter and Nat Shin Me, daughter of a Burmese prince in Mergui,
the dominating figure is that of the old Shan princess, Sanda Mala, mother
of Nat Shin Mé. A resolute woman, absolute ruler of her household, some-
what eccentric, yet with a genuine sense of humour, she was inspired by the
Shan Princess Tip Htila, whom Collis admired, being at the same time amused
by her, during his tour through the Shan States in 1938. In the shade of Sanda
Mala's personality all the other characters are rather colourless; the old
prince is more a decoration of the scene than a living man and also Mangan
is rather passive.

proper, forming rather its background, we may say without great exaggera-
tion that the main significance of this novel lies in their presentation for Collis
succeeded in touching their characteristic features in a sharp critical light.
Similarly as other English communities all over Burma and India, the
Mergui English lived in a deliberate islolation from the native population,
trying to avoid any unnecessary contact with them. Their club, into which
only Europeans were allowed to enter, was “a citadel into which they could
withdraw" for when the men had finished their work, they wanted to get
away from the Burmese, as Collis wrote in one of his autobiographies. And
their club where they tried to create "little England", a terribly boring and
unbearable place, only supported their nostalgia and bitterness against the
"horrible country” in which they had to serve. And as soon as they came
into a conflict with the native population they would hold together and side
not with the law but always with their countrymen, involved into the conflict.
When in Sanda Mala one of the English, Mr.Poole, was charged by the
Prince for extracting tin from his territory, all the club people supported
Poole against the Prince, not bothering to find out whose claims were right.
And when Commissioner Dellahooke justly decided the case in the Prince's
favour, all the English became scandalized. Dellahooke was accused of being
"pro-Burman" and Poole expressed the English ruling class attitude in these
words: "I've been made a public fool of, I've been laughed at by the nat-
ives. Is this the way we rule Burma? What's the good of us being here at all?
That fellow Dellahooke is unfit to be a British official. If he doen't protect
us from being tricked by the rascally Burmans, what's the use of him?” And
Commissioner Kempenfelt, after scolding Dellahooke for unsuitable hand-
ling of the whole affair, told him quite openly: "...as an officer of your
experience knows, there are ways of getting things done in this country." And
when Dellahooke is rebuked of injuring the British prestige we cannot help
comparing his position with that of Collis during his famous Rangoon trials
in the thirties.
Let us now draw our attention to another question: What is the actual
picture of Burma which we get from reading this novel? It is a country of
beautiful landscape, admired by the painter's eye, an object of exploitation
for a businessman, a place where a British officer is not wanted and where
there are no prospects for an educated Burmese girl; a beautiful land under
the yoke of colonialism. But - do also Burmese people live in this country?
How do they live and feel, what do they think, expect and fight for? What is
their attitude to the English rulers? Neither of these questions can be
answered because Collis has entirely neglected to draw a picture of the
Burmese people in this novel and we feel that there is actually very little
of true Burma in it.

This drawback together with the fact that the main characters are not
typical, all tending to be exclusive or exceptional, and the weakness of the
plot, make the book a failure. Despite the critical light, thrown at the English
community, the author failed in drawing a picture of the whole society. And
it might be disputed to what extent this novel really is a work of art for
places, which do not seem to be too far from the so-called "strap-hanger"
standard abound.
The Dark Door, a humorous novel which enables us to have a look into
a Burmese village, seems to be in certain aspects a better work than Sanda
Mala.
At the beginning of World War II, Moulton, a Secret Service agent is sent
to Siam to find out the reason of an unusual activity at Kra, a place at the
southernmost tip of Burma on the Siamese side. He goes to Burma as a tin
agent and tried to cross the Siamese border in the disguise of a Buddhist
monk; his effort not to rouse the smallest suspicion is turned upside down,
when he is considered a travelling hermit making miracles. We are
acquainted with his further adventures which are finally crowned by his
revealment of the secret, which is, in the meantime already known to
the English Times.
There are several good figures through which the author managed to pour
humour into this novel. The shrewd and practical Mrs. Farwell, a nice type
of a Chinese middle-class woman with a fantastic ability of turning every
trifle into good business, Carew, with his inseparable Dante, a book in
which he is always ready to find a parallel to any event from his life.
the Quixotic figure of the orderly, the village headman, seeking the secret
of the Grand Elixir and others.
Comparing The Dark Door with Sanda Mala we observe that though
the humorous novel enables us to have a look into the life of the Burmese
village, it shows us rather the habits, way of thinking and superstitions
of the peasants than their work and troubles. Thus Moulton, in disguise
of a Buddhist monk, is indicated to have affected a miracle or, later, no one
is astonished when the distinguised monk "disappears", leaving only his
robe and novice behind.
One of the main points of interest in the author's project of this book
was the presentation of a part of Mahayana metaphysics; this was done in
Moulton's sermon to the villagers and in his disputation with the Siamese
Royal Chaplain; both these parts might have been too learned and perhaps
too dry for such a book as The Dark Door, unless the author enlivened
them with his satirical touches and vivid humorous interludes.

Among the comparatively vivid characters of this novel there is, however,
a very weak one, whose very existence is rather doubtful. It is that of Hester
Farwell, Moulton's helper who is so lifeless, that she is hardly a human being
at all. Being an Eurasian girl, she has a blind admiration not only for her
English father, whom she did not even remember, but for all the Englishmen
and a feeling that she is in fact an English girl. Similarly as Nat Shin Mé in
Sanda Mala, she felt lost and isolated in Burma and longed for England.
For Nat Shin Mé England was attractive as a country of educated people; for
Hester it was a land of a better race. It is true that, provided certain circum-
stances be fulfilled, such girls might have existed in Burma. But looking closely
at both novels, we are struck by the fact that these two girls are the only
representatives of the young generation of Burma (for there are no young
Burmese men in these novels.) Are they really the young of Burma, Nat Shin
Mé,placed before the very year of Saya San's rebellion when the whole country
was hurling by hatred for foreign oppressors and Hester at the beginning
of the Second World War when Burma was left entirely defenceless against
the Japanese aggression? Remembering how Collis was able to draw these
years in his Trials in Burma showing the Burmese opinion and attitude in
such a true light. We can hardly believe that Sanda Mala and The Dark
Door were written by the same author. They are dealing with the situation in
an inadequate way, bringing forth exceptional and untypical figures. Nat Shin
Mé and Hester, isolated from times and their surroundings are not only
unsuccessful characters from the artistic point of view, but persons, who
rather deteriorate the picture of Burma.
Although the plot of The Dark Door has neither particular significance
nor does it reveal more important problems, the novel, showing some typical
aspects of a Burmese village life with several characters, taken also from the
lower classes of the Burmese society, is more successful than that of Sanda
Mala.
About Collis' romances which belong to the later period of his work (The
Descent of the God was published in 1948 while The Mystery of Dead
Lovers in 1951) we may say that they are more mature than the earlier
fictitious works; Collis seems to have found himself as an artist in them,
having a broad field for spreading his imagination.
In The Descent of God the author did not use written historical sources
as in most of his other books but turned to oral tradition which has preserved
the story of the origin of a cult of the Arakanese island of Manaung and to
his own experience with the scented hill during his visit of the island. And we
can only admire the author's ability to give so much of local colour to his
romance.

In the limited scope of this paper I was not able to do full justice to the
personality of Maurice Collis, sketching only certain features of his three
novels on Burma. I would like to conclude this immature attempt by drawing
your attention to certain problems which would show the significance of
Collis' Burmese novels as a part of his whole work.
Although Collis' desire to write was of an early date, he started his actua
literary career comparatively late; when his Siamese White was published,
he was already 47 years old, retiring after his Indian Civil Service career in
Burma. This was mainly due to the fact that he was somehow unable to
find a subject for writing, that he did not know how to make his first decisive
step. Life, pulsing everywhere around, did not seem to be a source of in-
spiration. And this feature remained with him during all his creative period.
Once a student of history, he had always a strong liking for the past which
seemed to be more stimulating than the present and it was only in the case
of Trials in Burma that he felt the necessity of recording events directly
from life. Although the characters of his novels with a contemporary setting
-at least some of them—had example in life, their actual plot and setting
were quite far from it. In most of his books he dealt with historical subjects
founded on some written source where the theme was already worked up;
Collis thus transferred the material in the same plane-that is from one
written form to another—similary as a painter who paints pictures not accor-
ding to real objects but according to other pictures.
The main merit of Collis' non-fictional work lies in his choice of subject,
selection of material from his sources and his own approach to the theme
in the form of comments, remarks and reflection through which he would
reveal facts and problems from quite a new angle, offering independent,
stimulating and sometimes rather daring solutions. Thus he often is more a
modern editor than creator of a new thing.
As a writer of fiction, Collis is rather unbalanced. His first novel, She
Was a Queen, quite a promising work, was soon followed by Sanda Mala
and The Dark Door, the standard of which was, both in contents and
form, much inferior. The dramas, two of which were dramatization of his
earlier historical works, did not bring an expressive tone of their own; the
lyrical atmosphere of Lord of the Three Worlds, however, showed a new
tone, which was later fully developed in his romances.

As I have already mentioned, Collis, as an author intimately acquainted
with the 20th century Burma, succeeded in showing very much of the country's
spirit in his works with historical themes and in his romances; his novels with
a contemporary setting, however, lack just this quality. As U Minn Latt
has observed, Collis “is aware that the surrounding determine the fate of the
individual, but his surroundings go very little out of the court and a handful
of foreigners" and he "troubles himself less to find out the conditions of life
of the ordinary peoples." This drawback is more or less characteristic for ali
Collis' works, both fictional and non-fictional, but most strikingly appears in
Sanda Mala where, apart from the Burmese aristocratic family, he deals
with a mere handful of foreigners; in works with historical themes he only
rarely leaves the court. Though not paying enough attention to the life condi-
tions of ordinary people, he is, especially in the historical novel and the
romances, very interested in their habits, superstitions and beliefs in
supernatural powers; in the romances he thus creates a mood which, stressed
by the poetic language, often leads the whole theme to the threshold of
supernatural world, leaving the life of human beings for that of divine and
half-divine dream-like creatures and apparitions.
Collis' characters are rather his weaker side. In the great majority they
are flat characters with one prevailing feature and some of his tragic persons,
like Rahangri in The Mystery of Dead Lovers tend to be too monotonous.
The women characters of his fictional works are more life-like than his men;
Collis usually centres his attention on a woman- —a princess or a queen-and
her career, leading to her rise to world power (like Queen Saw in She Was
a Queen or Tzu Hsi in The Motherly and Auspicious); in his romances -
the heroines are connected with a supernatural power, raising above the level
of other people into the world of divine beings. In all his works there is a
strong tendency to pay too much attention to the individual, his fate, motives
of behaviour, etc., while the picture of the whole society is rather neglected.
The language of all Collis' works is simple; in his contemporary novels it
is restricted almost only to colourless dialogues while the half poetic tinge of
the romances highly contributes to their dreamy atmosphere.
Concluding this brief paper we may say: the great majority of Collis'
books are historical non-fictional works by which the author brings to light
events or persons less known or presents a fairly known thing in a new light.
These works abound in quantity and also their quality seems to exceed that of
fiction. The standard of the novels with a contemporary setting is rather low
while the historical novel and romances raise to a higher level. Collis is an
author who comprehended and felt for the Orientals, especially the Burmese,
whom he knew so closely, to a much higher degree than the great majority of
Europeans who were employed in the East as administrators of a colonial
government.
