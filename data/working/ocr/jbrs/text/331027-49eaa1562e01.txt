There are several works of Abhidhan, path, katha,
tikā, nissaya, and lanka on Burmese porana (archaic
words). In the absence of a modern scientific Burmese
Dictionary, they have been indispensable companions to
Burmese scholars and students of Burmese language and
literature in ascertaining the meaning of many words that
have become obsolete. These works not only supply
expositions on difficult linguistic points but also serve as
"Collections of Quotations". Their compilers, who were
well steeped in Burmese literature, culled appropriate
passgaes from the Burmese classics of the 15th to the 18th
C., and interpreted the alleged porana as they stood in
particular contexts,--a method frowned upon by philology-
minded scholars of this age, who decline and conjugate
inflexional words and reduce them to their roots, who
dissect and analyse agglutinative expressions into their
elements, and who trace monosyllabic words to their
oldest forms in the language concerned and also in cognate
languages so as to arrive at their original meanings.
Nevertheless, these treatises, both in Pali and Burmese
prose or verse, represent the labour and the scholarship of
learned Burmans, who have laid a foundation on which
later scholars may build a better structure.
Of these works, the most well known are, (1) A poem
on Burmese poraṇa, alleged to be written by Shin Silavamsa,
a scholarly monk and a great poet of the 15th C.; (2) A
Porana katha, by Twinthin-taik-wun, a court poet and one
of the most prolific writers of the 18th C., who further
added to the earlier list vocabularies from the works of
the 16th, 17th and 18th Cs.; (3) A new poem on Burmese
porana an enlarged edition of the Porana katha in
verse, by some anonymous writers (19 C.); (4) Another
Porana katha, a refurbished edition of Twinthin-taik-

The poem alleged to be written by Shin Silavaṁsa
consists of five stanzas. Poranas are usually either preceded
or followed, as required by metrical exigencies, by their
Burmese equivalents. We have some reason to believe
as will be shown that the poem is the earliest work on Bur-
mese poraṇa. It was the precursor of Twinthin-taik-wun's
Porana katha, which mentions the existence of some such
earlier work,' It was a source of inspiration to later
collectors of archaic words. It is still referred to as an
authority up to the present day.
The belief that the poem was composed by Shin
Silavansa has been accepted without scrutiny. But, did
the famous and learned monk actually compose it? After
all, it might well be another case of ascribing the name
of a famous literary figure to a famous piece of work, a
practice not uncommon in any country with a long literary
tradition. *
To be able to give a fairly satisfactory answer, there
are three pre-requisites that should be fulfilled: (1) to define
what a writer must mean when he calls a word a porana,
that is to settle the criteria by which a word should be
recognised as porana; (2) to examine any evidence as to the
authorship available from later references especially, in
works of the 18th C.; and finally (3) to make a textual study
of the expressions and words contained in the
poem itself.
(1) A porana (Skt. paurana, P. porana) means an
obsolete or obsolescent old word. But, to the compilers
of the treatises on porana, as apparent from the lists
they have drawn up, a perana appears to be a word that
occurs infrequently in Burmese poetical works of the 15th-
18th C., and which need not necessarily be obsolete, for
many of the words pronounced to be porana still form
