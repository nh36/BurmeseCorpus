Hence the administration, under certain Kings, was actually carried on the lines of
some popular forms of government, ruled the country with the help of ministers, whose
number, rank, and functions were strictly defined by convention observed through the ages.
The institution of ministers was meant to exercise a wholesome check on tyranny. But as
the King's respect for it was only voluntary, its importance varies with (a) the character of
the King (b) the personality of each minister and (c) the needs of the time. Here in this
paper an attempt is made to briefly survey the King's Councillors during the reign of
King Mindon.

The civil, military and judicial branches of administration were carried out by the
five courts which usually went by the name of Hlutyon Ngayat. These five courts or Hlutyon
Ngayat were the
Byedike, Sheyon, Tarayon and Anaukyon. Of
these the Hluttaw and Byedike were the most important in directing and controlling the
entire administration of the country. The Hluttaw was the supreme council and exercised all
the powers of a senate, a high court, and a cabinet. Its functions were legislative, executive
and judicial. As the senate, it possessed the constitutional power of veto to any act or order
of the King. As the high court of civil and criminal justice, it tried all important cases, and
was the highest court of appeal. As the cabinet it exercised all the powers of government.
Every Royal Order was registered by and issued through it. The officers of the Hluttaw were
usually four Wungyis, one Myinsugyi Wun, one Athi Wun, four Wundauks and

It can not be denied that the tenure of office of these great men, like all others, depended
upon the pleasure of the King and that his wish was the law. It seems that strong and
powerful kings, however, took great care in selecting their councillors so as to have a
group of men with integrity and resourcefulness. In such cases the absolute power enjoyed
by the king was well balanced by the firmness of his councillors and sometimes he had to
yield as he could not have the whole lot of them sacked.3 But as the King's Councillors
were his nominees their personalities and strength of character enhanced his power and helped
him to acquire the popular acclamation of being ‘a just king, good and exalted".
The names, Wungyi, Wundauk and Atwinwun are descriptive of the responsibilities of
the King's Councillors. The literal translation of Wungyi would be the bearer of the great
burden and that of Wundauk, the prop or the assistant burden bearer, while Atwinwun
would be the bearer of the interior burden. Thus the names themselves indicate
the significance and the greatness of their responsibilities. The Wungyi was a man of
learning and was supposed to be conversant with all spiritual, temporal and court affairs.
In the appointment order, his office was defined: to be in charge of all the affairs of state
throughout the kingdom; to be responsible for the prosperity of the Religion, well-being

Wundauk was to give general assistance to the Wungyi in discharging the wide range of

technically in direct charge of the affairs of the palace. Their duty was to keep the king
always informed of the affairs of state. Thus all the Atwinwuns had to be in constant
attendance upon the King together with the other officers by day, and at night they had
to be in attendance in the palace in pairs. They were the link between the king and the
Hluttaw and hence all the royal edicts and petitions, applications and letters from the
Hluttaw to the king passed through them. They had to sort out which were to be sent up
to the king, or the Hluttaw or just to file them up in their office, i.e. the Byedike.
Sometimes they withheld some of the cases sent from the Hluttaw and instead of placing
them before the King, they were sent back to the court of origin for re-consideration,
etc.2
Like the Wungyis and the Wundauks, the Atwinwuns were also supposed to
be proficient in the basic three arts. Sir George Scott stated that the relative
rank of the Atwinwuns with the members of the Hluttaw was not clearly defined.3
But after a close study of the list of officers and the salaries they enjoyed respec-
tively, we find that the Wungyis were definitely higher in rank than the Atwinwuns.4 Most
of the Atwinwuns, after having an experience in administration for some years and also after
having the good name of being agreeable both with the king and his ministers, were promoted
to the office of Wungyi. Moreover, the insignias of the Atwinwuns were a bit inferior to
those of the Wungyis. Both the Atwinwuns and the Wungyis were almost equally influential
because the former, though of lesser rank had closer contact with the King. Sometimes, an
Atwinwun who enjoyed the royal favour would be greatly respected even by the Wungyis.
It is said that it had been a moot point whether the Atwinwuns were above or below the
Wundauks.5 They were definitely between the Wungyis and the Wundauks for later when
they were given half-yearly emoluments they were paid less than the former and a bit more
than the latter.

These were King Mindon's Councillors and according to Talboys Wheeler³ they were
subservient, smooth-tongued officials, whose ideas and aspirations all centred in the King.
They were not hereditary chiefs or nobles but were, to borrow Yule's words, 'notables of the
court' who got some township or district to ‘eat'. Formerly they derived their daily sustenance
from such lands. King Mindon, however, abolished this system and the payment of salaries
was substituted in s. 1223 (A.D. 1861).4 According to this scheme, the salary for a Wungyi
was fixed at 1000 kyats per mensum; that of a Wundauk at 500 Kyats while
Atwinwun received 660 kyats a month.5 The Myinsugyiwun and the Athiwun
were paid only 500 kyats a month6 though they were senior to the Wundauks
in order of precedence. As stated above these principal ministers were the senior gazetted
officers of the realm; but as we have seen their salaries were not of the same scale. The introduc-
tion of this scheme added very considerably to the royal revenues; but it tended to lessen the
power of the official community by depriving them of all provincial influence. Hence the
measure was found to be somewhat unfavourable. Most of the ministers preferred the old system
to the new7 as their income by the former was definitely richer than the fixed monetary allow-
ances. The King, however, had his way though he rendered himself personally very unpopular.9

Though these councillors lived only a short century ago, we hardly know anything
about them. The Burmese sources are very rare, being destroyed or lost. So most of the
information is gleaned from the accounts left by foreigners who visited the Court at
Mandalay. Now let us study these great ministers individually.
The Mainglon Myosa Wungyi-His name was Nga Tso. He was an Atwinwun under
King Tharrawaddy. When Prince Mindon revolted against his brother Pagan Min (1846–52)
in A.D. 1852 he joined the revolution from Thalonmyo with some followers.1 Hence King
Mindon, out of gratitude appointed him Wungyi and conferred on him the title Mingyi Maha
Yazathinkyan.2 He was sometimes addressed as Thalon Myosa Wungyi. Later he was given
Mainglon on the 2 waxing of Kason s.1216 (A.D.1854)3 and during the following month
(Nayon) he was promoted to the rank of Thado and thus became Mainglon Myosa Wungyi
Thado Mingyi Maha Yazathinkyan.4. He did not serve long under Mindon. During the
month of Tapotwe in s.1221 (A.D.1859) he died and was cremated with great pomp and
honour.5
The Myadaung Myosa Wungyi-His name was Maung Mho. Under Tharrawaddy
and Pagan he served as an Atwinwun and as he looked after the King's cannon and muskets

Mindon came to the throne after the Second Anglo-Burmese War when the Kingdom
had been much reduced and hence poorer in resources than that of his predecessors. But
the governmental machinery had grown and the foreign relations became more complicated.
Diplomatic missions were exchanged, peaceful relations were maintained, and commercial
treaties concluded. All these made the burdens greater and heavier for the bearers of the
great burden. They however, were found to be quite competent to carry out those duties.

Evidently most of the Councillors of King Mindon were not mere sycophants though
they existed on the King's favour. The Pakhan Wungyi and the Yaw Atwin Wun are the
most glaring examples. If they bad been mere puppets as E. B. Sladen stated would they
have dared to risk the King's displeasure in the carrying out of their duties. As we have
seen the Pakhan Wungyi went to the extent of openly ingnoring the King's direct appoint-
But unfortunately, these learned and capable councillors failed to influence the
King in making the much needed innovations in the country. Prince Kanaung, the Crown
Prince had started to modernise it on western lines by sending out young men to train in the
manufacture of arms and ammunitions and other mechanical and engineering works. A
shipbuilding yard, an arsenal, a gun foundry, welding and weaving factories, saw mills,
cotton mills, sugar mills, etc. were founded but were mostly abandoned or neglected after the
death of the Crown Prince in 1866.1 As the King showed no interest in these works the
councillors probably did not want to push the matter further though they knew well that they
were necessary. This was because these great men were cowed down by tradition and were
every inch sons of their age. Those who had been abroad were imbued with western ideas
and manners to such an extent that they favoured a constitutional monarchy. They even
entertained the foreign guests in the western style with plates, knives, forks, napkins, glasses,
etc. and served beer and sherry at table.2 Yet, these men were for ever conscious that they
were the humble subjects - the servants of His Majesty - to fulfill his wishes, to follow his
command, execute his orders and to give counsel only when it was sought. Therefore, they
never suggested anything on their own initiative. Moreover it was definitely laid down in the
Conduct Rules3 for the Wungyi's that they were to serve in the interest of the King.
In all the matters that (the ministers) had to manage, they had to bear in mind
(!) what was the King's expressed desire and, (2) how it could be made most
beneficial to his interest and they should invariably work accordingly.3
Mindon, like his predecessors, tried to keep his ministers down with a strong hand.
But as
we have seen, his ministers were no mere instruments for executing his orders, and it was
the King who had to yield most of the time. This, does not imply that Mindon was a weak
King or that his Councillors were too powerful. This is amply attested by the fact that
Mindon established himself firmly on the throne for twentysix years and ruled the country
peacefully and well. This was so only because he had the loyalty and cooperation of these
learned and capable councillors.
