The Society's Exhibition
The Exhibition which the Society held in the rains of 1935 having
aroused some degree of interest, it was decided to repeat the display on an
enlarged scale during the current year, and, by kind permission of the
Librarian and the Committee of the University Library, the Exhibition was
held on the 27th August in the Library building.
H. E. Sir Archibald Cochrane, the Patron of the Society, honoured the
occasion by his presence; and the total number of guests and members
present was some two hundred. After tea had been served in the main
reading-room of the building, the President of the Society briefly thanked His
Excellency for his presence, and called on Mr Luce to explain in outline the
general nature of the exhibits. The guests then adjourned to the upper rooms
of the building where the actual exhibition was on view.
The exhibits included illustrations of the prehistoric archæology of
Burma, arranged by Mr T. O. Morris; a collection of Burmese illuminated
manuscripts lent by the Trustees of the Bernard Free Library; missionary
manuscripts lent by Judson College; Chinese books and maps arranged by
Mr Luce; a collection of notable Burma books arranged by U Khin Zaw; and
books, maps, and pictures illustrative of the history of Rangoon. In addition
to literary exhibits, there were a number of items from the Prior Collection,
including some very old votive plaques, an ancient Burmese silver bowl, and
an exceptionally fine series of Burmese weights; and Mr Justice Leach lent
for exhibit a Karen drum, and two old cannon (survivals of the First
Burmese War).
The interest of the Exhibition was immensely added to by the presence
of representatives of the hill-tribes of Burma in their national costumes, this
part of the Exhibition having been arranged by Major Green with the
cooperation of Colonel Rich and the help of men of the Burma Rifles and of
students of the University. Major Green also provided an exhibit of handi-
crafts and of photographs of the lesser-known tribes. Mr Luce, in the course
of his introductory address, observed that "These tribes occupy over half
of Burma, and number 40 per cent of its population. . . All the later
migrations, the Karen, the Shan and the Tibeto-Burman, including both the
Western and Eastern waves, are shown, presenting all stages of culture, from
Shans' who are one of the oldest of races, to Nungs just emerging from the
Bamboo Age. Karens are represented by Sgaw, Pwo, Brec of Toungoo, and
Hill Sgaw of Papun: Shans by the Great Tai of Namkham. To illustrate the
Western Tibeto-Burmans, there are Kamhows from Tiddim in the N. Chin
Hills, Falam Chins and Lushais from the Centre, Hakas from the South; also
Chinboks from Pakokku Hill Tracts. To illustrate the Eastern Tibeto-
Burmans, there are Marus of the Nmai Valley, whose language is so close to
Burmese though they have been parted for over a thousand years: Nungs,
from the loop of the Taron: Chinghpaws, or Kachins as Burmans call them
from Bhamo: Hkahkus from the Triangle, and Black Lisus, men of the
crossbow, from the Salween N'mai Hka Divide. It is probably the first time

Plates I-III. The history of the weights is not known; but the silver
bowl is said to have belonged to the Commander-in-Chief of the Burmese
Army before the War of 1886. On his death the bowl was inherited by his
widow, Sanci Ma Khin, who treasured it very much. Mr Rodney Swinhoe
tried to buy it from her for exhibition in the Burmese Pavilion at the
Wembley Exhibition, but she would not part with it although he offered Rs.
500. Madame Jules Martin persuaded Sanci Ma Khin, shortly before she died
at a great age, to sell the bowl to her husband, M. Jules Martin, who subse-
quently sold it to its present owner, Mr Prior. Dr Martin Hurlimann had tried
to buy the bowl from M. Martin for the Zurich Museum, but the negotiations
fell through.
The larger figures on the bowl represent the Burmese months and the
smaller figures the days of the week. It is said that the completion of the
bowl occupied one full year, the figure for each month being embossed on the
day of the full moon of that month. The bowl is considered to be of
considerable antiquity, though its history before the middle nineteenth
century is not known.
