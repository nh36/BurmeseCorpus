The Rebellion of Prince Tharrawaddy and the
Deposition of Bagyidaw King of Burma, 1837.*
Bagyidaw ruled over Burma for 18 years (1819-1837). In 1837 his
younger brother the Prince of Tharrawaddy rebelled, and within a few weeks
forced Bagyidaw to surrender the throne and the government to him. This
article deals with the circumstances, causes and course of the Rebellion and
particularly with the part played in it by the British Resident Lt.-Col. Burney
then in residence at the Royal City of Ava.
Burney was in residence at Ava as a representative of the Governor-
General in India and by virtue of the Treaty of Yandabo, from April 1830 to
June 1837 (with some interruptions from ill health). He gradually came to
acquire considerable personal influence over the King's Ministers, and
although the British Residency was hated both by the Government and the
people, being considered to be a badge of national humiliation, Burney
himself was held in high respect so that he was able to function as Resident
with enviable success. During the course of this Revolution of 1837 the part
played by him in the cause of humanity won for him universal praise.
Bagyidaw was popular with his subjects in Ava because he often held
boat races, shows, elephant fights and other fetes. He was also good natured
and not willing to shed blood. But he possessed neither ability nor strength
of character. "The King is a poor weak fool, incapable of comprehending
any rational argument," says Burney. (Bengal Secret & Political. No. 358
Cons. 20th August 1830, Burney's Journal.) His Chief Queen had been raised
from low life; she was proud, bigotted and avaricious. She exercised an
altogether considerable influence over the mind of the King. Her brother, at
one time a fish monger, was created Menthagyee or Great Prince and
President of the Hlut-dau. He possessed considerable intelligence, was
dignified in his manners, but was devoid of diplomatic skill and was grasping
beyond bounds. Bagyidaw's only son was Tsa-Kya-Men by a deceased
Queen of Royal blood. At the time of the revolution he was 25 years of age.
Throughout his father's reign he was kept completely in the background by
his step-mother and her brother Menthagyee. He was not even recognised as
Crown Prince or Ainshe Men i.e. Lord of the Eastern House, a position
second only to the King.
In 1831 Bagyidaw became subject to fits of melancholia and for the
next six years ceased to take part in public affairs except occasionally. In
September 1831 he appointed a Commission of Four to carry on the govern-
ment in his name. It consisted of his brother-in-law the Menthagyee and the
three Royal brothers the Princes of Tharrawaddy, Thibau and Ko Woon
Men. (Ibid. No. 363 Burney's Letter to his Govt. dated Ava 15-10-1831).
Under the Queen's influence power fell entirely into the hands of Menthagyee.

He became the factotum of the Empire. He and the Queen began to fill all
high appointments at the capital and in the provinces with their friends and
adherents. It was commonly reported that they had collected an enormous
fortune by squeezing the people and were the two richest people in the
country. The Princes of the blood Royal were all thrown into the
background and there was considerable apprehension in the Royal family
that Menthagyee would dethrone the King, set aside Tsakya-men and make
himself the master of Buima. Such a revolution would endanger the lives of
all the Princes and Princesses of the Blood Royal. (India Political Cons. Range
194 Vol. 35, Letter from Burney to Govt. dated Ava 3-3-1837.)
All the Princes were highly dissatisfied with the state of affairs but none
of them had the courage to express their feelings except the Prince of
Tharrawaddy who was one of the greatest Jagirdars in the kingdom. Even
he, out of regard for his brother the King controlled his feelings and avoided.
embarrassing Menthagyee's Government; only he gradually withdrew himself
from the Commission of Four and seldom even visited the Palace. Being a
man of foresight, however, he began to collect arms and increased the num-
ber of his followers and retainers, not with any hostile intentions to the King
or his throne, but to be prepared in the event of the King's death to prevent
Menthagyee from usurping the throne or injuring the King's son and brothers.
Menthagyee was keeping a good watch upon the Prince and looking for an
opportunity to disarm him. A pretext was soon found. (Ibid. para 3).
The Prince's own sister the Princess of Pagan appointed as her Woon,
or Minister, Nga Yè a well known bold and resolute character and who was
charged with having been the leader of bandits. She had also in her posses-
sion since the close of the First War with the British (1824-1826) a number
of small guns and jingals. Menthagyee's Government charged the Princess
of having collected firearms in her house and appointed a dangerous character
as her Minister. An order was issued in the name of the King to seize the
arms and also Nga Yè. Accordingly on the night of the February 21, 1837,
Government troops suddenly surrounded the Princess's house. She and her
Woon escaped but the firearms were taken. The Princess fled to the house
of her brother the Prince of Tharrawaddy and he advised her to return to
her own house which she did. The Woon, however, could not be found, and
the Government, suspecting the Prince of Tharrawaddy of harbouring him,
sent a party of troops with the request that Nga Yè be surrendered. The
Prince denied all knowledge of the Woon's whereabouts; but Menthagyee and
the Ministers summoned some of the principal officers of the Prince and put
them in irons. The Prince again declared that he was totally unaware of the
fugitive's hiding place, and having reason to belive that the King was equally
unaware of what was going on, and fearing that Menthagyee was plotting to
usurp the throne, he gathered all his followers arround him and prepared for
resistance. (Ibid. paras 4 & 5).
On the afternoon of February 22, the Prince sent a messenger to
Burney to inquire what part he would be disposed to play in the event of a
quarrel between himself and Menthagyee. Burney clearly gave the
messenger to understand, that he had orders from his Government in India,

Burney attempted to bring about a reconciliation between the Govern-
ment and the Prince, but Menthagyee and the Ministers rejected his offer.
Next, he expressed a desire to see the King, but the Ministers refused to
grant his request, and said, that the King was fully cognizant of all that was
passing, and that they were merely endeavouring to carry out his orders.
Burney plainly told them that he believed that they were acting without the
King's knowledge. He also warned them that their measures had exceed-
ingly irritated the Prince and some serious consequences would ensue.
Finally, he recommended that one of the Woongyees should go to the Prince
and satisfy him that the Government measures were the orders of the King
himself, and that the whole question would be settled as soon as Nga Yè was
surrendered. The Ministers, however, under the influence of Menthagyee
decided against this wholesome advice. "They all approved my suggestion
says Burney, "but pretended that it could not be adopted because there was
no precedent for a Woongyee going to a Prince's house". (Ibid. para 5).

The same day Burney advised the Prince to surrender Nga Yè if he
knew where he was, or to try and apprehend him. The Prince "turned to a
Pagoda which was visible from his chamber and made oath that he did not
know where Nga Yè was, and observed that he believed the apprehending of
that man was a mere pretext, and that Menthagyeh and his party would not
be satisfied even if he, the Prince, could seize and surrender that man; but
that if they would only give him time, he would send out his followers to
search and apprehend him". (Ibid. para 7).
On the morning of the February 24, the Ministers summoned the
Prince's first minister and confined him with seven pairs of irons; next they
dispatched a military force to search the Prince's house. What their action
led to might be described in Burney's words :-
"The guard at his gateway however told the troops that they could not
enter without the Prince's permission. The troops tried to break open the
gate when the Prince's party from within fired four or five muskets, killed
one man and wounded two. The Government troops with the Myolatwun,
the General officer Commanding them, fled back to the Lhwottau and repor-
ted what had passed. The Menchagyeh and the whole Court were thrown
into the greatest dismay and confusion, and preparations were made for
receiving an attack upon the Palace, which was immediately expected from
the Prince." (Ibid. para 8).
The Ministers had not expected the Prince to resist a search of his
house; they were wholly unprepared for the situation created, and now
began to think only of their personal safety. But the Prince at the same
time fearing that a second and a more vigorous attack would be made upon
his house, immediately drew out all his family and followers, embarked them
in boats and crossed over the Irrawaddy to Sagaing. The Government had
on the spot 3,000 to 4,000 troops while the Prince had not more than 500
armed men, but no attempt was made to intercept him while he was crossing

Twelve hours after the Prince had crossed the river the Government
recovered from the paralysis by which it had been overtaken, and not only
began to make defensive preparations but sent a force of 2,000 men to
Sagaing and another to plunder the houses of the Prince and his followers.
The Ministers also had several of the officers of the Prince and of his sister

remonstrated with them against this line of conduct, and told them "that
they were exasperating the Prince and provoking him to return and attack
them, and that their proceedings when reported by me to other countries,
would be most disgraceful to them" (Ibid. para 9). On this occasion at
least Burney's warning proved effective in a measure, and the executions
ceased.
From Sagaing the Prince of Tharrawaddy proceeded northwards towards
Moksobomyo "seizing the muskets of some soldiers whom he met with on
the road, but giving them a regular receipt for the same and keeping his
men in excellent order, preventing them from committing any depredations
on the villages through which they passed and in most of which he put up
placards, stating that he has no design against the King or the King's son,
but that the injustice and oppression of others had forced him to retire from
Ava." (Ibid. para 10).
The force of 2,000 men sent by Menthagyee to apprehend the Prince
was augmented to 4,000, but the Royal troops tamely followed him being
either unwilling or afraid to overtake him. "The pursuing force", wrote
Burney to his Government "was commanded
was commanded by the Ken Woon, an ignorant
old man, who instead of trying at once to overtake and apprehend the Prince
or persuade him to stop by offering to negociate a reconciliation with the
Government, desired only to frighten the Prince's party still more and
accelerate its flight. He believed that the Prince was running out of the
country towards Munipore as fast as he could, and taking good care to keep
at a respectful distance in the rear, halting every night on the ground which
the Prince's party had left in the morning, the Ken Woon fired his guns and
jingals incessantly in the hope that the report of these arms would be sure to
drive the Prince out of the Burmese territony. The Prince reached
Mouttshobo on the night of the 28th February. On the following day the
Ken Woon and his force of 3 or 4000 men halted at Halen a large village 12
or 14 miles to the South ward of Mouttshobo, and hearing that the Prince
was going to make a stand in that city this officer determined upon waiting
at Halen for reinforcements from the capital." (Ibid. Vol. 41 Letter from
Burney to Govt. dated Ava 24-5-1837).

While the Prince was strengthening himself, Menthagyee was hoping

The King was given to understand that his brother had meditated an attack
on the Palace in order to seize the throne, but that the Government had

Burney warned the Ministers of the fast developing danger and suggested
that the dispute could be settled by marrying the King's son to the Queen's
daughter and by conferring upon the former the title of Ain-ye-men. But
this would have deprived Menthagyee of all patronage and power, so that,
says Burney, they were "too blind and too full of pride or too much under
the influence of Menthagyeh to hear my suggestions, and they trust to a hope
which everyone else seems to consider fallacious, that the Royal troops will
overpower the Prince of Tharawadi." (Ibid. para 13).

On the March 4 Burney had a private audience with the King, the
Ministers also being present. The King's mind, Burney reports, "appeared
to be in a far better state then what I had expected to find it. He told me
that he was now well again and requested me to keep foreign merchants
quiet. I referred to the present state of affairs, saying 'If there is heat
or trouble in front of the house there will be no comfort or happiness in the
rear'." (Ibid. para 16). Burney had a desire to explain the true situation to
Bagyidaw and to suggest a policy of reconciliation, but he found him so
well impressed with Menthagyee's view of the dispute that he felt it unwise
to press the subject. (Ibid).
It was most judicious and politic on the part of the Prince of Tharra-
waddy to take his post at Moksobomyo which was the old seat and capital of
the great Alaungpaya the founder of the reigning dynasty. People from the
surrounding country began to flock to his standard from all directions, but
above all he was joined by many leaders of the banditti together with their
numerous followers, and by many who disliked Menthagyee's Government
and desired a change, or to whom a prospect of gain and plunder was
inviting. Burney reported to his Government that "parties of these men
went out and forced the inhabitants of the surrounding villages to join them
setting fire to their villages where the inhabitants refused. The Prince's
Emissaries....went about spreading a report that the King was dead, that
Menthagyeh had usurped the throne, and that the Prince of Tharawadi had
retired to Mouttshobo to make a stand there where he had been joined by an
immense concourse of people." (Ibid. Burney's Letter dated Ava 24-5-1837
para 7; Vol 41). "All the country" says Burney, "to the north of Ava and

Soon the Prince was able to assume so bold and confident an attitude,
that he opened the gates of Moksobomyo and challenged the royal troops to
come and attack him. The old Ken Woon waited at Halen for reinforce-
ments but himself had no stomach for fight. On the March 8, a small
party of the Prince's men so frightened him and his whole force that he
hastily evacuated the place leaving behind his guns and jingals. His retreat
was stopped by the Bho Mhu Woon, the King's brother, who was advancing
with reinforcements from Ava. The Bho Mhu combined the two forces but
failed to recover Halen. He encamped at Tharain and Letweyue two
villages six miles south of Halen, threw up some defences and applied to
Ava for further reinforcements. The Prince did not remain idle. He sent
out small bodies of troops which attaked Debayen, Phalangoun, Yeoo and
other places which were garrisoned with royal troops and contained an
abundance of guns and ammunition. "The King's troops fled" says Burney,
"at the first approach of the Prince's party which took the Governor of
Debayen, a near relation of the Queen, prisoner, and laid waste a great
portion of a very fine district. It was also generally reported and believed
that at Debayen the Prince's party had discovered and taken an immense
quantity of gold and silver and jewels which had been concealed there by the
Queen and Menthagyeh, and that the Prince was distributing this treasure
very liberally among his followers." (Ibid para 3). This report was wholly
false but it was of the greatest service to the Prince: the number of his
followers continued to swell. (Ibid).
The situation continued to worsen. The Ministers began to summon
troops from the southern districts up to Myede, "but although they collected
and dispatched 17,000 men against the Prince, not a man seemed disposed
to fight for them. Many of the troops after making their appearance at Ava
either returned to their villages or were induced to desert over in large
bodies to the Prince". (Ibid. para 4). A force of nearly 4,000 men, under
the command of the Prince of Thibau, the King's brother, proceeded along
the east bank of the Irrawaddy, but was obliged to stockade itself near
Tsinguh and was unable either to advance or retreat. Another force of
2,000 men under the Let-Ya-when-mhen went along the west bank of the
river with the object of recovering the neighbouring posts which had fallen
into the hands of the Prince, but it failed to achieve anything. Meanwhile
the troops of the Bo Mhu Wun, south of Halen, although raised to 10,000
men, were unable to make any impression whatever against Moksobomyo.
On the other hand large numbers of soldiers began to desert to the Prince
and soon the royal troops were reduced by one-half. (Ibid).
Events began to move fast, and at last on March 21, Burney received a
confidential communication from Menthagyee stating "that all was lost, that
none of the Royal troops which had been sent against the Prince of Thara-
wadi would fight him, and that the English Resident was the only person to

Burney saw the King and his Ministers twice on the following day and
offered to go to Moksobomyo and bring about a reconciliation between the

While Burney was preparing to go to Moksobomyo news was received,
on March 23, of the capture of the Royal Choky of Kyouk-ta-loun by the
Prince's troops, and there was a rumour that the next blow would fall upon
the British Residency. Burney felt uneasy about leaving the Residency which
contained not only his own family but also the families of many Indian and
foreign traders; also three American missionaries with their families had
taken refuge in the same area. Besides, there was no one but his Indian
Subadar Shaikh Abdul Latif to place in charge of the Residency. "But I
thought it my duty" says Burney, "to see if any exertion of mine could put a
stop to the civil war that was raging, and I had great confidence in the
Subadar which he fully realized”. (Ibid).
The Residency was placed in a state of defence, and at noon on March
23, Burney left Ava and crossed over to Sagaing. He was accompanied by
an escort of Burmese Musketeers provided by the King, two Indian soldiers,
a few Indian lascars and some personal servants. He found the whole route
before him infested with robbers and plunderers. The Burmese Escort showed
great unwillingness to proceed, but Burney pushed on with the help of an
intelligent guide and depending upon the faithfulness of his own men. When
he was not far from Letweyue the Royal Commander sent 50 horses and
100 musketeers to meet Burney. The King's troops were in an extraordi-
nary state of panic: "the moment they saw my party" says Burney, "they
turned round and fled, and it was with the utmost difficulty I could persuade
them to approach me". (Ibid. para 9).
From Letweyue Burney proceeded towards Tharain and reached the
camp of Prince Bo Mhu Woon at 10 p. m. on March 24. Immediately before
his arrival the rebel troops had begun a general attack upon the Royal forces.
The Bo Mhu Woon informed the assailants that a mission under the British
Resident had arrived from the King, but they considered the intelligence to
be a mere ruse, and fired and yelled with redoubled vigour, and continued the
attack until 1 a. m. "Both parties, however," writes Burney, "seemed to me
to be firing at an angle of 45 degrees and to be hurting each other very little.
The state of the King's troops will scarcely be credited. With the exception
of the Bo Mhu Woon and the Commander of the Letweyue position, every
person whem I saw or spoke to, was in a most pitiable state of fear and
anxiety ready to run at the slightest alarm. All the officers were quiet
peaceable men with families in Ava where they had always been employed in
Civil duties and were not only entirely ignorant of military service but most
cordially averse to it, whilst the private soldiers were poor villagers
summoned at a moment's notice from their homes and sent on a most
unpopular service without pay or provisions". (Ibid).

On the morning of March 25, Burney opened a communication with the
advanced post of the Prince about a quarter of a mile distant from the
Royalist camp. He received a civil message and proceeded to that post.
There he was furnished with a suitable escort to convey him to Moksobomyo.
The state of the Prince's army was a most remarkable contrast to that of
the Royal troops. "Most of the officers belonging to the Prince," says
Burney, "were bold reckless characters and notorious robbers, and the private
soldiers although miserably armed and very deficient in military appoint-
ments in comparison with the King's troops, many of them even at the
advanced posts having only sticks and bludgeons, were yet full of confidence
owing to the knowledge which they had acquired from numerous deserters
of the state of panic of the King's troops and full of threats and boastings
and of false tales of the Prince having been joined by hundreds of thousands
and by large parties of wild Khyens and Kakhyens". (Ibid para 10).
Burney reached Moksobomyo at 2 p.m. on March 25. The Prince sent
out a party of horse and an officer of rank to meet him and himself received
Burney with great kindness and cordiality. Hostilities between the two
armies were now suspended. The conversation and discussion that took
place between the Prince and the Resident may best be described in Burney's
own words :-
"I soon discovered that there was not the slightest chance of his listen-
ing to any proposition for reconciling him with the Government of Ava.
Upon hearing the object of my Mission he observed, 'I will not act like the
English and retire from any territory upon which I have once planted my
standard. At his request I retired and prepared a short note to try and
effect a reconciliation I sent him the note at 9 o'clock at night and he
summoned me at 10 and kept me until 1 o'clock in the morning going over
a long list of his grievances and of instances of misgovernment and rapacity
on the part of the Queen and her brother Menthagyeh...To the proposi-
tion of making the King's son Ain-Ye-Meng he observed that his father the
late Ain-Ye Meng had directed the present King to consider him the present
prince of Tharawadi as the successor to the throne before the King's son. I
used my best endeavours to excite his fraternal feelings, reminding him how
very kind and affectionate the King had always been to him from his youth,
and entreating him to avoid the reproach of injuring one who with all his
faults had been so excellent a brother to him. But the Prince was not to be
moved and openly expressed an opinion that his Brother did not deserve to
reign for having raised a woman of low origin to the station of first queen.
I hinted that the Ministers of Ava were still summoning troops from all
quarters.......He laughed at the intelligence and shewed that he was intimately
acquainted with everything that had been said or done at Ava......and that in
fact some of the officers and Ladies of the Palace were in secret correspon-
dence with him". (Ibid para 11).

From the general attitude of the Prince, Burney felt convinced that the
former had made up his mind to strike for the throne and that he would
certainly capture Ava. He, therefore, made an attempt to prevent as far as
possible murder and massacre, and save the lives of the King and officers.
He asked the Prince that if he persuaded the King to open the gates of
Ava at once to him and submit to his authority, would he promise not
to destroy the city or take the life of any of the officers or inhabitants. The
Prince replied in the affirmative "and then distinctly assured me", says
Burney, "that if the Government of Ava would not resist him but would open
the gates of the city he would not injure his Brother the King in the smallest
degree, molest any of his officers, put a single soul to death or allow the city
to be destroyed and plundered. I repeatedly asked him, may I make such a
pledge in your name to the Ministers of Ava and he repeatedly answered in
the affirmative." (Ibid para 14).
The Prince's second son Thait-tengyeh exercised the greatest influence
over his father and wished to recommence hostilities after waiting for three
days. When Burney pointed out to him the impossibility of returning an
answer in so short a time, he said, “Very well, for the sake of the English
Resident we will give the King's troops a fourth day before we attack and
destroy them." Burney found all the officers of the Prince full of threats
and boasting, and most anxious to prevent reconciliation since they did not
wish to be deprived of the rich harvest of plunder in the city of Ava. Burney
warned Thit-tengyeh that the whole of his proceedings would be reported to
foreign countries and urged him to so act as to secure a high character for
wisdom and humanity in other parts of the world. He replied, " Yes, yes, I
know all will be printed in the Gazette (using the word) and I will have my
statements inserted then too." (Ibid para 15).
Burney left Moksobomyo on March 26 at 4 p.m., passed through Halen
and Tharain, and reached Ava on the 27th evening at 9 o'clock. The whole
of the 27th he was on horseback and covered 40 miles under a hot sun.
Early on the 28th morning he saw the King and Ministers, and reported to
them the particulars of his mission. He advised them not to trust the Bo
Mhu Woon's army, and on their confessing that no other fresh troops were
available he communicated to them the pledge of the Prince that he would
not shed blood if the King and his Government submitted to him. He at
the same time advised the King not to trust the Prince but to obtain a fresh
pledge from him. The King requested Burney to go again to Moksobomyo
and obtain a definite pledge, but Burney was physically not in a condition
to undertake such a journey a second time and consented to send his clerk
Edwards with a letter to the Prince. (Ibid para 16).

On April 2, the whole of the Royal Army, retreating before rebel forces,
crossed over from Sagaing to Ava and the same evening the advanced
divisions of the Prince's army occupied Sagaing; by April 4, Ava was
surrounded on almost all sides by rebel forces. The struggle for the throne
was soon brought to a head by the mission of priests under the Prince of
Mekkhara. The details of the interview between the two princes are not
available, but Tharrawaddy made two demands :-(1) That the Queen be
removed from the presence of the King, and (2) that Menthagyee, the Bho
Mhu Woon, the Myawadee Woongyee, three other Ministers and seven of
the principal military officers who had been most faithful to the King go over
to his son Thait-ten-byn at Sagaing. On fulfilment of these requirements he
promised to stop all hostilities, come over himself and take charge of the
Hlut-dau of Ava, and not injure a single individual in person or property.
The deputation returned to Ava on April 6, and the King and his Govern-
ment decided to accept the terms. (Ibid. para 21).
The Prince of Mekkhara told Burney that he believed that Tharrawaddy
would keep his pledge, and asked him to accompany the Ministers to Sagaing.
Burney proceeded to the Palace and found the 13 officers ready to cross over
to Sagaing apparently confiding in the goodness of the Prince's intentions.
"But the communication" says Burney," appeared to me so very extraordin-
ary, demanding as it did the persons of all the Ministers and officers who
composed the most efficient members of the government and garrison that 1
could not avoid suspecting some cruel treachery, and offered to precede the
party to Thait-ten-byn and endeavour to secure them civil treatment by my
presence.'
." "At this time," continues Burney, " there were at least 7 or 8000
troops within the walls of Ava infinitely better found in arms and ammunition
than the Prince's forces and with a large number of guns of every description.
But these troops were not only panic struck to an incredible degree but much

The young prince received the officers with civility, but refused to
permit them to return to Ava. Soon after Thait-ten-gyeh took possession of
Amarapoora, and on his arrival in Sagaing his first act was to place two
pairs of irons on Menthagyee. (Ibid).
The Prince of Tharrawaddy arrived at Sagaing on the evening of
April 8. He had no interview with his brother the King, but the very next
day, in spite of all his promises and pledges, he took over the sovereignty of
the Kingdom and gave out that his brother had abdicated in his favour. He
took the title of "Yatana Thainga Kounbaung Myo Ya Than Meng" i.e. the
King who has dominion over the city of Yatana Thainga Kounboung. He
housed Bagyidaw in a mat building to the south of the capital; the Queen
and her daughter were removed from his presence and lodged in a distant
mean building. All the military stores and other valuable property were
transferred to Sagaing. The separation from his Queen caused the ex-King
so much distress that finally she was allowed on April 16 to return to her
husband. (Ibid. para 23).
As to the 13 Officers, Tharrawaddy had his brother the Bo Mhu Woon.
confined near his own house in Sagaing; he put irons on Menthagyee and
the rest, and sent them to the common jail in Ava. He also apprehended
and confined in irons 20 others of the Ministers and officers of the late
Government and confiscated their property. The principal ex-Ministers were
examined and under severe punishment they were made to say that Men-
thagyee had intended to put the Prince of Tharrawaddy and all the other
members of the Royal family to death and usurp the throne. The wife and
two daughters of Menthagyee and the families of some of the imprisoned
officers were also subjected to horrible tortures in order to induce them to
reveal their supposed hidden property. (Ibid).
The next day April 16, Burney visited the jail and a most heartrending
scene fell upon his view. He found there Menthagyee and about 30 other
men, who had lately been the first Ministers and Officers of Burma, confined
in irons, their legs in chains, with scanty dirty clothing on them, and treated
as common felons. They all expressed great gratitude for what Burney had
tried to do for them, and begged him to continue to intercede in their favour.
According to Burmese custom prisoners were not fed by the government,
but none of the servants or friends of the unfortunate men had the courage
to bring food for them. (Ibid. para 28).
Burney was so over come by the sight in Ava jail that he feared the new
King would break his pledge. He protested strongly against the treatment
of the ex-Ministers and advised Tharrawaddy to so act as to secuse fame
and honour in foreign countries. His reply was as follows:-" Col. Burney
has not got my pledge in writing, and the 500 men in Parliament in England
will laugh at it as no written paper can be produced. It is my Royal
prerogative to put to death any of my subjects whom I find guilty of crime."

Bagyidaw was kept in confinement and died a natural death in 1845. It was

ing out a general massacre of the Ministers and officers of the late King as
well as the sack of Ava. (Ibid. paras 29-45).
The new King appointed his son Thait-ten-gyee President of the Hlut-
dau. The new Woongyees, Atwenwoons, Woondauks etc., were largely drawn
from his supporters, the robber chiefs. The capital was first transferred to
Kyouk Myoung, but the King was seriously inclined to make Moksobomyo
the seat of his government; finally, however, he decided upon Amarapoora
and to this city he arrived on the 12th December 1837.
