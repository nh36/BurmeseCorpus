In the field of Sino-Tibetan, where even the precise classification of the languages has
not been determined and the nomenclature has consequently not become stabilized, it seems
preferable to introduce a logical system of nomenclature. The names of linguistic groups
have consequently been given terminations which have the widest similar application in the
names of Indo-European linguistic groups. Thus the ending - an denotes the family, as Sino-
Tibetan, corresponding to the same termination in Indo-European. The ending -ic denotes
a main division of a family, as Sinitic, Manic, Daic, Baric, Burmic, and Karenic, correspond-
ing to similar subdivisions in Indo-European such as Germanic, Keltic, Baltic, Slavic, Hellenic.
And the ending -ish indicates a sub-group of one of the main divisions of a family; thus
Burmish, Loloish, Katšinish, Kukish and Luish are sub-groups of the Burmic division of
Sino-Tibetan. This ending is similarly employed to denote the IE sub-groups: English, Irish,
Lettish, Polish, Spanish, Swedish, Danish, and in slightly corrupted form in Welsh and
French.

Occasionally a linguist chances upon a language of no great importance
for comparative purposes or for the number of its speakers, which has
been very briefly and imperfectly recorded, yet which, because of the
problems it raises, arouses considerable interest.
Such is the case with Mru.
The greater portion of its vocabulary agrees with Kukish, particularly
with Southern Kukish. However, because the Mru inhabit territory
occupied chiefly by Southern Kukish peoples they live alongside the
Khami such lexical similarities are suspect of borrowing from Kukish.
-
-
Behind this is an older stratum of the language, such as the greater
part of the numeral system and some other common words, which represent
a Tibeto-Burmic linguistic stock which is certainly not Kukish and is more
archaic in some respects than written Burmese. This stratum of the
language may be a borrowing from a now non-existing Tibeto-Burmic
stock.
The reason for believing that this stratum is also borrowed is that,
after eliminating all the words mentioned in strata 1 and 2, there remains
quite a number of words, some of them very common words such as those
designating parts of the body and the numerals for "one" and "two",
which bear no resemblance to any corresponding Tibeto-Burmic words, and
indicating a possible non-Tibetan origin of the Mru.
These three strata of the language may be designated the oldest, the
middle, and the newest strata.

"
In the following discussion, all the Tibeto-Burmic parallels for a Mru
word will not be given, but only those which are the nearest to the Mru
word. Thus while Bu. waṁ bear is undoubtedly the same root as occurs
in Mru tom, the latter is closer phonetically to K. *tǎ-wŏm, O. Bh. dom.
Similarly Bu. wak "pig" looks as if cognate to Mru pak, but no satisfactory
phonetic explanation has yet been found for the interchange of initial p and
w in Tibeto-Burmic; but Mru pak does correspond to Mikir pʻak, Bh. p‘ag.
Mru wi "blood" is undoubtedly cognate to Bu. swe:, but the Mru word
does not resemble the Burmese form as closely as it does Trepang wi or
Vayu vi. Also Mru k'im "house" looks as if it had some connection with
Bu. 'im, but it is closer to O. Bh. kʻyim.
Where two or more parallels, all equally good, are found for a Mru
word, and one of these parallels is Kukish, it has been assumed that, because
of the geographical proximity of Kukish and Mru, the influence has been
Kukish, and the other parallels have usually been excluded from
consideration.

It is at once apparent that Mru is much closer to Kukish in its
vocabulary than it is to Burmish. But how about the phonetic agreement?
Konow (or Grierson?) states regarding Mru that "in the main it follows
the phonetic system of Burmese, —". I find no evidence to support this.
Where Burmese writes final ui as in 'muig': (really 'mui) "sky", Mru has
mu; Bu. "kui “nine", but Mru tǎ-ku. In having final u equivalent to -ui
in Bu., Mru agrees with Kukish. The vowel i in the Mru word for " eye'
agrees with Kukish, not with the ya in Bu. myak. The e in Mru pe "to
give" agrees with Kukish pe, pek', not with Bu. 'pe which is from Sino-
Tibetan *pi. Mru mai "fire" agrees with Khami mai, not with Bu.
'mi. The preservation of the prefix t-2 in the Mru words tǎ-ru, tǎ-ku "six",
"nine", agrees with Kukish, not with Burmese which has k'rok, 'kui.

Mru agrees, then, neither lexically nor phonetically with Burmese, but
rather with Kukish.
But the Mru live alongside the Khami, a Southern Kukish people, and
such lexical similarities are suspect of borrowing from their more powerful
Kukish neighbors. These lexical parallels with Kukish may then be
tentatively termed the latest stratum of the language.
One might suppose that with the elimination of the Kukish borrowings,
the resulting vocabulary was the original Mru. But it is doubtful if this
is the case. Much of the Mru vocabulary which remains seems to belong
to a still older stratum of borrowing belonging to a middle period of the
history of the language. The relationship of this part of the word stock
is most clearly seen in the numerals.
The Mru word for "nine", tǎ-ku, cannot be from Burmese because
it preserves the original prefix t-, which Burmese does not. On the other
hand, it cannot be from Kukish proper, as the Kukish word was *tǎ-kua,
i.e. had an augment in -a. It does correspond to O. Bh. dgu.
The Mru word rǎ-hnit "seven" does correspond to Bu. k'ŭ-hnatš.
Kukish has quite a different form and so does Old Bhotish. On the other
hand, Mru riyat "eight" corresponds almost exactly with L. riat, but
not with the Khami which has tǎ-ya, kǎ-ya. And if the word were
borrowed from Kukish, we should expect it to be borrowed from the closest
neighbors of the Mru, the Khami.
Now one can hardly suppose that the Mru went about borrowing
numerals here and there, "seven" from the Burmese,
from the Burmese, “eight" from the
Kuki, “nine” from some Tibeto-Burmic stock which was neither Burmese
or Kukish. It is more probable that all the Mru numerals which correspond
to Tibeto-Burmic come from some Tibeto-Burmic stock which was neither
Burmish nor Kukish, nor any other now known.
If this hypothetical Tibeto-Burmic language rested upon the Mru
numeral system alone, the evidence would indeed be weak. But a consider-
able number of Mru words other than numerals do have lexical parallels
outside of the Kukish and Burmish groups. Such are:

PREFIXES
There is some evidence of original prefixes having been preserved in
Mru. This is particularly noticeable in the system of numerals. The various
consonant prefixes all seem, however, to have fallen together, regardless of
their origin, into the prefix tǎ-. If this is true, it must not be assumed that
the original p- or m- or k- prefixes have been replaced by another prefix t-.
It is probably due to phonetic degeneration; for in Kukish proper, if the
prefixes are preserved at all, there is not a p- and an m- prefix in the
same dialect, but only a p- or an m-, representing both the original prefixes.
Mru probably shows a further phonetic degeneration, all the original pre-
