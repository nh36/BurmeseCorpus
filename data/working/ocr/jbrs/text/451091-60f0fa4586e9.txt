(1) Introduction
Natural rubber was discovered by Columbus on his second visit to South America.
Three centuries passed before it was brought into commercial use in Europe.
Rubber is obtainable from numerous varieties of trees, but nearly the whole of
world's rubber comes from a tree known as Hevea Brasiliensis, belonging to the family
Euphorbiaceae. The tree thrives best in equatorial climatic conditions, characterized by
constantly high temperatures and even distribution of rainfall through out the year. An
annual precipitation of about 80 to 100 inches suits it best. It likes well drained grounds
and an elevation ranging from sea-level to about 1000. Above 1500' elevation, it becomes
stunted. Soils, derived from basic igneous rocks, enable the rubber tree to grow to a large
size and give high yields. Those derived from acid igneous rocks (e.g., granite) suppost
good stands of rubber. Rubber may grow fairly well on alluvial soils if the drainage is
good and the water-table lies at an average depth of about 42". Soils, derived from shales
and phyllites, usually contain a lateritic hardpan, which resists the penetration of the roots
of the rubber tree.

In 1873, 2000 Hevea seeds from the Amazon were successfully transplanted at the
Royal Botanical Garden at Kew, near London. Later, some of them were dispatched
from Kew Garden to equatorial and tropical areas of the British Empire for transplanting.
According to the available records, Hevea rubber was first introduced into Burma
at Mergui around the year 1876, to be more exact, at the Taw-ya-kyaung area lying near
the entrance to the Mergui aerodrome site. While most of the rubber plants, used for
the experiment, came from the (Royal Botanical Garden, Kew, later, some, probably, also
came from) Ceylon.'
The first rubber plantations were set up by the British companies, the Crown
Rubber Company being the pioneer. Seeing the success of the European rubber com-
panies, the Chinese, Burmese and other Asian peoples also started establishing rubber
plantations. With the beginning of the 20th. Century, the rubber acreage of the district
grew rapidly. Soon, rubber plantations came to appear in some other districts of the
country also.
When rubber was first grown, the price for rubber was £1 (K. 15/-) per pound.
But, because very few people owned estates then, only a handful of people benefitted by
the extraordinarily high price. As the rubber acreage and out-put increased, the price of
rubber gradually declined.
In 1922, the British Parliament enacted the Stevenson Rubber Act to keep the
production of rubber at around 60% of the out-put of rubber for the year ended 31st
October 1920. The export quota was lowered or raised around the 60% figure in accor-
dance with the fall or rise in rubber price.
In 1925, partly because of the Stevenson Scheme and partly because of the greater
demand for automobiles in the United States and the rapid post-war economic recovery in
Europe, the price of rubber once again rose to a height of two and a half kyats (3 shillings
4 pence) per pound causing a rubber boom in the country. The increase in the demand
for rubber was to some extent due to the fact that man had found many new uses for
rubber in the manufacture of many articles. The rubber boom made people, living in
those areas, which are more or less suitable for the growth of rubber trees, extend the
rubber acreage and out-put.
In 1928, the Stevenson Scheme was dropped, because it failed to lower the rubber
out-put proportionately with the fall in rubber price and hold the rubber price above the
unprofitable level. The failure of the Stevenson Scheme was partly because its restrictive
aim was thwarted by the smuggling of rubber from the British possessions in South-East
Asia (including Burma) to non-British possessions. The scheme unintentionally caused
the American rubber-consuming firms to set up their own rubber plantations. Hence, it

The increase in the world natural rubber out-put became coupled by the increase in
the out-put of synthetic rubber, made from coal, coke and petroleum and the introduction

By 1930-31, the supply of rubber became much greater than the demand for rubber.
Moreover, in that year, there was a general depression in the U.S.A., which was the largest
buyer of rubber in the world. The general depression in the U.S.A., that later spread to
the whole of the capitalist world, reduced the demand for things which contained rubber.
This in turn drastically reduced the demand for rubber. In Burma, the rubber price
plunged down to 3 pices or 3 pyas per pound, i.e. 4/5 of a pence per "pound. That made
the production of rubber unprofitable to almost all the rubbr plantation owners in Burma
(see reflections on Diagrams 2 and 3). The few rubber planters, who still managed to
make both ends meet or make a little profit, whose plantations were located on more or
less ideal locations and were efficiently run.
In 1936, a new quota system for the production of rubber was introduced in Burma,
as in most other rubber producing parts of the world. The quota system was used again
to artificially cut down the out-put of rubber to match the demand. Due to the quota
system, the rubber price went up somewhat and the production of rubber became tolerably
paying. Since then, the rubber acreage of the country had been increasing steadily.
During the Second World War, some rubber trees were abused by unruly people,
some destroyed by aerial bombings and strafings and still others by other military actions.
However, the extent of damage, suffered by the rubber estates, in comparison with the
total rubber acreage in the country, was slight. It was so, because the main arena of
fighting was located outside the main rubber areas. Partly because of disturbed political
situation, partly because rubber could no longer be sold to the traditional western market,
and, also, partly because it could not be sent in any appreciable quantity to Japan, the
only possible buyer, as a result of the Allied attacks on the Japanese shipping, the out-put
of rubber once again dwindled almost to nothing. At the time, some of the rubber,
produced, was used for the manufacture of fuel for cars, as it had been found that cars
could be run on rubber fuel. But, due to the primitive way of distillation, the rubber fuel
clogged up the carburettor and produced too much smoke in the car cylinders. Hence,
the carburettor needed cleaning and the cylinders needed decarbonization too often. In-
spite of the short-comings, rubber fuel did succeed in keeping many cars and trucks in
Burma moving. But for the locally produced rubber fuel, the hard life in Burma during
the last war could have been much harder.
Around 1951-52, with the recovery from war effects in many parts of the world, and
with the appearance of two import new buyers in the world rubber market, i.e. the Soviet
Union and Peoples' China, the price of rubber once again rose to about K. 3.00 (i.e. about
£ 1/5) per pound, causing another rubber boom in the country.

Noticing the boom, the Government wanted to take a share in the profits of the
boom and made itself the sole buyer of rubber in the country. After 1956, rubber buying
firms were set up at headquarters of rubber growing districts to serve as buying agents for
the Government. They were supposed to have based their price quotations on Singapore
price quotations less 12 pyas (3 1/5 pence) per pound for expenses and freight. Due to the
lack of competition in buying, the local rubber price came to decline appreciably. Hence,
the boom ended, and as rubber earned little profit, the rubber estates were no longer main-
tained as well as before. But, rubber was (and is) a good earner of foreign exchange and
Burma's foreign exchange position had plenty of room for improvement. Realizing the
above situation, the Government decided to give better incentives to the people, engaged
in the production of rubber. Accordingly, the Government took steps for giving up its.
monopoly in the buying of rubber by allowing non-government buyers to buy rubber from
the rubber producers at competitive prices and to export it themselves to foreign countries.
Rubber buying firms in the districts, representing the Government, were to continue buying
rubber for the Government in a freely competitive manner with non-government buyers.
The Government expects to recover at least a part of the decline in profit, caused by the
sacrifice of its monopolistic position, from the export duty, charged on rubber, exported
by private exporters. It is hoped that with the reintroduction of the free rubber market
within the country, the out-put and export of rubber will increase.
Since Burma gained independence, the number of European-owned big estates has
declined sharply. Most of them have been bought over by the well-to-do Burmese citizens
of the rubber growing districts. The rapid exodus of British interests from the rubber
industry scene was primarily due to the lack of law and order in the country side. Because
of the insurgency, it has not been safe for estate owners, especially big estate owners, to
supervise the operation of their estates at close quarters. So, they are more or less at the
mercy of their managers and other employees. Under these circumstances, the standard of
efficiency in the operation of the estates naturally has been low and dishonest practices on
the part of employees have been more frequent than at normal times. Much rubber, then,
has reached the black market and much, it seems, has been smuggled out of the country.
The law-less elements sometimes try to extort protection money from the estate
owners. The estate owners, then, are placed in an unenviable position. If they pay the
protection money, they risk prosecution by the Government. If they do not, they risk
persecution by the lawless elements.
(2) Burma and the World Rubber Market
Between 1948 and 1958, Burma's position as a rubber producer dropped from the
11th position to the 14th position. In the former year, out of 21 listed rubber producing
states, Federation of Malaya, Indonesia, Thailand, Ceylon, Vietnam, Sarawak, Liberia,
Brazil, North Borneo and India were ahead of Burma. In the latter year, of the 18 listed
states, only Papua, French Cameroons, Brunei and French Equatorial Africa were behind
Burma. By 1958, in addition to those states which were ahead of Burma in 1948, Nigeria,
Belgian Congo and Cambodia had overtaken Burma.

As of the year 1958, Indonesia and Malaya stood out as a class by themselves,
Indonesia producing 678,3000 metric tons of rubber and Federatton of Malaya 674,300
metric tons. In the same year, Thailand, which stood third produced 139,600 metric tons,
while Burma produced 33,600 metric tons, i.e. only about of the amount, produced by
Thailand.
Burma then is still very much under-developed in the field of rubber production
and is on the low rung among nations as regards rubber export and the manufacture of
rubber goods. Burma's export of rubber normally amounts to but about 0.5% of the
world's rubber export, even though Burma has been exporting about 90% of its rubber
out-put every year.¹
The rubber industry of Burma is to a great extent dependent upon the world
rubber market, because the country cannot consume most of the rubber it produces. On
the 20th September, 1960, delegates from 21 countries, including Burma, held the 15th
meeting of the International Rubber Study Group. Major subjects discussed at the meeting
included (i) the problems of stabilizing the world rubber price, and, (ii) the supply-demand
position of both natural and synthetic rubber in the world market. The above meeting
was followed by another meeting on the 26th of the same month, under the auspices of the
Natural Rubber Research Institute. More than 280 scientists attended the meeting.2
The general export of rubber from Burma to other countries was permitted under
licences issued by the Agricultural and Rural Development Corporation. The Corporation
appointed its agents in Malaya, U.K. and on the continent of Europe. As a result of the
lifting of the monopoly, producers now can obtain prices more in line with the interna-
tional market. Due to a shortage of rubber in world market, rubber prices rose consider-
ably during the year 1959, in spite of the fact that United States and United Kingdom
released substantial quantities from their stockpiles.³

(3) Significance of Rubber Industry in Burma
As of July, 1960, the 116,000 acres of rubber lands produced 12,000 tons of rubber
a year.
Because Burma generally exports about 10,000 tons of rubber per year, it annually
acanires about K. 40.000.000 worth of foreign exchange from its rubber. Unlike in the
case of rice, rubber can be sold readily to a large number of countries.3 One advantage
of rubber as a crop is that it can be grown on relatively poor and hilly areas, where other
crops
will not grow. It is easier to raise rubber than rice. Rubber estates are safer from
calamities than rice farming. It is quite possible to establish small rubber estates by
individual enterprising labourers without incurring too much expenses. For the time
being, the quality of rubber, produced by small estates, is generally poor. However, since
all that is necessary to improve the quality of rubber is to prevent the dirts from getting
into the rubber, it is quite possible for the small estate planters to produce good quality
rubber. Rice, the most important export item brings in about K. 800,000,000. If rubber
industry is fully developed, it can bring in nearly double the foreign exchange, accounted
for by the rice export, and also give employments to cover 500,000 people. On an
average, about 1000 acres of rubber give employments to about 350 people.

(5) Distribution of Rubber Areas
TIN HTOO
The rubber areas are located mostly in Lower Burma, especially in the Tenasserim
division. The division contains about 2/3 of the country's total rubber area. The estates
are generally located on sloping grounds and they often have access to road communica-
tion. Some rubber estates have access to railways and some to streams or seaways.
The Tenasserim division roughly coincides with the Tenasserim coastal strip. In
1928-29, its rubber area amounted to 78,000 acres out of about 95,000 acres of the whole
country, accounting for 82% of the latter. By 1960, the proportion of the total rubber
area of the Tenasserim division in relation to the country's total had somewhat declined,
But, still the proportion remained impressive i.e., 102,000 as against about 130,000 or 78
as against 100. Due to the preponderance of the Tenasserim division over other divisions
in the field of rubber production, the trend of the country's total rubber production fairly
closely reflects that of the Tenasserim division (see Diagram 2).
The Pegu division, which roughly coincides with the eastern part of the Deltas
Region, had a rubber acreage of about 16,000, accounting for 17% of the country's total,
in 1928-29. By 1938-39, as a result of the transfer of some rubber areas from the Tenas-
serim division to the Pegu division, the latter's rubber acreage achieved a sudden increase
at the expense of the former i.e., by nearly 10,000. Since 1939, the former has shown a
steady increase in rubber acreage while the latter has shown a slow, steady decline. This
phenomenon is accounted for by the fact that the Pegu division is not climatically as
suitable as the Tenasserim division for rubber planting and production. The rubber
acreage of the Pegu division (i.e., about 20,000) constituted only about 15% of the country's
total in 1960.
The Irrawaddy division has all along been standing a poor third in rubber acreage.
In 1928-29, its rubber acreage of around 500 constituted only about .5% of the country's
total. In 1959-60, its rubber acreage amounted to but .4% of the country's total. The
division coincides roughly with the western part of the Deltas Region.

(6) Incidence of Different Sizes of Rubber Estates
Regarding the trends of changes in the total areas under plantations of different
sizes, it can be stated that the smaller the estates the greater has been the gain in total
acreage and the bigger the estates the less has been their gain in total acreage.
During the period 1948-49 to 1959-60 the greatest increase in total acreage was
attained by the 0-50-acre estates. The total acreage of this class of estates increased by
47%, i.e. from about 36,000 to about 53,000. The total acreage of 50-100-acre estates
increased from about 9000 to about 10,000. That of 100-500-acre estates expended by 5%,
i.e. from about 20,000 to 21,000. During the same period, the total acreage of above-500-
acre estates even decreased by 2%, i.e. from about 50,000 acres to about 49,000.

(8) Manufacturing of Rubber Goods
The making of rubber goods within the country is done mostly in the Rangoon
area. The rubber works in this area are producing tennis shoes, canvas shoes, gum-boots,
rubber slippers, and, bicycle tyres and valve tubes. They employ about 1500 workers.1
The country also has firms or factories, which are concerned with the manufacture
of leather shoes with rubber soles, foam rubber for cushions and mattresses, rubber
balloons, cushions for cups and saucers, life buoys and inflatable pillows. Burmese
slippers with rubber soles are made on an appreciable scale in the Magwe division.

(9) Problems of the Rubber Industry in Burma
Back in 1953, the government announced that it would not nationalize the rubber
industry within 30 years.2 However, soon after that announcement, the government
nationalized the rubber export and also decided to impose income tax on rubber planters.
The government's actions dampened the enthusiasm on the part of rubber planters to
maintain and expand the rubber industry. But, after General Ne Win's government came
into being in 1958 the nationalization of the rubber export enterprise was withdrawn and
an attempt was made to replace income-tax on the rubber industry with export duty on
rubber:
Owners are generally disatisfied with the Government's policy of taxing any planta-
tion which brings in an income of more than K. 4200 (approximately £280) per year. The
plantation owners have to spend K. 1300/- to K. 1600/- (approximately £90 to £100) for
maintaining each acre of their plantations for about 9 to 10 years before the trees start to
yield rubber. So, rubber estate owners feel that requiring them to start paying income-tax
one year after the establishment of the plantation is unfair According to the planters,
for an outlay of K. 100/-, they can expect but a profit of K. 1.67, because a sum of
K. 98.33 has to be paid as income tax.3 Some people claim that the Government will
get more revenue from the rubber industry if it encourage the industry and collects taxes
only on the sales of the commodity.

(11) Conclusion
The Britishers introduced Hevea rubber into Burma around the year 1876. Later,
Chinese, Burmese and other Asian peoples also participated in rubber planting, and with
the beginning of the 20th Century, the country's rubber acreage expanded fast. During the
rubber slump period of the thirties, only the well equipped Britishers were able to operate
the rubber estates to make slight profits. They were able to do so partly because they had
established their estates on ideal locations.
The second world war caused only slight damages on the rubber plantations of the
country. However, as rubber could not be exported during the war time, the rubber pro-
duction virtually stopped. A little rubber that was produced, was used as raw material
for the manufacture of rubber fuel for cars.
During the rubber boom period of the fifties, the rubber buying and exporting
business was nationalized. The nationalization burst the boom inside the country, and as
a result, the rate of investment in the rubber industry declined. The government then
rectified the situation by denationalizing the rubber buying and exporting business, for
rubber was and is an important earner of foreign exchange. That brightened up the
prospects for the future development of the rubber industry in the country.
The country's position among the rubber producing states of the world is on the
low rung, being the 14th among the 18 listed states, despite the fact that the rubber
industry is important to the economy of Burma. Though Burma is still primarily just a
producer of rubber, it has begun to use some of its rubber in manufacturing goods, and it
is likely that the country will try to increase its ability to consume its own rubber profit-
ably. If Burma's rubber industry becomes fully developed, rubber may come to replace
rice as the most important earner of foreign currency, bringing in about K. 40 crores
(approximately £27 million) worth of foreign exchange."
Except in post-slump years of the thirties, the rubber acreage of Burma has been
rising fairly steadily. During the recent years, the country's rubber acreage has made an
appreciable climb. However, according to official estimates, only 8% of the total land
area, suitable for rubber planting is being used for such purpose. Hence, there is plenty
