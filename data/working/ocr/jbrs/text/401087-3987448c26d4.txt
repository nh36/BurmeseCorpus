TYPE OF RESEARCH REACTOR FOR BURMA '
by
U Hla Nyunt
A study of the attached summary of reactor developments abroad
will make it evident that even
even small underdeveloped countries
are planning ahead for a small research reactor program. Before
any country can expect to benefit from the many promises of
the peaceful uses of atomic energy, many people trained in Nuclear
Science and Engineering is an essential prerequisite, this new branch of
learning incorporating the various disciplines of knowledge involved in
the study of atomic energy. For training in Nuclear Science and
Engineering, a Research Reactor, which ought to be more properly
called a training or Experimental Reactor is an essential tool, and that
is exactly the reason why so many small countries are planning for a
small research reactor.
A nuclear reactor is essentially a device for producing and controll-
ing a nuclear fission chain reaction with neutrons accompanied by the
release of energy, mainly in the form of heat, and the production of
neutrons and nuclear radiations. A research reactor is mainly for
producing neutrons and nuclear radiations, and cannot produce
electricity.
If Burma is to try to keep up with the developments in the use of
atomic energy, even amongst its own circle of underdeveloped count-
ries, she will have to have more and more people trained in Nuclear
Science and Engineering, and sooner or later a small research reactor
will become a necessity.
It is the object of this paper to consider the pertinent factors
affecting the choice of a research reactor type for Burma, within
the limitations of our purpose and requirements.

(A) Particular Purpose and Subsequent Flux
•Before choosing the type of research reactor, any country or insti-
tution should have clearly in mind the particular purpose for which the
reacter is being installed and a detailed plan of the long term use the
reactor will be put to. A research reactor is essentially a source of
neutrons, and groups of trained personnel experienced in neutron and
nuclear radiation studies, who would be qualified to make an efficient
use of the available neutrons is an important prerequisite.
The purposes and uses of a research reactor may be classified into
the following main Categories
(1) Training
(2) Research
(3) Planning and development of power reactors
(4) Production and use of Radioisotopes
(5) Gamma Irradiation Facility.
The research and training program is in turn determined by the parti-
cular thermal neutron flux. A high flux (useful for research) is
usually connected with high powers, but not necessarily together. In a
given reactor, the flux is proportional to the power output at a given
moment. But two different reactors operating at the same power may
not have the same flux. Since the power produced in a unit volume
is what determines the flux, compact designs are ideal for a research
A disadvantage of the compact design, on the other hand, is
that it permits fewer experimental facilities. Fast flux is useful for the
studies of interaction of fast neutrons with matter (radiation damage,
solid state studies and fast cross section determinations), and if both

Existing and new materials which may be applicable for reactors

Shielding Studies
The shielding arrangement is generally a very costly part of the
reactor.
The efficiency of shielding of material locally available in
Burma should be investigated for purpose of using them in concrete
used as biological shield for future power reactors.
In addition, experiments can be done in heat generation in shield
by capture gammas, optimisation of alternate layers of different shield-
ing materials in one composite shield, and radiation attenuation in
different materials.
(3) Planning and Development of Power Reactors
For efficient conversion of heat into electricity, the temperature of
the core has to be as high as possible, limited only by engineering
difficulties. The corrosion of fuel cladding by the moderator or coolant
increases rapidly with the increase of temperature. Other problems are
melting points, phase changes, heat exchangers, conductivity of coolants,
chemical reactions between coolant and moderator, pressurization, loss
of thermal conductivity due to bubble formation etc. Therefore research
work should be done on the following subjects of control of corrosion,
melting points of cladding materials, heat exchange design, pressure
vessel design, and film coefficients.

(4) Production and use of Radioisotopes
The use of radioisotopes in future by Burmese medical doctors,

comparatively cheap Sb-Be photoneutron source by having Sb 124
re-irradiated after its short half life of 60 days.
(5) Gamma Irradiation Facility
The spent fuel elements, removed from the reactor core for
chemical reprocessing act as sources of strong gamma radiation for
about three to six months. The sterilisation effect of gamma radiation
on food such as potatoes, meat and grain, is gaining ground every day.
In particular, storage of paddy and preservation of grain is one of our
headaches, and the spent fuel elements from our research reactor, while
left to cool, could be a big help in preventing the spoilage of our tons
of paddy. Also, because of the poor refrigeration facilities in trans-
portation of vegetables and meat, prices in Rangoon are much higher
than in the places of origin, and if only gamma irradiation could come
to the rescue it will indeed be a boon.
The cracking of petroleum by gamma irradiation is also one of
the fields of study Burma should be interested in.

(B) EXPERIMENTAL FACILITIES
As far as experimental facilities afforded by the various types are
concerned, the natural uranium - graphite reactor, due to its large core
volume makes neutron flux available over a large volume. In addition
to the possibility of simultaneous arrangement of a large number of
experiments, radioisotopes can be produced on a large scale. In fact
this type is now-a-days mainly used for large scale isotope production
which is not necessary in Burma. The limitations of low flux makes it
very unsuitable for any experiment of the materials testing type.
In the heavy water reactors, due to the large slowing down length
in heavy water, the experimental volume is still considerable to provide
a large number of experiments. Later high power and improved types
of this design approach 10 14 flux, making it also suitable for certain
high flux experiments. The disadvantage is that compared to light water
reactors, the ratio of fast to slow flux is low. Because of its compara-
tively smaller volume than graphite reactors, they are not used for
radioisotope production on a large scale.
In the light water moderated homogeneous reactor, the core is
small and approximates a point source of neutrons making beam
experiments possible. The disadvantages are small irradiation volume
and a comparatively low flux of 10 12.
In the light water moderated heterogeneous reactors of the
Swimming Pool or Tank Types, the advantages are the flexibility of
arranging experiments, a comparatively high flux of the order of 10 13,
and a high fast to thermal flux ratio. The main disadvantage is the
rapid decline in flux with distance from the core due to the small
slowing down length and diffusion length of neutrons in water.

Availability of Materials
The choice of a particular type of research reactor will obviously
depend upon the availability of materials, either locally or from foreign
countries. The problem will be discussed under the two main headings
of nuclear fuel and shielding material, as these two items are not
included even if a research reactor is to be bought ready fabricated
from abroad.
Nuclear Fuel This is of course the heart of the reactor and its
availability certainly the most important factor. Between the choice of
natural uranium and enriched uranium, the main advantage of the
choice of natural uranium is the easy availability in a nearly free
market (§ 18 per lb. or $ 40 per Kgm ). If natural uranium is to be
used however, the irony of nature is that abundant ordinary light water
cannot be used as a moderator and either graphite of nuclear purity
( $ 0.33 per lb.) in many tons, or heavy water ($ 28 per lb.) will have to
be used. The disadvantages of using natural uranium are (1) Weak
flux for a given power and consequently ( 2 ) more severe restrictions on
choice of reactor materials for strict neutron economy (3) big reacting
volumes to minimize surface neutron losses and therefore (4) elaborate
and costly cooling facilities, and (5) large construction costs for
building.
The use of enriched uranium, where the percentage of U 235 has
been artificially increased, has got certain technical advantages over
that of natural uranium, namely increase in multiplication factor and
decrease in the critical mass, although the control of enriched reactors
is slightly more difficult because their small size makes them rather
susceptible to variations in flux and power. However, the cheapest
and most versatile research reactors developed both in U.S. A. and
U. S. S. R. all use enriched uranium. The difficulty with enriched
uranium is that it is not available in the open market, and the only
way to get it is through bilateral agreements with U.S. A. or U.S. S. R.,
the only two countries who can afford to offer others or through the
International Atomic Energy Agency. It is to be noted that the
maximum enrichment available to foreign countries is only 20% from
U. S. A. and 10% from U. S. S. R.

The length of time a fuel element can be left in an operating
reactor without need for replacement is of course a very important
factor for small underdeveloped countries like Burma, which might not
be able to do their own fuel reprocessing for a number of years to come.
The need for refuelling arises because of many factors such as radia-
tion damage, resulting in physical distortion of shapes, mechanical,.
thermal, and corrosional fatigue (mainly in natural uranium reactors),
poisoning by fission products, and in the case of enriched fuel types,
simple depletion. In cooled reactors, with a flux under 1013, the
majority of the fuel elements will be serviceable for a number of years,
whereas for reactors above 10 13, it might be a matter of months, as
for example, 6 months in CP5. Fuel elements in the Swimming Pool
type, if operated at 100 KW, mostly need not be replaced until after
about 3 years operation, but of course, if the power level is increased
the fuel life will decrease accordingly. The homogeneous water boiler
type uses enriched uranium in the form of a solution of enriched uranyl
sulphate in water, and therefore fuel reprocessing reduces to a compa-
ratively simpler chemical problem.
Shielding Material
Besides the building housing the reactor and associated laboratories,
sufficient shielding will have to be provided around the core and
reflector with locally available material. Shielding is to reduce neutrons
as well as gammas to safe tolerance levels around the working space of
the reactor. Neutrons are most efficiently slowed down and eventually
absorbed by lighter atoms whereas heavier atoms are required for
gamma shielding. The problem is complicated by the fact that
secondary gammas also arise out of neutron capture in the shield itself.
The theory of shielding is highly complex, and experimental results
have to be highly relied upon for shield designing. The practical
shielding material now most commonly used for research reactors of
normal research flux is Barytes concrete of the density of about 3.5.
In Burma, since lead seems to be available cheaply, lead concrete seems
to be economical. A cast iron thermal shield followed by 4-8 feet of
concrete with lead punchings seem to be possible for our Burmese
reactor. In the Swimming Pool Type, the water itself acts as most of
the shield, and the lead concrete will be necessary only in the lower
walls, ordinary concrete being sufficient for the higher parts.

In the matter of shielding also, the smaller the active lattice, and
the more compact the design, the smaller will be the total volume of
the shield. The use of natural uranium, besides involving the high
cost of heavy water or graphite also brings up the cost of shielding.
This is another argument in favour of enriched uranium reactors.
Controls and Safety
Heavy water reactors have one advantage over light water reactors
in the fact that the mean life of neutrons in water is much greater than
in light water. So for the same excess reactivity, the reactor rising
period will be much larger in a heavy water reactor and the control and
safety machanism will find time to operate.
While working with a light water reactor great care should be
taken not to introduce any considerable excess reactivity suddenly.
Also automatic shut down equipment should be very quickacting.
Homogeneous reactors have the advantage that due to very high
negative temperature coefficient of the fuel solution, a sudden rise in
power is stopped by the core becoming subcritical. The production
of gas by dissociation of water and high corrosion rate of the fuel
solution on the core vessel and cooling circuit introduce difficulties of
control.
Natural uranium graphite reactors need the presence of small
excess reactivity. This type is therefore comparatively safer from a
control point of view. Due to the much larger core volume, a large
number of control rods can be used keeping the flux distribution more
uniform.
Nuclear reactors, and research reactors especially are designed
now-a-days so that there is inherent safety, by having a large built in
negative reactivity coefficient. This is done by thermal coupling of the
fuel elements to those portions of the reactor which give a substantial
reduction to the neutron multiplication when heated. For example, in
the case of enriched water moderated reactors close thermal contact
between the fuel elements and the water moderator leads to reduction
of density and vaporization in case of a runaway, and automatically
shuts down the reactor. The control system is also designed so that
if there is too high a power level, a serious reduction in coolant flow,
or any major failure of fuel elements, monitoring by detection instru-
ments and control channels leads to a scram. For small countries like
Burma just planning for a first research reactor, it is advisable to

choose designs avoiding the use of high temperatures, pressures and
mechanical stresses to avoid the risk of a violent dislocation even if a
runaway occurs. Auxiliary cooling circuits are necessary to counteract
delayed heat production even after the reactor is scrammed. However,
no mechanical system is absolutely fool proof and the probability of
an accident cannot be ruled out. It is now generally accepted that a
reactor runaway cannot be compared to an atomic bomb explosion.
In fact a nuclear reactor runaway has little or no mechanical violence
in itself, but as a consequence, the rupture of fuel elements may lead
to escape of radioactive fission products, and the chemical reactions
between different components could lead to a chemical explosion of
considerable violence. For this reason, a gas tight building, not
necessarily explosion proof may serve to prevent the spread of
fission products if an accident does occur. The CP 5 building is main-
tained at a slightly lower than out side atmospheric pressure in a gas
tight building to minimize outward leakage risks.
The main hazard therefore is the spread of radioactive dust,
vapour and gas. As in other countries, the Government of Burma
will have to set up a Reactor Safeguards Committee to conduct a study
of the meteorology and hydrology of the proposed reactor site before
final approval. The meteorological and hydrological characteristics
studied are temperature inversion, mountain drainage, prevailing wind
direction, rainfall frequency, run off, water supplies, oceanography
and seismologic data. It is desirable to have the wind blow from the
reactor to uninhabited areas, and not to be located on a main water-
