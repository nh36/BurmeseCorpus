Experiments on maze learning are no longer popular with modern
psychologists ever since the discovery of new and more effecient types
of learning situations such as those presented by the Skinner problem
box and the Lashley discrimination apparatus. The chief difficulty with
maze experiments is the existence of a variety of maze types which
makes standardization impossible. An added difficulty is, the labour
involved in recording the data objectively and accurately. However, there
are still two good reasons for continuing experiments with mazes. First,
maze learning is one of the oldest types of learning experiments used
in comparative psychology and as such has not only historical interest
attached to it but also enables one to understand and appreciate how
modern theories of learning have developed. Secondly, maze learning
resembles a problem so commonly met with in a real life situation.
Consider a man in the street of a new city attempting to reach an
appointed place. He is essentially in a maze, One is then led to ask
such simple yet fundamental psychological questions as to how he learns
what he learns in a new situation as this, whether what he has learned

What has remained controversial for the psychologists from a
wealth of maze data is the more basic problem of what is actually
learned and how it is learned. Numerous psychologists have tried
to contribute to the solution of this fundamental problem of
learning theory and they can be roughly divided into two groups,
namely, the Tolman Group and the Hull Group with Tolman and Hull
respectively as their spokesmen. Tolman has contended that the study
of a rat's behaviour at a choice-point in a maze provides a good start
for the development of a sound theory of learning. His wellknown
theory so developed may be termed a “field of cognitive theory". Hull's
theory is recognised as a "reinforcement theory". To the question
"What does the organism learn?", Hull answers that it learns to make
a response to certain stimuli. To the question, How does it learn?",
he answers further that it learns through gradual accumulation of habit
strength which depends on the action of receptor-effector process in
close contiguity with reinforcing conditions. Tolman's theory is
diametrically opposed to Hull's. First, Tolman rejects the principle of
the automatic action of stimuli. He would rather look upon stimuli as
signs or guide-posts, not as response-releasing triggers and contends
that the learning of the organism consists in learning the meaning or
significance of these signs. Learning is therefore not so much formation
of habits but a series of cognitive changes. The organism learns the
relationships of objects or what-leads-to-what with reinforcement having
only a very indirect relationship with the actual learning. In his view,
learning is not the mere automatic reproduction of reinforced condi-
tioned responses but the establishment of cognitions or the restructuring
of the perceptual field in terms of goal objects.

The influence of both theories for a psychology of learning has
been considerable. In spite of the fact that a systematic bias is present
in both points of view, it is generally recognised that they are not
mutually exclusive and represent merely different assessments of the
general importance of certain variables of which learning is a function.
There is a wealth of evidence to support both views and yet further
research is needed in order that one may give a verdict between the two
theories or reconcile the conflicting interpretations. The following
experiments were carried out to test certain logical implications of both
theories.
The experiments may be classified into three divisions. The first
experiment deals with the problem of transfer of training, the second
with Hull's goal gradient hypothesis, and the third with Tolman's latent
learning principle. Another experiment was conducted to test the place
versus movement interpretation of maze learning.
Experiment I Transfer of Training
-
Accidental observations by the earliest experimenters with mazes
showed that the learning of one problem seemed to influence the solu-
tion of other problems favourably. Dashiell reported that even general
adaptations of the animals to laboratory conditions led to positive
transfer and that rats seemed to have learned the general orientation
toward the food box which was later transfered to new maze situations.
Hunter observed favourable transfer even when the new maze had
the reversed arrangement of the previous maze. However, in later
experiments he discovered negative transfer.

One may ask what is transfered. We can say definitely that what
is transfered is not sequence of movements. In the literature, a number
of conditions conducive to positive transfer have been pointed out but
there are apparent differences in the studies compared. The general
consensus of opinion is that positive transfer is due to general factors
while negative transfer is due to specific factors, namely, interferences.
This is obvious because there is considerable evidence that even
with mazes designed to produce learning of movements opposite of the
movements learned in the original maze, we still find positive transfer.
It is therefore clear that what is transfered is not learned movements.
One explanation is that positive transfer is due to reduction of
emotional factors, decrease in exploratory movements and tendency to
return to the starting place. In our experiment we found considerable
evidence in support of this explanation. In the early part of the
experiment, urination and excretion, long fixed stops at the starting
place and end of blind-alleys, attempts to go down from the maze,
crouching and tremblings were found with all the rats. As the experi-
ment proceeded such emotional behaviour gradually diminished.
Since the number of errors made in each blind-alley was recorded
throughout the experiment we may also test Hull's famous goal gradient
hypothesis. According to Hull, the first correct paths to be learned
are those nearest to the goal or food-box. In other words it means
that there will be an increase in number of errors in a backward order.
Graph 4 shows that the results of our experiment are in agreement with
the prediction to be drawn from the hypothesis. The graph has been
plotted from the average number of errors made by all rats on the day
when each of them reached the criterion for the first time. It can be
seen that 66% of errors were made in the first half of the maze and
33% in the second half. The smallest amount of error (8%) was made
in the final blind-alley which was nearest to the goal or food box.
In conclusion we may say that our experiment clearly demonstrates
positive transfer of training in maze learning. It also indicates that
transfer is not due to similarity in sequence of movements. In addition,
the experiment provides data supporting Hull's goal
hypothesis.

among many paths to the same goal the organism will choose the

Apparatus: T-shaped elevated mazes, each one foot in length,
were used.

Experiment III - Latent Learning
As discussed previously, the Hull Group emphasises the role of
reinforcement (reward) in learning. They explain the correct choice of
turns in mazes as learned responses conditioned by reinforcement, the
implication being that where there is no reinforcement there will be no
learning. Now a serious blow to this reinforcement theory of learning
has come from latent learning experiments carried out by Tolman and
his disciples. By latent learning we refer to learning in the absence of
immediate observable reinforcement for the responses. For a time such
learning is unobservable and remains hidden but the effect of such
learning can be readily inferred from the performance of the individual
following the introduction of reinforcement later. Hence the name,
latent learning. This experiment was designed to observe whether such
learning takes place in a maze situation.

Experiment IV-Place Versus Movement Learning
The reinforcement theory of learning implies that in a maze the
organism learns the correct sequence of movements. The cognitive
theory, however, asserts that the organism learns to follow signs to the
goal or a particular behaviour-route, not a movement pattern. In
1946-47 Tolman, Ritchie and Kalish performed a series of experiments
to support the second view. From one experiment they were able to
report that even after cerebellar damages the rats retained what they
had learned previously. In another experiment the rats first learned
a particular maze and then the paths were covered with water. Now,
under this second condition the rats needed to make a new kind of
movement pattern, i.e., swimming. They still retained what they had
learned previously. From such data Tolman's Group has concluded
that maze learning is essentially place-learning. Our experiment was
designed to test this interpretation.
Apparatus T-shaped elevated mazes with units one foot long
were used. A central path eight foot long with moveable starting
paths each one foot long were so combined to produce a T-maze
pattern. Other materials used were a stop-watch, an electric bulb and
data sheets. The whole pattern is illustrated in Figure IV.
