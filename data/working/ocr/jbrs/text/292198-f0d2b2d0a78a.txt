Netherlands India
(1) Historical and Political
In "Netherlands India " ( Cambridge University Press)
Mr. J. S. Furnivall introduces his readers to the history and the
present condition of a part of the world of which most of us know
too little. Netherlands India which we more often but less correctly
refer to as the "Dutch East Indies ", is a territory whose geographic
situation and climatic condition render it by no means dissimilar
from Burma, and whose development provides both parallels and
divergencies from that of Burma. The study of the system of
government which the Dutch have adopted is well worth while, not
only for the intrinsic interest of the matter, which Mr. Furnivall
so ably brings out, but also for the lessons which may be learnt for
our own guidance. Mr. Furnivall provides an excellent summary of
the history of the Netherlands India in the pre-European age, and
shows clearly the various cultures which have prevailed there at
different times and which have evolved into the modern culture.
Here the parallel with Burma is obvious, even in such minor
points as the economic value of the periodic fairs which were held
at the major ports in earlier days, just as, for example, the great
Shwe Dagon Fair at the Tabaung festival was annually held for
centuries. European influence arose when the Portuguese began to
trade to Malacca for spices, but the Portuguese settlements perished
largely through their own tyranny and oppression, and by the end
of the sixteenth century it appeared as if the tropical Far East
would pass under Muslim domination.
In actuality, however, it was the Dutch who came to predominate;
the closing of the harbours of Portugal to the Dutch when Portugal
passed into the hands of the Spanish King forced the Dutch to seek
tropical luxuries, notably spices, for themselves; and so in the last
decade of the sixteenth century a number of trading companies were
formed which in 1602 were combined into the Dutch East India
Company by the genius of Oldenbarnaveld, who succeeded in
overcoming for this particular purpose the provincial and parochial
jealousy which was for centuries so marked a feature of Dutch
history. The Company's charter was authorised by the States-
General which became a shareholder; but the States-General did
not exercise any effective control; at no time did the States-General
assert the principle early laid down by the English Parliament in
the case of the East India Company of London, that the Company
was subject to the sovereignty of Parliament and that Parliament
might prescribe such conditions as it chose for the continuance of

The Portuguese were soon swept out; but the rivalry of both
French and English forced the Dutch to take firm steps to establish
a monopoly; and that could be done only by making themselves
the political masters of the seas and the land. The " Amboyna
Massacre" of 1623, when a number of English venturers
executed with, from the English point of view, no justification,
practically ended the London Company's efforts; and it was to a
great extent the success of the Dutch in driving them from the
Indies that forced the English to turn their attention to India
proper. Having disposed of their European rivals, the Dutch were
able, as opportunity served, to extend their control over the Asiatic
inhabitants, though for long they confined themselves to little more
than the sea-board.
,,
The business of the Dutch Company, as of the English Com-
pany, was trade, and just as in India the European employees were
known as "writers", etc., so in the Netherlands Indies, employees
ranked as Upper Merchants, Merchants, Junior Merchants, etc.
The officers of the Company were concerned in administrative func-
tions only in that they exercised judicial authority over other Euro-
peans. Asiatics, whether native or foreign, were under the authority of
their own headmen, though towards the end of the eighteenth
century officers were appointed who, in addition to commercial
duties, were expected to report on the behaviour of the "regents
who were the actual rulers of the people.
"The Company
aimed above all at trading privileges: only with reluctance and in
order to control the trade did it extend its rule, first over the ports,
and then along a narrow coast line, and not until the middle of the
eighteenth century, when the trade in spice was losing its import-
ance, did it assume the privileges of sovereignty. One effect of
the extension of sovereignty, or perhaps a cause of it, was that the
income of the Company came to include tribute in kind, which in
course of time began to form the principal source of revenue;
though in this the Dutch differed markedly from the English
Company in India.
"
Gradually the Company undertook the regulation of local
production, and restrictions were placed on, for example the num-
ber of clove trees which might be planted, and again the production
of coffee was restricted. The territories came to be, in fact, a vast
plantation. But this policy had disastrous results for the indigenous
inhabitants. Slave labour was used to ensure production, and a
slave-trade, with all its attendant horrors, grew up in the islands;
restrictions on cultivation forced cultivators into dacoity; all economic

activity other than cultivation was suppressed; and the dependence
of the "regents" on Dutch power instead of on their customary
authority and the traditional consent of their people, caused a break-
down of the whole system of social life. Corruption was rampant
among the officials of the Company.
Thus, as indeed was the case with the English Company in
India in the eighteenth century, the indigenous people suffered, the
Company moved steadily towards bankruptcy, and the only gainers
were the Company's employees. The English Company, however,
survived while the Dutch Company failed. The reasons for this appear
to be that, apart from the war with England in 1781-84, which ruined
Dutch commerce generally, the development of the Industrial Revolu-
tion, fifty years earlier in England than in any other part of Europe,
produced a demand for a prosperous India which could absorb Eng-
lish manufactures, whereas this circumstance did not operate in Hol-
land; again, the influence of the 'Nabobs"-the retired employees
of the Company who used their ill-gotten gains to make themselves
a nuisance in English politics, rendered it essential to remedy the
situation; whereas the oligarchic system of government in Holland
did not give opportunity for such a crisis to arise. Moreover, the
English Parliament had a measure of control over the Company which
the Dutch Government lacked.
The troubles of the French Revolution killed the Company
finally. The new Batavian Republic, one of the client-states which
the French set up, took over colonial affairs and the Company
was dissolved in 1800. Various schemes for reforming the admini-
stration were considered, but before anything effective had been done,
the Indies were seized by the English on the invitation of the exiled
Stadhouder. The new government of Sir Stamford Raffles com-
pletely altered the whole basis of administration. The Dutch had
wanted Eastern produce; the English wanted to sell their own produce.
From the English point of view, it was essential to promote the
prosperity of the indigenous peoples so that their consuming power
should be increased; whereas an increase in their consuming power
was detrimental to Dutch interests. Also, the English held the seas.
and could carry on trade; whereas the Dutch ever since they fell
under French control had been practically precluded from overseas
trade. The main object of Raffles's policy was therefore to break
down the monopoly of trade and to concentrate on "the advancement
and improvement of the people". In effect, therefore, in the few
years that he controlled the Indies, Raffles endeavoured to intro-
duce the system with which he was familiar in India. And the
success of his work is indicated by the fact that his reforms of the
administrative and judicial systems were to a considerable extent
continued by the Dutch later. In the view of a Dutch writer,

After the retrocession of the Dutch East Indies at the end of
the French Wars there followed a curious development known as the
"Culture System", which has no parallel in the history of British India.
The essence of the Culture System was that "people should pay
their dues in kind instead of in cash" in effect, the duty of the
officials came to be to supervise cultivation, on the proceeds of
which they received a percentage, and this cultivation became com-
pulsory, Government taking the produce which it needed at its
own price. European contractors were encouraged to help operate
this system. At the same time, home industry was encouraged by
the establishment of a closed market in the colonies, and both
imports and exports were carried in Dutch vessels only. For the
marketing of the produce, a special company, the Nederlandsche
Handelmaatschappij, was formed, under the control of the King
of Holland.
The result was that "Java became one large State business con-
cern". In administrative matters, the former merchants had become
officials, but the hereditary position of the "regents" was confirmed,
and the practice of holding the Vergadering, or conference of local
officers, in each district and sub-district was introduced as a valuable
means of keeping the Government in touch with the people. The
Culture System had unfortunate results in some directions, not unlike
the effects of the old Company's rule; officials concentrated on the
cultivation of export crops, and neglected the essential crop, rice.
The headmen, with the support of Government, exacted, to an extent
previously unknown, compulsory labour; the concentration of market-
ing in alien hands deprived the people of any opportunity of gaining
experience of trading. Both the social system and the economic
sense of the people were destroyed. Moreover, the financial system
continued to be unsound. In 1840 it was admitted that no final
balance of the accounts in Netherlands India had been struck since
the settlement with the English in 1816; and the resultant scandal
led to the reform of 1840 by which the Dutch Parliament was given
some degree of control over Indian finance, but even so the power
of Parliament was in practice limited to an examination of the
accounts, and the control of Government remained with the King.
Further reforms were effected in 1854, under which arbitrary rule
was to be replaced by the rule of law, and ten years later it was
provided that the Indian budget should be passed annually by the
Parliament. So far the object had been to secure from the Indies
a surplus of income over expenditure which would form a contribu-
tion to the home treasury; it was the failure to provide that surplus
which had led to the demand for a reformed system. But the
expensive wars in which the Government was involved with Achin

"Moreover, in British India the European element, both in official
and non-official life, is confined to the higher ranks, in Netherlands
India all down the scale, among both officials and non-officials, there
are Europeans, and many are of far humbler social standing and on
far lower pay than in British India. These men often belong to
democratic political associations and to trade unions in Europe, and
they bring their politics and their unionism with them to the East".
The nationalist movement, under this influence, has tended to
become anti-capitalist and revolutionary, and has led to strikes and
to serious riots. As a natural reaction. a Fascist movement has
sprung up among the Europeans; and "thus the course of political
development, among Europeans and natives alike, has shown a
steady trend in the direction of extremes".
In administration the development of autonomy has been far
slower than it has in India. Local councils were formed in the
principal towns in 1905, and in the Java residencies in 1907;
subsequently councils were constituted in some of the smaller towns
and some rural areas. But all the members were nominated except
that in the urban areas provision was made for the partial election
of Europeans in 1908 and of Asiatics in 1917, while originally the
majority was both European and official, with an official as chairman.
In the central administration no form of representation was
introduced until 1916, when a Volksraad with half its members
elected was instituted: but the elective members were chosen not
by popular vote but by the local councils, so that this reform cannot
be regarded as a serious instalment of autonomy.
Even so, the Volksraad showed itself almost violently opposed
to the executive. After the reforms of 1927, though the Volksraad
was increased in size, it was still largely nominated and the elective
element was still chosen by the local councils. It received, however,
wider powers in finance and legislation; but there is no symptom
of "responsible government"; the system is rather that of "repre-
sentative government". The system is thus widely different from
that now established in Burma. It may also be noted that officials
who are members, whether elective or nominated, exercise wide
freedom of speech and do not hesitate to criticise Government ; and
it is an accepted principle, and surely a healthy one that free
expression of opinion in the Volksraad shall not prejudice an
officer's career. Another contrast with Burma is that officers may
freely criticise Government in the Press: also healthy principle;
while the European legislators do not form a single racial party, but
are divided by varying shades of political opinion. The great defect
of the Volksraad is that it confers the power of criticism without
imposing responsibility.

There are other contrasts with Burma. In Netherlands India,
the official of the specialist services has never been paid less, and
has at periods been paid more, than the administrative officer
point which the Burma Fiscal Committee failed to mention in the
course of its somewhat extensive analysis of comparative rates of pay
(from which, indeed, the scales of pay in Netherlands India, surely
at least as relevant as those in the Sudan, were entirely excluded).
Another difference from conditions in Burma is the high standard
of education, which is fixed at the same level as the standard in
Holland itself. In social matters also the general level is in some
ways high, at least as high as in Upper Burma; and in some
respects, such as the complete suppression of mendicancy, higher.
It may be noted that education is not among the subjects
transferred to autonomous bodies, and that firm measures are taken
to suppress unlicensed teachers. The Dutch policy towards religion
is also note worthy; instead of pursuing the policy laid down in
British India by the disastrous Proclamation of 1858, the Government
has sought the co-operation of indigenous religious institutions. As
was the case in Burma in pre-British days, a register of clerics is
maintained and no unauthorised person may assume a religious title;
the local clerics are appointed by the Regent or headman;
Government appoints benches of clerics to try certain types of cases
and in every township court a cleric is appointed adviser on
religious matters; the income of the mosque treasuries is derived
from contributions levied under official regulations and Government
contributes to the building of mosques.
All these and many other matters are detailed in Mr. Furnivall's
work and the reader from Burma may well ponder over this
absorbingly interesting account of Netherlands India, and consider
what we in Burma may learn from the successes and from the
failures of our Dutch neighbours.
(2) Social and Economic.
As the sub-title indicates the book is "A study of Plural
Economy." This central theme is dominant in many chapters of
the book, and is the subject of the concluding chapter, where the
author writes:
"Here then we would seem to have one reason, perhaps the
chief reason, why Western economic theory is but of limited appli-
cation in a tropical dependency. The reason lies in the political
constitution of a tropical dependency as a plural society, because in
a plural society there is no community as a whole, and problems of
political and economic science differ fundamentally from those of
homogeneous societies. In a homogeneous society the basic problem

of applied political science is how to provide most adequately for
the expression of social will....But in a plural society the basic
problem of political science is far more elemental, it is impossible
to provide a vehicle for the expression of social will until there is a
society capable of will, and the basic problem of political science in
such a community is the integration of society."
The reader of the first edition of Mr. Furnivall's “Political
Economy of Burma" will find a continuity of thought in the two
works. But in the intervening eight years Mr. Furnivall has fortified
his earlier convictions with considerable research into Dutch colonial
literature which reveals many authorities with ideas similar to his
own.
The plurality of society in Netherlands Indies, as in most
tropical countries and in the United States of America arises, writes
Mr. Furnivall, because of the co-existence of two or more com-
munities within the same country, with quite different backgrounds
as to culture, religion, race or language, or any combination of these
factors. Hence the relationships between members of the different
communities are reduced to the economic sphere, almost exclusively,
as they leave in other spheres with regard to non-economic matters.
Mr. Furnivall goes on to develop the thesis that this plurality of
society is intensified by the presence of more than a million Chinese,
monopolizing most of the retail trade of Java, and insulating the
Japanese from contact with the Europeans and hence with capita-
listic methods. The reader must tread warily lest he trip up over
Mr. Furnivall's simultaneous contentions: (1) that plural economy
emphasizes only the economic side of life as regards relationships
between separate sections of society and (2) that the Japanese have
been kept in a pre-capitalistic condition, economically, because of the
working of the system of plural economy. Dutch writers have gone
much farther than Furnivall in emphasizing the pre-capitalist economy
of the Javans. As indicated below, Furnivall feels that in Java, as
in Burma, the indigenous peoples have made great progress in trades
where both production and consumption are carried on by
themselves.
The reader in Burma cannot help comparing the position
of Chinese in Java with that of Indians in Burma, and Furnivall
assists in many places, by comparing and contrasting the two
communities.

control over economic life, the Government of the Netherlands
Indies has always been much more paternalistic, or socialistic, in

Land alienation, just now likely of enactment in Burma, has
always had some place in the rural economy of Java. There are
many exceptions to the rule against alienation of land, and many
estates are "leased" by large companies for long periods of years
from the native owners, but "in comparison with British India
moneylenders own very little of the land.”
From very early times the State has carried on enterprises as
entrepreneur. Cinchona, tin, forest products and coal-these are
only a few of the enterprises in which Government has either estab-
lished a monopoly or competed with private businesses. Pawnshops,
which are Government owned, and managed by Javans, from the
standpoint of the customer are definitely superior to private
pawnshops. Twelve thousand small paddy banks in Java (very
slightly co-operative in nature) are under state control.
Since 1929 the Government has taken a lead in fostering
restriction schemes which have attempted to bolster the prices of
rubber, tin, sugar, cinchona and (with regard to imports) rice. It
is to be recalled, however, that the Dutch did not participate in the
Stevenson Restriction Scheme for rubber, soon after the world war.
One cannot but be impressed with the immense number and
variety of welfare agencies and efforts in Netherlands Indies. The
franchise is limited to literates, but a Bureau for Popular Reading
has carried on an amazing work in the promotion of adult education.
It has published 1000 volumes, in editions averaging 5000 copies.
It
appears that schools are not as yet as common in Java as they are
in Burma, and that the theoretical rate of literacy is lower, but the
circulation of books, which is one fair test of what literacy does for
a people would seem to indicate that Java is in an enviable position
among Asiatic peoples. The official lists of voters furnish the Bureau
with an excellent list of people to whom circulars may be sent,
advertising forthcoming volumes. Welfare is frequently promoted
by "gentle pressure from zealous public officials, and of course
instances may be cited of an excess of zeal in the exercise of such
"pressure". Village committees and general meetings, village funds
to provide bazaars, studs, dispensaries, schools, pure water supply
and street lighting are among the common objects of the zeal of
uplifters, and the village bazaar seems to be the chief source of
income with which such projects are financed. Let no one imagine
that Javanese villagers are much more inclined than Burmese
villagers to leap with joy to tasks making for improvement of rural
conditions. The changes have come chiefly as a result of patient
and persistent external pressure.

Village libraries have kept pace with the other activities of the
Bureau, and in 1930 there were 2686 libraries under its auspices.
Travelling book vans go about from village to village selling the
books published by the Bureau. As many as 2500 books per mensem
have been sold by one of the agents in charge of a van.
In some respects welfare activities in Java have not made any
greater progress than in Burma. Literacy is less widespread, but
when one considers that pupils in the Dutch-Vernacular schools are
required to learn Dutch, English, German, Malay, Sudanese and
Javanese, one must be surprised that there are any who survive the
ordeal. On the other hand, for the vernacular schools, vocational
education has progressed to a degree which might excite the envy
of the framers of Burma's "Campbell Report." In Java, as in
India, an ironic result of the welfare activities of a foreign govern-
ment has been a tremendous increase in population so that: "It is
still a general complaint among European well-wishers of the
Javanese that they increase in numbers instead of welfare."
Since 1900 Dutch officials have forcibly combined villages in order
to give more generous incomes to a smaller number of headmen.
The resulting unnatural divisions have been resented by the people
and some headmen dare not show themselves in certain parts of their
jurisdictions. Java like Siam is still blessed with a Capitation Tax.
Although the Chinese have built up an almost impregnable
position in retail trade and in many types of small-scale manufacture,
the native Javans have not been wholly lacking in initiative. Buses
and taxis furnish opportunities for investment and for employment
among the Javans, particularly as the rickshaw has always been
prohibited or discouraged in Java. In some cases Javans have even
built up successful rubber plantations, using European methods, and
even settling business transactions with cheques. The recent influx
of Japanese business men has resulted in more jobs for Javans, for
whereas European and Chinese shops tend to employ clerks of their
own races, the typical Japanese shop is run chiefly by Javans.
And Japanese competition in Netherlands India has made
amazing progress, whereas the trade of the Netherlands with her
eastern colonies has declined more than proportionately. In 1830
nearly half the trade of the Netherlands Indies was with the home
country and just before the World War Netherlands still enjoyed a
third of the trade, but by 1930 this share was cut down to one-sixth.
Students of contemporary social and economic problems in
Oriental countries should read this thoughtful work, which is well-
documented and indexed, and provides a mine of useful information
regarding the fourth most populous country of Asia.
