Both the Mon and the Burmese inscriptions relate the same events,
which may be divided into five sections:-
Section I introduces the Buddha seated at the foot of the Räjāyatana
tree at the end of the seven weeks after attaining Buddhahood.
This occupies the first 18 lines of Mon Face Ä and the first
25 lines of Burmese Face A.
Section II describes the interview of the two brothers with the
Buddha (Mon Face A line 18 to the end at line 52 and Burmese
Face A line 25 to B line 26),
Section III describes the theft of the two hairs by the Naga King
(Mon Face B up to line 8 and Burmese Face B line 26 to line 35).
Section IV tells of the last days of the two brothers (Mon Face B
line 8 to line 13 and Burmese Face B line 35 to line 41).
Section V describes the repairs done to the Pagoda by Mon Kings
(Mon Face B line 13 to the end at line 40 and Burmese Face B
line 41 to the end at line 47.)

A glance at the texts will show that the top lines of all the four faces
are more or less illegible especially at the sides. The worst preserved of
all is Mon Face A, which in many places has been restored with the help
of the corresponding Burmese face. On the other hand, Burmesc Face B
is undecipherable towards the end (section V): only Sona and Uttara can
be made out of the names of the missionaries and Kings mentioned in the
corresponding Mon Face B, which fortunately is well preserved towards.
the end. The Burmese account is more diffuse than that of the Mon.
Thus the Naga King opens the Mon Face B, whereas he does not come
till line 26 of the Burmese Face B. The Burmese inscription suffers
towards the end for its diffuseness in the beginning and the middle; for it
cannot give more than 9 lines to the last section which takes up no less
than 27 lines in the otherwise less diffuse Mon inscription. On the
whole the two inscriptions supplement each other so that we get a fairly
complete account of the original document. The list of the Mon Kings
who made repairs to the Pagoda are valuable as evidence of contemporary
history. And it is safe to assume that the inscription stones were set up
by the last King mentioned, namely, Dhammaceti, the author of the
famous Kalyani Inscriptions.

of the Mons. Thus the link with the Shwe Dagon in Lower Burma is
established.

The Name of the Shrine.
It has been seen above that Trihakumba and Pokkharavati are
names of Singhuttara. The distinction between the two names seems
to disappear and the town is referred to as Trihakumba Pokkharavati
or Pokkharavati Trihakumbab. However that may be, Trihakumba
drops the ha and becomes Trikumbac. This is further corrupted into
Dagon d, which still further changes into Lagune. The Bagui Dhatuwanf
identifies the Isle of Rammanagur, which we have seen lies to
the east of Simghuttara with the modern Trihakumba Lagun town.
Thus Rammanagur, east of the Shwe Dagon Hill was the original town
of modern Rangoon. The Prakui Rajathaputs derives the name in this
way "Why was it called Rammanagur? Long ago a king named Aram-
maṇaraja ruled there. The succession of kings was 32. It was originally
named Arammana town after King Arammaṇaraja, who first built it.
When King Arammana was no more, the name later on changed to
Rammanagur."
The Buddha on Mt. Simghuttara.
The Skein Dhát Sokh says "In the 8th year of Buddhahood the
Buddha practised piety on Mt. Samsumagiri. After the term of re-
sidence was over, the Omniscient Buddha attended by 2000 saints came
on a tour through the sky The Omniscient Buddha with 2000 Saints
arrived at Asitañcanagur town in the country of Ukkalapa. King Siri-
dhammāsoka of Asitañcanagur surrounded by the townspeople made a
great worship and ministered to the Buddha and his Clergy with food.
In his faith the King offered the Buddha his crown. Then the Buddha
and his Saints having reached the 4 rivers, Vaņṇanadi, Migganadi,
Gonadi, Ganganadi practised piety for a while on Mt. Simghuttara of the

on the north.”

Such are the details of the story of the founding of the Shwe Dagon
Pagoda, which has grown out of the Mahavagga account of the meeting
of the two brothers with the Buddha. Whatever may be the real date
of the founding, it is a comfort to know that the sacred hairs abide in the
Pagoda and will continue to do so, as long as it remains the object of
adoration by the Buddhist world. For we have the assurance of no less
an authority than Buddhaghosa, who says, "Then, in whatever place
the relics do do not get worship and honour, they go, by the power of
the Buddhas' will, to a place where they get worship and honour."
