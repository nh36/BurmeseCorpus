SOME SOCIAL EFFECTS OF THE RELIGION AND
SACRIFICES OF THE ZAHAU CHINS.
The object of this article is to outline briefly the religious ideology
and sacrificial ritual of the Zahau, to show the correlation between
these and other aspects of their culture, and to illustrate something of
the value of Anthropology to the administrator. As, however, the part
of Burma of which I write is not very well known, I think a very short
description of the physical background of these people would not come
amiss.
The Chin Hills lie between the plains of Burma on the east and
the Lushai Hills of Assam on the west. The inhabitants are known to
us by the Burmese term Chin, and they are closely related to the
Lushai, about whom Col. Shakespeare wrote a monograph, and the
Lakher of whom Parry wrote. The Zahau sub-tribe occupies the
western half of the Falam subdivision in the centre of the Chin Hills
district. The country is very hilly, and covered with fairly dense sub-
tropical forest. The people are agricultural, cultivation being entirely
on the so-called taungya system, though of a more regulated character
than usual. Mithan (bos frontalis), pigs, goats, dogs, and fowls are
bred, mainly for use in sacrifices, though a good number of fowls and
pigs are killed for the entertainment of village and private guests.
The religion of the Chins, including the Zahau, has been described
as animism; but as I have recently read several treatises each putting
forward different theses on the subject of what is animism, I am no
longer certain of what it means myself, and will therefore jettison the
word and state simply that the Zahau believe in the existence of
supernatural beings having human characteristics, which they call
khuavang or rai: in the ghosts of ancestors, which they call kala: and
in evil spirits which they call huai, all of which I will deal with later
when discussing the object of various sacrifices.
They believe that all these spirits appreciate good food and
liquor, and that communication with them can be established by means
of sacrifices at which ritual presentation of food to the spirits takes
place; they believe also that the earth and the sky are divided up into
various strata inhabited by different spirits.
The Paradise of the Zahau, which is call pial rang, is understood
to be in the sky, while the Land of the Dead, or mithi khua, is below
the earth, and consists of several villages to which the Guardian Spirit
allots the ghosts of dead people according to the manner of their
demise.

Rank on earth is retained in the mithi khua, and there is a special
mechanism for ascertaining the exact social status of any spirit newly
arrived from the Land of the Living. Those who have all the necessary
qualifications, that is to say, who have completed the Feasts of
Ċelebration for the taking of a head and the killing of many specified
varieties of wild animals and birds, and who have completed also the
Feasts of Merit (which I have so named to fall in line with a useful
terminological precedent created by Dr. Hutton in his books on the
Assam tribes), and whose daughters have remained virgins until
marriage, rise to Paradise and live happily ever after. In this
connection it should be noted that a wife shares the credit for any feast
given by her husband during the time they were living together.
In the case of the large majority who cannot aspire to Paradise,
conditions are very different. They live in spirit form for one normal
life cycle in the mithi khua, after which they "die "again and go to a
tiny spirit world no larger than a basket, where after one more spirit
life they perish and turn into butterflies. I noted this belief did not
prevent Zahua from killing butterflies.

The beliefs connected with the road to the mithi khua and the fact
that spirits can return along it to communicate with the living have a
direct controlling effect on ritual acts at burial. For instance, when a
-
pregnant woman dies an axe is placed beside her corpse so that her
ancestors' spirits can split the hollow tree trunk through which the
ghosts of all such unfortunates must attempt to squeeze their distended
bodies on the way to mithi khua. An interesting point in connection
with such deaths is that a plantain just about to burst into flower is
planted above the grave, in the belief that when it does flower the dead
woman will be able to give birth to her spirit child. Again it is custom-
ary to place beside any corpse while lying in state the articles used in
life the weapons, haversack and nicotine gourd of a man, and the
spindle, weaving apparatus, and pipe of a woman, while the corpse
itself is decorated with the regalia of earthly honours, that of a mighty
warrior having the red goat's hair plume of the head-taker, that of a
holder of the Feasts of Merit having the headdress of tail feathers of the
racket-tailed drongo. These activities are necessary because the spirit
is recalled from the Land of the Dead on the last day of the mourning
ceremonies to join in the final beer-drink, and it would be gravely dis-
pleased if it found that due honour had not been paid to its earthly
shell.

The first sacrifice in the statement, the Nau rai, or Child's Sacri-
fice, approximates to a baptismal ceremony as the invocation introduces
the Child to the notice of the spirits and requests their guardianship
of it by day and by night. Following this, usually on the first sign of
indisposition of the child, come the khlan luh and the pipu luh, two
specifically ancestor-appeasing sacrifices, the one for deceased ancestors
and the other for surviving grandparents. These two sacrifices must
not be confused with the various magical ceremonies carried out with
the definite object of curing some particular illness. All such take the
form of "ransoming" the spirit of the sick person out of the hands
of the huai and hring by offering sacrifices and small mud models of
mithan etc. The khlan luh and the pipu luh are performed only when
the puithiam, or diviner, finds that it is the anger of the ancestors, and
particularly of the grandparents, which is causing the trouble. Unlike
the house sacrifices, these have no time-correlation to the sacrifices at
the Feasts of Merit.
Later in life, when divination of sorcery demands it, the hring
dauh and the uwi hring sacrifices (to entertain malignant spirits) are
performed, the first giving protection against sorcery or cursing emana-
ting from the village of residence, and the second against world-wide
dangers of this nature.
The first khla kawh ceremony comes when a child is two or three
years old, after which it occurs annually. It is designed to call back.
the spirit when divination shows that it is wandering and so causing
sickness to the child or adult. In later years, after a man has given
two mithan Feasts of Merit, he must perform khla kawh with a large
pig once every six years, or if he holds Feasts of Merit at shorter
intervals, once between each feast.

Lastly there is the sacrifice to khal to avert death by accident, a
contingency which I have shown would result in the segregation of
the deceased's ghost from its more normal fellows in the Land of the
Dead. This sacrifice takes place at adolescence in the case of a boy,
and soon after marriage in the case of a girl.
After a man has set up separate house for himself, the series of
house sacrifices commences with the Nutar vai, or offering to the Old
Woman, Tha Nem, who is regarded as the special protector of several
sub-tribes of the Shimhrin group, including the Zahau. In this series,
which have many details of ritual in common, and which follow each
other in a set sequence, there are nine ceremonies. The first four are
called vawkparai because a male pig (vawk pa) is killed, and the next
two vawknu rai, because a sow is the victim. After these come three
sacrifices, to the spirit Van Tsung, who lives in the sky, to the spirit
of the Rih Lake, and to the spirit of the mual, or communal altar oi
the village. The last of the series is a purification ceremony for the
house.
In the statement I have grouped together three pairs. This
requires some explanation. It refers to the fact that after holding the
Feasts of Merit up to and including the Sia hra thah, the householder
must kill a mithan instead of a pig in the first sacrifice to Nutar and
Khawzing respectively, following this up with a pig sacrifice in the
new moon. The two are considered by the Chins as halves of the
same sacrifice for purpose of the series. The whole series takes, in
theory, six years to complete, but if in the meantime the householder
continues with his Feasts of Merit, he must speed up these sacrifices
accordingly, completing a series between each of the mithan feasts.
I have recorded below specimen invocations from two of these
sacrifices, to illustrate the fact that they are for general prosperity
and not only to ensure the health of the family. It is this feeling that
they act as an insurance against harmful influences that creates the
link between them and the Feasts of Merit. They must be interposed
between each of the major feasts because it is believed that the social
progress made through feast-giving renders a man a more obvious
target for the envy and ill-will of both man and ghost.

There is a basic similarity in the ritual of all sacrifices, that is to
say, the material objects used are identical for all categories, variation
in use occurring in accordance with the species of the offering. For
instance, whenever a mithan is killed, a large vawrh bing mug is used
to hold the beer for the libations, and the fatal thrust is given with a
spear when a pig is killed the beer container is a haituk gourd, and
a bamboo spike is the instrument of death: when a fowl is killed it is
beaten on the back with a hoe-handle or similar shaped piece of wood,
and the simple haihrek spoon is used to pour the beer. In all cases
the zu is poured over the creature offered while the spirits are being
invoked, the invocation being repeated when, after the flesh of the
offering has been cooked, the sa serh or spirit food is laid on the altar.
The sa ser consists of a small piece from each of the recognised
"joints" and vital organs, with which is mixed a little cooked millet.
Places of communal sacrifice can be identified by the presence of
the altar, which consists of four flat stones, one laid horizontally and
the other three forming a low wall round the back and sides, and the
sacred cooking stones, of which the most famous are the kualung thu
or Village Stones of Sunkbla, the mother village of the whole Shimhrin
group, about which many quaint legends are told.
One such place of sacrifice, the village mual, is usually opposite
the headman's house (Zahau headmen generally being also the village
priest) in an open space shaded by a large banyan tree. Others will
hnar.
be found at each mar, or resting place on the main roads entering the
village, at which war and hunting parties are met and regaled with beer
on their return from successful expeditions. In the various fields into
which the village lands are divided similar altars are reserved for the
agricultural sacrifices, and so on.
For the personal and household sacrifices held in the house and
courtyard there is no permanent altar, the procedure being to lay a
flat stone on the ground at the spot where custom demands that an
offering shall take place, and to put the spirit food on that.
Certain prohibitions protect all places of sacrifice from defilement.
It is forbidden to tie up any cloven hoofed animal in the immediate
vicinity, and also to cook food at the sacred fireplace. The punishment
for breach of these tabus is, in the first case, the confiscation and
sacrifice of the animal tied there, and in the second case, the payment
of compensation of equivalent value of the usual sacrificial offering
made at the defiled altar.
All animals killed must be free from blemish, that is to say, in the
case of mithan they must be absolutely black above the knee-joints, and
have no broken horns, hooves, or teeth, no open sores or torn ears or

So much for what can be called the regular religious sacrifices. I
will not deal with any of the communal or agricultural sacrifices because
they would require too much space, those already outlined will serve
the purpose of this article.
I can now pass on to the other than "religious " occasions at which
sacrifices occur, that is, at the Feasts of Celebration of martial and hunting
success, and at the Feasts of Merit. These must be studied in quite a
different light, because it is through them that a man achieves social
advancement in this world and the next. In other words the sacrifices
already described are carried out for fear of consequences which follow
sins of omission in this respect, while the ones I am about to describe
are the subject of earnest striving and effort because of the material
and spiritual advantages that accrue.
Interesting side-lights on religious ideology are to be found in study.
ing these feasts. The ral aih, as the dance in celebration of a head.
taking is called, is never seen nowadays for the obvious reason that
killing is forbidden by law, but the belief that the holding of this feast is
still an essential qualification for admission to Paradise dies hard. One
hears arguments to the ellect that the spirits must know that it is now
beyond the reasonable expectations of a Chin to take a head, and that
they will therefore no longer insist on this condition being fulfilled. At
the same time, when in the past wars have occurred and Chin sepoys
have been used on military duty, one or two heads found their way to
the Hills by parcel post! When this practice was discovered and rigo-
rously punished, the hair of dead enemies was substituted instead, until
in the end the only means of stopping the business was to forbid the
holding of any dance resembling the rat ath.
Similarly in the case of game anima's now extinct in the hills, such
as elephant and bison, the local shikaris comfort themselves with the
thought that in this case also the spirit will not demand the impossible.
But this does not prevent those who can afford to do so from taking our
game licences and going out to hunt these coveted animals in the forest
reserves in the plains of the Chindwin valley.

animal is not much good for anything except proving hunting skill, the

In the case of tiger, a mithan is required for the sacrifices, and it is
considered unseemly for any person who has not completed the Siahra
thah Feast of Merit to hold the Pawpi vui (Tiger's burial). Thus any
young man killing a tiger would hand the carcase over to his father
or other senior patrilineal relative to hold the feast, and if these
relatives were not wealthy enough to do so, he would give the honour to
the headman of the village.
Feasts of celebration are held when any of the following species are
killed-elephant, bison, tsaing, hog deer, sambhar, barking deer,
pig, gural, serow, tiger, leopard, the muvanlia eagle, the Paradise
Flycatcher, and a snake called rul ngan. The finding of a nest of khuai
ngal hornets is also celebrated, as it is believed that these will later
come and protect the corpse of their ravisher on his death.
In all cases it is believed that holding of the feast will result in an
addition to the spiritual following of the successful man when his ghost
meets its challenger on the Wrestling Yard of the Guardian Spirit. On
this grim journey he will be preceded by the ghosts of all animals killed
in chase or sacrifice, of all enemies killed in war, and (if they should
predecease him) of all the virgins he succeeded in seducing in life-a
cavalcade of tribute to his earthly glory and evidence of his rightful
place in Heaven.
Finally there is the series of the Feasts of Merit. Commencing
with the insignificant sarual, there follow the progressively expensive
feasts of khlailam, siathum thah, siahra thah, and khuangtsawi. At the
last of these three mithan and three large pigs are killed, representing
something like Rs. 250 in cash. Taking into consideration the series of
household sacrifices that have to be interspersed between each of the
major feasts, it is not surprising that few men get as far as the
khuangtsawi. But a majority will reach the siathum thah, and certainly
everyone will hold the sarual. What usually happens is that a man who
cannot collect enough wealth to buy mithan or large pigs will keep on
repeating the sarual as often as possible, thereby keeping in with his
flesh-due receiving relatives, and getting a nose in front of his still
poorer neighbours.

I have shown that earthly status is believed to determine spiritual
status, and that the feasts are the means of improving earthly positions.
A description of the detail of increasing social prestige will occupy the
remainder of this article.
Everything connected with the feasts is thoroughly institutionalised.
The drinking of the liquor supplied by the host for guests proceeds in
strict order of precedence in feast-giving, even the method of serving
the beer being so organised as to make this possible, that is, the pots
of beer are set in a row in order of quality, each having a tube stuck
into the centre of the brew, through which each man can suck up his
quota. By this means the greatest men can be given the best liquor.
For the hoi polloi, the poorer beer is siphoned off into large tubs, and
handed round in mugs or drinking horns.
The division of the meat of animals killed (those interested will
find futher details in the J. R. A. I. Vol. LXVII. pages 15 to 31)
deserves the closest study, because in this one finds many clues to
the integrative forces of society, not only affecting the kinship groups,
but embracing the whole of the community. The diagram in Plate II
gives some indication of the proportions allotted to different groups at
a typical distribution, that of the pig flesh at khlailam.
There is a very clearly defined system of mutual assistance at the
feasts: brothers, classificatory sisters, mother's brother, wife's brother,
and best friend (rual) must all help in labour or kind in return for
for specific portions of the flesh of sacrificial victims, and other rewards.
Practically no meat remains to be eaten at the feast except smail slices.
cut off each joint given away, these being dried and served as appetisers
with the beer. The hausa pawi who have given feasts divide the meat
set aside for them in strict order of precedence in feast-giving, so that
it always pays to try and get through the series quickly and thus keep
ahead of one's competitors in this sphere.
There are many instance among the Chins of the Falam subdivision
of the meat division system being used as a means of repayment of a com
munal debt or obligation. Now and again, when a village has been
fined and one man has put up the whole of the money, he has in exchange
been given the right to "eat", in perpetuity through his descendants,
a certain piece of meat that would otherwise have gone to the hausa
pawl. Again elders who have represented the village on any particular-
ly dangerous mission, such as going down to the sorcery-ridden plains
for Government Durbars, where they are likely to be attacked by the
evil spirits of the lowlands, have been rewarded by flesh dues, and
so on.

On reaching a certain level among the Feasts of Merit a level which
varies from tribe to tribe, and sometimes from village to village, a man
becomes eligible for election to the village council, thereby gaining
access to a still wider range of perquisites such as the sharing of meat
killed to entertain village guests, or at feasts held out of the proceeds
of fines levied on offenders against custom, or at village communal
sacrifices, etc. Also, as the statement shows, progress in the series
entitles a man to wear a distinctive blanket and ornaments on days of
high ceremony, and completion of it gives the right to build a khlam
hut on his courtyard verandah, and to make windows in his otherwise
gloomy house.
The result of all the benefits accruing to feast giving is an easily
observable keenness to produce enough material wealth to permit of
this luxury: unquestionably the feasts are a very important incentive to
productive activity. The institution of feasting enables the headman,
blacksmith, and sometimes the priest to collect a reasonable return for
their labours without imposing too great a strain on the poor, the flesh
dues representing, in effect, a form of differential taxation. Over and
above the easily observable advantages of feast giving, there are more
obscure resuits such as an increased "migration value. "
By this I
mean that the holder, by virtue of the promises made to the spirits
during the invocations, becomes a potential giver of further feasts, and
as such is a highly desirable immigrant to any village community. If
therefore a quarrel with his headman or relatives or
relatives or some such
calamity makes a move desirable, neighbouring villages will be only too
glad to receive him and transport his goods and chattels free of charge.
In the field of recreation and amusement, the Feasts of Celebration
and of Merit stand pre-eminent. They are the sole means of organised
enjoyment known to the adult Zahau, but as a study of that aspect alone
would fill another article, I will have to let it pass for the moment and
draw together the threads of my arguments.
The following points can be emphasised :--
1. That the religious belief carrying most weight in moulding
the daily life of the people is that in the life after death the spirits
retain the social rank and wealth that they achieved on earth.
2. That this belief, working through the institutionalised system
of social advancement by the Feasts of Celebration and Merit, which
reciprocal obligations in the kinship and
in turn forms the basis of the
social groups, plays an important part in social integration.
3. That by stimulating and regulating consumption of local
products, sacrifice has a deep beating on the whole economic system in

This summary gives some indication of the factors conditioning
the values placed by the Zahau on his various ritual activities and cus-
tomary usages. There are many ways in which it is possible to arrange
for the payment of dues to headmen, fees to specialists, obligations to
relatives and so on, and it seems to me that the clue to an understanding
of why this particular system prevails among the Zahau may lie in
their religious ideology.
The necessity of disposing quickly of the perishable flesh of animals
too large to be eaten by one family, the fact that mithan are difficult
to herd, the uncertainty of the future in the pre-annexation days of
slave-raiding and head-hunting: all these would have their part in the
formulation of the system of economic exchanges, but I think it cannot
be denied that the local conception of Heaven plays an equally import-
ant role in determining this issue.
Nowadays Christianity, Medicine, Education, recruitment for the
army, and the introduction of a monetary system and foreign trade
goods are all in their several ways assaulting the citadel of animism
in the hills, and with it, the stability of local society. The Pau Chun
Hau cult and other strange phenomena of selective assimilation are
already to be seen.
The further development of this process of absorption of foreign
cultural elements can only be gauged accurately from one standpoint-
that of a thorough understanding of the forces at work.
For those in charge of primitive peoples, one can hardly over.
estimate the, shall I say, analysability of the primitive's ideas on the
justice and wisdom of reforms and innovations. Only by believing that
that formidable mystery usually called "primitive conservatism" is
divisible into more easily understandable components can we maintain
our faith in our abilty to plan for the future.
Personally think that modern Social Anthropology offers the
"Man on the Spot" in primitive territories a unique mental instrument
for the dissection of difficult problems of this nature, and if this article
really has conveyed the impression that the relation between the re-
ligious ideology and the other aspects of Zahau culture is susceptible
of reasoned analysis, part of the debt which I owe to Anthropology
will have been repaid.
