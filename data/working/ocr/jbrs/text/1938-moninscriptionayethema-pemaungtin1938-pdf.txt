A Mon Inscription by Kyanzittha at Ayetthema Hill
In April 1936 I paid my first visit on a holiday to Ayetthema Hill
on the shore of the Gulf of Martaban, two and half miles between the
Stations of Mayangon and Taungsun, 100 miles from Rangoon on the
Moulmein railwayline. To the south at a distance of about 4 miles.
across low land by the shore is a higher hill on which stands the
Myathabeit or Kyaungtaya Pagoda. To the east about 2 miles from
Myathabeit and 5 miles from Ayetthema and connected almost un-
interruptedly by a range with both lie the twin hills Kelatha (Kelasa)
and Kelathapha (Kelasabha), which must be over a thousand feet high.
Ayetthema Hill is about the height of the Shwe Dagon Hill and is
now the abode of a pious monk U Kawthanla whose special function is
the reciting of Pali Scriptures every night. Ruined pagodas and piles
of old bricks along the ridge of the hill give evidence of the existence
of an ancient town which has passed away. The Nan-U Pagoda (Palace-
front Pagoda) on the north east side of the Hill is still well preserved.
My attention was particularly drawn to a neglected pagoda on the
west side of the Hill about 200 feet high close to the shore. It is of the
type of the Shwe Dagon built on an octagonal base. From enquiries
made of several old villagers, I learnt that there was once a big
inscription stone inscribed in the Mon language standing on the plat-
form; that one summer day more than 50 years ago it was dug up by
coolies under a government official and taken away in a barge towed
by a launch; and that the villagers, young and old, men and women,
after protesting in vain against this act of descrecation appealed to the
Nats and wailing and shouting, beating their breasts and wringing
their hands, they followed the inscription stone on its way to the shore
as they would follow a funeral. When I asked my informants to what
place it was taken away, one man said that he believed it was taken
to the Rangoon Zoo. Another man, nephew of the man who was
village headman at the time of the incident said that whenever he
visited Rangoon he would, after doing obeisance at the Shwe Dagon, go
to the Zoo, where he saw an inscription stone which he believed was
the one taken away from his native hill.
Now the mention of Rangoon Zoo set my mind a thinking. The
present site of the Rangoon General Hospital was once occupied by the
Zoo and the Phayre Museum. And what the Ayetthema villagers
called the Zoo was also the museum where the Inscription stone would
be placed. This clue made me think of the old Mon Inscriptions which

to prove this conclusively by referring to an old Note by the late Mr.
Taw Sein Ko, on a Archæological Tour through Ramannadesa in
December 1891 and published in the Indian Antiquary. I remembered
having read this note years ago. On studying this Note again I found
this statement on page 7 "There used to be a Talaing inscription near
Ayetthema, but it was removed to the Phayre Museum at Rangoon
about eight years ago." The inscription stone now stands in the
University Library, Rangoon labelled "Place of origin unknown." The
contents of the first part of the inscription giving Kyanzittha's legend-
ary career are the same as those of Kyanzittha's Myakan Inscription
at Pagan. The latter part contains the date 1098 A.D, the year in
which Kyanzittha restored the pagoda, styled Kyāk Talan. The name
by which it is known now is Mya Thein Tan. It is interesting to find
an Old Mon inscription by Kyanzittha in the heart of the Mon Country.
Both the late Dr. Forchhammer and Mr. Taw Sein Ko identified
Ayetthema with the ancient Taikkulā and the Golamattikanagara of
the Kalyāņi Inscriptions, set up at Pegu by King Dhammaceti in 1476
A.D. The description given in the Inscriptions supports this identifi-
cation: "At that time a King, called Sirimasoka, ruled over the country
of Suvannabhumi. His capital was situated to the North west of the
Keläsabhapabbata cetiya. The eastern half of this town was situated on
an upland plateau, while the western half was on plain country. This
town is called, to this day, Goļamattikanagara, because it contains many
mud-and-wattle houses resembling those of the Gola people. The town
was situated on the sea shore" (Kalyāņi Inscriptions, translated by
Taw Sein Ko, Government Press Rangoon 1892-p. 48). As has been
pointed out by Dr. Blagden in his translation of the Mon text of the
Kalyani Inscriptions in Epigraphia Birmanica Vol. III part II p. 185,

Myathabeit Hill on the northwest side. And I have in my possession a
