BIRDS OF BURMA. By B. E. Smythies. Edited by H. C. Smith and
P. F. Garthwaite. With 31 full-page coloured plates by Lieut. Commander
A. M. Hughes, R.N. xxix, 589 pp. Map. 1940. A. B. M. Press, Rangoon.
Rs. 15.
I have been asked to write a review of the Birds of Burma and though
an amateur's criticism is bound to suffer from 'subjectivity' I take
great pleasure in doing so, as representing one of the great majority to whom
the book will appeal, that is the people who want, as the Introduction says ‘a
concise and accurate account of the birds of Burma written in non-technical
language,' with above all the coloured pictures that are what the beginner
needs.' I feel also under a personal obligation to the public spirited persons
who brought this book out for an added enjoyment of the compound, of the
evening walk, and of those many junglefowl beats where all the birds of the
jungle (except junglefowl) seem to come and perch in front of one's house.
What greater tribute to the book can be paid than to admit that it has already
saved from death or at least from a bad fright more than one junglefowl by
diverting the shooter's attention to birds of other species.
Till this book came out the ordinary amateur, who liked to see and to
put a name to what he saw, was under considerable difficulties. The only real
authority was the 6 volumes of the Fauna of British India dealing with birds,
which no one but an expert was likely to carry round with him, and still less
to find his way about. Of course there was Oates' Birds of British Burma,
2 volumes published in 1883 which, though hardly 'a concise account written
in non-technical language,' was excellent as far as Oates' own experience went.
But it did not extend north of Thayetmyo, and the nomenclature is rather out
of date. Still with the aid of that eked out by the ingenious system of identi-
fication by colours, sizes, peculiarities and habits in Dewar's Key to the
Common Birds of the Plains of India it was possible for a beginner to make
a good deal of progress. Then appeared Whistler's Popular Handbook of
Indian Birds, which was nearly what one wanted but was unfortunately not
quite Burma. This introduced coloured pictures - just enough of them to
be exasperating.
-
But the Birds of Burma gives all one can reasonably ask. To begin with
there are the pictures, which are what most of us really want. It would be
difficult to imagine more perfect pictures of birds than Commander Hughes'
original water colours, and they have been well reproduced. 290 pictures
doesn't sound a large proportion of the 1235 birds dealt with in the book,
but they cover a surprising number of the birds that catch the eye.
In a Rangoon garden with assistance I identified some 35 birds: I
have since found a picture of every one of these in the book. An
Upper Burma dry zone garden has since added another half dozen, and

-
And coming to the descriptions, these are of course much simplified by
the numerous pictures, but they are good and simple. That was where a book
like Oates was so terrifying. Unless a bird is very conspicuous one doesn't
see it well, and even with the aid of glasses the incorrigible habit of fidgetting
that so many birds seem to have makes it difficult to isolate distinctive colours
without infinite patience and leisure. Eventually one runs - or thinks one
runs some distinctive marking to earth and tries Oates. Take for instance
the common little white-throated babbler, the swe of the dry zone. The des-
cription in Oates is 'Forehead and a line on either side continued up to the
eye grey, each feather with a blackish stripe down the centre; top of head,
neck and back with the scapulars ruddy brown, each feather with a dark
brown shaft stripe; rump olive brown, and the upper tail coverts the same,
with indistinct shaft stripes; wings and wing coverts olive brown, some of the
greater coverts indistinctly dark shafted; ear coverts and sides of neck ruddy
brown; lores black; chin, throat, cheeks, and upper breast white; remainder
of lower plumage ruddy brown; tail olive brown, with numerous cross bars
of darker brown.' But short of shooting a specimen one doesn't see all
this; what one sees is 'an untidy brown bird with a long moth-eaten tail
and a white throat and breast,' exactly as the Birds of Burma says.
Summaries of the characteristics of many of the Families and Sub
Families are given which help put one on the right track. A gallant attempt
has been made to reproduce the calls and songs in Roman characters, which
is probably as successful as such an attempt can be. It was however a dis-
appointment to find the call of the Rains Quail reproduced as whit whit'
rather than 'tweet tweet,' being convinced that this and not the canary (pace
Animal Grab') is the only bird that really says 'tweet tweet'.
"
f
Of the more scientific aspect of the book I am not qualified to speak,
but the care and patience which has gone into the preparation of the systematic
hand-list at the end are obvious. Page xiii of the Introduction has a com-
plete answer to any cavaller at the number of birds included in the book.
The whole delightfully written Introduction in fact is very readable and sets
out the case for the book admirably. It freely admits too the difficulties of
the amateur observer. What one sees, even with glasses, is so very different
from a 'bird in the hand.'

A word as to Burmese names. The introduction points out that only 162
Burmese names have been recorded for the 1235 birds in the book, and that
most of these (108 to be exact) were recorded by Oates over half a century
ago, and suggests that the coining of suitable names for birds at present with-
out them would be a very useful piece of work. Considering the era and
conditions, Oates' collection of names, most of which still hold good, was a
remarkable piece of work; local enquiry will certainly produce some more,
but probably not a great many. The last 50 odd years have only added 50
odd Burmese names to Oates' list though many Kachin names have been
added from Myitkyina while enquiry up the Chindwin would probably pro-
duce Chin and Naga names. Nor is it quite a fair criticism to say that there
are only 162 Burmese names for 1235 species or to complain, as the summary
under Woodpeckers does, that whereas there are in Burma no fewer than 40
different kinds of Woodpecker, each with a distinct English and scientific
name, in Burmese all are apparently known as thit-tauk-hnget. After all the
only English local names are Woodpecker, and for one particular variety the
onomatopoeic Yaffle'. In the same way there is only one genuine English
local group name for all species of tern, pheasant, laughing thrush, and
many others, each of which has a Burmese group name (zinyaw, yit,
wayaung-hnget). Most if not all the other English names must have been
invented by scientists to distinguish the species. The cultivator who
is only familiar with one or
or two species doesn't need these distinc-
tions. The invention of similar names for the species in Burmese would
present no great difficulty. Principal Pe Maung Tin has already done some
work on these lines in connexion with naming both birds and animals
at the zoo. But it would be an advantage if it could be done by Burmans
acquainted with the birds in nature, so that they could avoid too slavish a
translation of the English name, which is not always a good description of
the particular species.
