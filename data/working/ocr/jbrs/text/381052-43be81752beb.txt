SEASONAL FLUCTUATIONS IN THE BURMESE
ECONOMY'
by
R. M. Sundrum, B. A., B. L., Ph. D. 2
( 1 ) Background of the Investigation
This paper may be considered as an interim report on one line of
research being conducted by the Economics Research Project of the
University of Rangoon. This line of research is concerned with the
compilation of a Business Indicator for the Burmese economy. A
business indicator to be a useful measure has to reflect sensitively even
fairly small changes in the economy and has therefore got to be based
on time series which are themselves reliable in the sense of portraying
accurately and promptly significant changes in the factors influencing
business activity. As a preliminary, therefore, to the construction of
an Index of Business Activity, it was thought desirable to collect a
number of relevant time series and subject them to some sort of check
on the question of their overall accuracy, and the promptness of their
publication. One aspect of this check is to study the story they tell of
the seasonal fluctuations in the economy, verifying the results of analy-
sing each time series both with general impressions of academic students
members of the business community and officials of the administration
who understand the seasonal rhythm in the motion of our economy,
and in regard to the mutual consistency of the various time series.
Hence the present investigation.
(2) Method of Analysis
The main statistical technique used in the present investigation to
isolate seasonal movements from broader changes in the economy has
been a 12-month moving average centred at calendar months to permit
of comparisons; ratios to such a moving average were computed, and
averaged over as many post-war years as statistical data permitted, and
then adjusted to have a mean over the year of 100.

This is usually considered one of the most satisfactory methods of
isolating purely seasonal factors. It was not felt useful to go in for
greater refinements that were theoretically possible but not advisable in
this preliminary study. However, we must mention straight away a
number of weaknesses of the method. If the present method is applied
to any series whatsoever, whether it is sensibly affected by seasonal
fluctuations or not, we will end up with seasonal indices which will
generally show some departures from annual averages, and the impor-
tant theoretical question arises whether they do indicate a definite
seasonal pattern, or whether they should be considered, from a statisti-
cal point of view, as mere sampling effects. This question might have
been settled in each case by an appeal to analysis of variance or other
tests of seasonality, but again it was felt that the assumptions to be
fulfilled in such analyses would not be justified in the present case
and a full theoretical test had to be postponed. Instead, some remarks
are appended in the appropriate places as to the intensity of the
seasonal effects revealed by the moving average method.
As explained before, the study is mostly confined to the post-war
period from 1946 onwards. Most of our series have thus been very
short, and too much reliance should not therefore be placed on the
final results. In addition to the shortness of the time series on which
we are operating, these years have been disturbed times and perhaps
our method of analysis tends somewhat to exaggerate the abnormal
- situations in some years over a much longer period and thus to distort
the picture somewhat. This is to be seen particularly with data relating
to the Burma Railway earnings.
In spite of these limitations of our sources of information and our
method of analysis, it is felt that the final picture that is revealed of
the seasonal movement of the Burmese economy is interesting, and
useful in indicating, however roughly, the direction of changes, even
if the quantitative effects may not be too reliable. The research
workers on this project have been only too well aware that perhaps
after all they may not be producing information that is not already
well known among the people for whom this is meant. Still, our
business community is young and particularly the present tendency of
the administration to guide and control the business world somewhat
inexperienced and it is hoped that they might possibly benefiit from
some of the conclusions which we have ventured to draw in the
following pages.

(3) Prices
We begin with a study of price movements. Two important price
series were used, namely:
-
I (a) Wholesale Price Index of Agricultural products published
in the Quarterly Bulletin of Statistics (hereafter referred to
as Q. B. S.) from June 1950 to June 1954.
I (b) Consumers' Price Index (including Rent) for a Burmese
family, prepared by the Directorate of Labour, from
January 1947 to May 1954.
The wholesale Agricultural Price Index shows a smooth movement,
high from June to December, and low from January to May. This is
perhaps only to be expected, though the period of high prices lasts
perhaps a little too long. The Index is an unweighted average of prices
of 5 groups of article, cereals, pulses, oilseeds, other foodstuffs and
non-foods. In view of the overwhelming predominance of the cereals
group, this method tends to underweight the prices of that group.
The Consumers' Price Index tends to follow roughly the same
movement, though its high and low periods tend to occur a little earlier
than those of the Wholesale Agricultural Price Index. It is natural
that agricultural prices dominate the movement of the Consumers'

Secondly, imports and exports appear to be concentrated in different
parts of the year. It is interesting however to note that the dip of
both indices in the month of April continues in this period, though
much less noticeably for imports.
One consequence of the fact that imports do not any longer
follow the same pattern of seasonal fluctuation as exports is that
Burma tends to suffer from less effective utilisation of shipping space.
This is already aggravated by the fact that our imports are of the
compact type of goods, whereas our exports are of the bulky type, a
factor which led even in pre-war times, to the fact that 20 per cent of
incoming merchant shipping was in ballast, while only 9 per cent of
outgoing merchant shipping was in ballast. The position regarding the
post-war period is not so clear. It appears that the percentage in
ballast of incoming shipping has been consistently higher, whereas the
percentage for outgoing shipping has varied about the pre-war figure.
The fact that imports tend to pack towards the end of the calendar
year is also reflected in the pattern of our current receipts and
expenditures of foreign exchange. For a number of post-war years,
large deficits in current transactions tend to occur about September or
October, so that exchange reserves earned in the early part of the year
from our exports have to be tied up for a rather long time to meet the
large imports of the latter part of the year.
It is interesting to speculate on the causes of this change in our
trading practice. The most significant fact, of course, is that the post
war period has been one of fairly stringent trade control. However, it
still remains to be explained why such shifts have occured, especially in
the pattern of our imports. Although the data on which the present
analysis is based are not entirely adequate for firm conclusions, the
facts thrown up here must be taken into account in any appraisal of
this period of trade control and in any future reformulation of such
trade policies with a view to promoting economic use of shipping space
and of our exchange reserves.

On examination, the statistics obtained regarding the earnings of
the I. W. T. are found to be unsuitable for our present purpose.
peak in March is reliable and the dip in April is explained as being due
to the conditions of the tides during that month. Apart from that, the
other indices are either unreliable statistically, or the data on which
they arise are not satisfactory. For example, the peak in September
looks suspiciously like the consequences of the closing of accounts at
the end of the fiscal year, and therefore not to be taken too seriously
for our present purpose.
On the other hand, for the periods tudied, the seasonal pattern of
the railway earnings seems fairly stable. It is interesting when compar-
ing this with the indices for Exports of the country, III (b), to find a
very close correspondence. The obvious explanation is that the Burma
Railway activity is chiefly influenced by the conditions relating to the
export trade. The relative importance of the Railways in the handling
of the rice export is indicated by the fact that in 1950-51, Railways
handled 27.5 per cent of the total volume exported, and that in 1951-52,
this proportion went up to 36.50 percent.
On the other hand, we may draw the conclusion that domestic
trade does not seem to enter very significantly into railway activity.
This is interesting from the point of view of Transport Policy, in a
developing economy, for it would be desirable for improvement in
transport measures to play a really effective part in the development of
the domestic market for local produce.

the banking system, possibilities of credit creation lie unused for quite
the major part of the year; the magnitude of this sort of idle credit
potential may be indicated by the fact that in 1953, for instance, the
difference between the seasonal high and low figures amounted to
K. 70 millions. The big problem is how this may be diverted partly at
least to finance the cultivator's seasonal needs.
The indices for Notes and Coins in circulation are based on a very
short series, but they would seem to be one of the most regular forms.
of seasonal variations in the whole group which we have been studying.
Variations in the volume of this form of money supply are registered
by withdrawals and returns in the accounts of the Union Bank of
Burma, but ultimately they represent the extra money which is taken
out of the banking system in order to purchase the annual rice crop
from the agricultural sector. While increased withdrawls from the
banking system early in the year can be expected to follow a fairly
regular pattern year after year, owing to the normal practice in the
assemblage of the rice crop, it is surprising to note that the amounts
returned to the banking system also follow a regular pattern. The
conclusion would seem to be that the rural population engaged in the
agricultural sector also follows a fairly rigid time schedule in their
economic dealings with the rest of the economy.
The indices of Bank clearings are interesting. One would expect
them to indicate more closely than any other series the variation of
business activity in the urban centres. Each index in our series is an
average of at least five years' figures and can therefore be expected to
be fairly reliable. Still, we cannot find any simple explanation of the
variations in this series on the basis of the other series we have studied
so far. The irregularities in the early part of the calendar year
appear to reflect the balance between the lull in export trade on the one
hand and the briskness of import trade which in post-war years tends
to occur in that period; there is the same dip in the month of April,
which we have noticed elsewhere. In the later part of the year, this
series follows the series for Notes and Coins, and presumably
reflects the deflationary effect of the banks taking back the money
supply. In the middle of the year however, clearings, show a high
level. Perhaps the most important influence on this is the scale of
Government expenditures, which tends to be high in this period. The

(7) Conclusion
Now, we can permit ourselves some general reflections on the
seasonal movement of our economy. We have tried to bring together
some of the most important economic time series available. The most
striking feature of our present analysis is that all these series have a
large seasonal variation which seems ultimately to link up with the
agricultural season. The net result of all this may therefore be
summarised in the remark that, over and above the direct share of
agriculture in the national economy, agriculture sets the pace for the
greater part of the economic activities of Burma.
