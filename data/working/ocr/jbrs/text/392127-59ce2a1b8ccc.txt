THE PROCESS OF COMMUNICATION
Essentially, communication is the process whereby a person or
group, says something, in a distinctive way or through a certain medium,
to another person or group, with a desired effect. Facts about this
process can be elicited by asking the five questions, "Who?" "Says
What ?," "To whom?" "Through What Medium ?" and "With
What Effect?" Before discussing printed materials as a communication
medium we shall examine, in general, these five aspects of the
process of comumnication, namely: (i) The Who Says or the Com-
municator; (2) The What? or Content; (3) The To Whom? or
Audience; (4) The Through What Medium? or Channels, and
(5) The Effect, which we shall describe in terms of Principles of Mass
Communication.
Communicator
The message originates somewhere. This is the communicator.
Occasionally, the man who communicates the message is sometimes,
but not always, the originator of the message. Thus, we must ask:
(i) who originates the content?, and (ii) who encodes the content,
sending it toward the intended audience through the selected channels?
At both stages, the message passes a gate-keeper and selection is
unavoidable.
Studies of the communicator in western countries are numerous.
These include the studies of influences behind newspapers, magazines,
and radio networks, and how ownership affects editorial policy. The
control at the source of a communication system is a matter well worth
studying, for it may be that a few people at the top influence the
attitudes of millions.

As Dewey points out, “communication is a process of sharing

A communication program, as with an education program, must be
carefully considered in terms of its aims and objectives. What are the.
people to learn and what are they to do as a result of each individual
message and also over the long stretch of time? Communication is
education, and education is communication. As education is a life-
long process, one experience building upon and modifying the others,
so a communication program must be well planned to build upon and
modify the experience of the intended audience.
It is not enough to direct people to become civic conscious. What
does civic consciousness mean? What should people know and what
should they do in order to become civic conscious? Until a specific.
communication program of this nature is outlined, results will be
akin to that of a shotgun as compared with a carbine. The force at
the outset will be similar but at the time it reaches the target the energy
will be diffused. A communication program must be planned far in
advance, much as a building program must be planned far ahead, for
as the bricks at the top depend on the bricks at the foundation, so the
effect of communication are cumulative, the latter depending on the
forme..
Once the aims and objectives and specific aspects of content are
outlined, presentation must be considered carefully in terms of the
audience. As we have often heard, it is sometimes not so much what

Audience
Much of what is said through communication channels is so
remote from the life of the intended audience that the process of
sharing experience breaks down. Kurt Lewin, a leading American
sociologist, has described the individual's existence in terms of
"life space. " 8 The "life space" concept might be visualized in
terms of a series of concentric circles radiating outward from a
central core. At the centre are the interests, needs, abilities, etc., that
are basic and vital to the very existence of the individual.
These core
facets of the individual's existence are those which eminate from his
everyday activity, from his struggle with problems of everyday life.
The circles more distant from the centre are those that relate to the
peripheral of the individual, areas of activity and of knowledge which
don't affect him directly but which may interest him as a member of
society as a whole.
-
Others have pointed out that the individual is first and foremost
just that an individual. He is a selfish creature and is interested in
himself and in his immediate need as an individual. His second
stage of interest and need relates to his family and his third area of
need relates to his relationship with his immediate community. Fourth
he is interested in the country as a whole, and only fifth and last is he
interested in world developments and problems so remote that he will
never have much effect on them one way or another. It is true that
world events affect each and every individual immensely and it is true
that in highly literate societies, world events often rate high public
interest, but such interest is always quite secondary to the more
immediate and pressing considerations relating to individual and
family problems.

Channel
The medium or the channel used in sending ideas to the intended
audience are significant from several points of view. First some ideas
can best be presented through certain channels because of their nature
or because of their immediacy. Announcements of impending danger,
for instance, are best made over the radio, through the newspaper, or
through a combination of channels, certainly very rarely through
books. Description of skill techniques are usually best presented in
the form of illustrated books, motion pictures, or other visual media
and less frequently on the radio where words are the medium and where
the listener can never turn back the pages or ask questions.

Animals communicate by using signs to serve a pointing-out function.
When a dog barks and gets food, then he gradually associates barking

regulating your own action. Your stimulus to the other person becomes

are two-way.
It is man's ability to symbolize that enables him to re-arrange

Principles of Mass Communication
Since communication is a process involving several stages, it is
necessary, as has been seen above, to break research down in terms of
specifically what portion of the process you wish to study. Thus a
study of the who in our "Who Says What to Whom " structure
involves control analysis. Illuminating studies have shown the
influence of gate-keepers on the final message in news reporting.
The reporter is first gate-keeper. The news-room and the various
editors are the second gate-keepers. Press services as receivers of
nation-wide press releases are further gate-keepers. Truly, the
message that gets to the reader depends a lot on who is talking, and on
who controls the communication channels.
•
A study of the what is content analysis. Content analysis, as
used during the war by a number of countries, helped analyze publica-
tions suspected of being subversive. Various criteria were used,
against which the content of the publications was measured, and if the
content consistently compared favorably with subversive characteristics
then the publication was deemed to be subversive.
Content analysis plays a part in other aspects of experimental
communication research. When attempting to test the effectiveness of
a piece of communication it is usually necessary to do a thorough
content analysis so as to be able to measure the effectiveness of the
various aspects of the message.
A study of the whom of the communication is audience analysis.
Here we have such studies as those of Hooper and Nielson in radio
listening, and readership surveys such as those conducted by the
Continuing Study of Newspaper Readership. Audience analysis can
be in terms of potential or in terms of those actually receiving the
message. That is, a readership survey studies the degree to which
subscribers or purchasers of a newspaper or magazine read the item in
question, while a study of potential audience might be a broad social
survey. In such a survey we would study the characteristics of a target
audience, an audience not yet captured.

A study of the channels used is media analysis. Here a number of

Without going into detail to describe the varied studies in each
of the categories mentioned above, we will list a number of principles
that have been established during the past twenty years, principles, of
mass communication which can be applied to any communication
situation, according to the proposed content, channels used, and target
audience. 13
1. All media are effective; the nature of the material, the
audience and the situation determine the best one to use. However,
face-to-face contact is strongly recommended for definite reinforce
ment of a message sent by other channels.
2. Mass media can get across factual information and can'
influence opinions and attitudes. It is easier, however, to communicate
factual information than it is to change opinions or attitudes.
3. Existing opinions and attitudes can be reinforced, and latent
opinions and attitudes can be made active more easily than can
opinions and attitudes can be changed. Similarly, it is easier to create
new opinions and attitudes than to change existing opinions and
attitudes.
4. Canalization of existing needs, that is, offering new ways of
satisfying existing needs, is much easier than creating new needs
through the communication process. Again, it is necessary to know
much of the intimate life of the intended audience in order to know.
how best to send the intended information.
.
5. Educated and well-informed people prefer both sides of an
issue. Otherwise, a discussion involving all possibilities only tends to
confuse. For less educated audiences, information must be structured
so that the basic points are very clear and are emphasized, and involved
intellectual discussion minimized.

Readability
Even assuming that the publication arrives and is available, the
publication must be easy to read by individual mumbers of the target
audience. If the material requires excessive concentration, or if the
vocabulary is out of the reach of the intended audience, the publication
will have very low readership.
Journalists, authors and educators have long suggested that it is
not difficult to write simple, clear, concise prose that is easy to read
by everyone. Readability and comprehension studies, however, have
shown that many newspaper articles are written at a level several
grades above the average reading ability of the newspaper readers.
Other studies have shown that government prose, business communica-
tion, advertising copy, and so on, is often written at a language level
designed to lose the audience before the audience has ever been captured.

influencing the communication potential of printed material. They do,
however, give a valid index as to reading complexity of the material.
The way the component factors of a readability formula are
isolated by most researchers is to first establish a criterion of difficulty
by having a series of passages read by adults or children of various
levels of education. Respondents are then tested for comprehension
of the passages.

completeness of comprehension as registered by respondents with

however, have found that two factors combined in a multiple regression
equation predict reading difficulty as accurately as a four or five factor

contextual situations and repeated a number of times to ensure mecha-

seven or eight times before it can be assumed that the word will be
subsequently recognized. Similarly, if complete meaning of the word is
to be developed, the word must be carefully couched in contextual
situations which adequately communicate to the reader the various
meaning patterns.

Pre-Testing
Even if general reading interests of a potential audience are known,
it is wise to pre-test individual publications in terms of communication
potential. Such tests allow improvement of the publication before it
is published and also contribute data toward a clearer understanding of
the process of communication through printed material.
The simplest technique of testing communication potential of the
presentation is to mimeograph the material, choose a number of
subjects who are representative of the target audience, and have the
subjects read the mimeographed pre-test copy. The test subjects can
then he questioned as to their reactions, and as to what they under-
stand and remember of what they have read. Absurd recall errors are
often forthcoming from such testing, and it usually is a simple matter
to see where the publication lacks emphasis, or is unclear, or otherwise
needs improvent.
Other pre-test techniques can be applied to title and cover design.
Several cover designs can be placed on a table and representative
subjects asked to choose the one that most attracts them. Similarly,
test subjects can be asked to tell what they think the material is about
on the basis of the cover, and if they would be interested in reading it.

cription-type) presentation. Copies of one booklet will be distributed
to every household of one village, and copies of the other to every
household of a second village.
Interviewers will return after several days to ask what was done
with the booklets. Who read them, who did not, and what was remem-
bered by those who read the booklets. Results will then be studied to
see which style obtained greatest readership, which communicated most
clearly (as evidenced by ideas remembered by those who read the
booklets), and so on. Results will be specifically useful in preparing
the final edition of the booklet, and will also be significant in generaliz-
ing as to type of presentation that is most effective.
Post-Testing
Another method of measuring reading interest is to test actual
magazine, newspaper, or book purchasers and subscribers to see what
portion of the material is read and what portion is not, which ideas
are remembered and which are not. In this way, it is possible to
establish a certain priority of interest in terms of what is read and
what is retained.
The Magazine section of the Burma Translation Society, under
U Htin Gyi, has been experimenting with a questionnaire included in
each magazine asking the reader to check against a list of articles
which appear in the magazine. A respondent may check Yes against
articles he enjoys and No against articles he does not enjoy. The card
is self-addressed and stamped so as to make its return as simple as
possible.
This method again has the advantage in that it is simple and
requires littte staff to administer. However, it is limited, first, in that
respondents are only those who wish to respond. There is thus an
automatic selection. Since only a small number of readers take the

The second reason why such a study lacks validity is that the
respondents may not have read all the articles they check. Many
respondents check all articles on the list as "like" or "dislike" yet
it is probable they do not read every article. Only a personal
interview of an extended nature can determine which articles have been
read by the respondents and thus those articles upon which he is
qualified to comment.
The third limitation of such mail questionnaire studies involving
categories of like and dislike is that the information may be
mistinterpreted since there are many factors in liking and disliking a
piece of printed material. The same article with bold illustrations,
less abstruse style, better organization, and so on, might be checked as
liked by the same person. Liking or disliking of an article, as we have
already suggested, may involve feelings toward the author, inability to
read the text with full comprehension, lack of proper organization and
content, and other factors not dependent on the thematic content itself.
Long term effectivenses of any communication program, of course,
can be measured only by observing changes that occur in the life space
of the audience. Very few studies of this nature can be made by
publishing concerns. Educational groups with workers in the field,
however, might well measure their program as a whole from time to
time in order to determine what changes in opinion, attitude, and
action have taken place as a result of the educational program. Social
surveys are not a one-time operation. Such surveys are needed in the
early stages of a communication or education program to establish the
needs, interests, and patterns of thinking of the intended audience, and
are needed at intervals to measure the success of the program in raising
social, civic, economic and cultural competencies.
SUMMARY
As an art,
Effective communication is both an art and a science.
there are always those intangibles that are never quite predictable. As
a science, there are those recurring phenomena that can be controlled
with adequate application of what we know of principles affecting the
communication process.

RESEARCH ON COMMUNICATION
157
Much basic research underlying various principles of communica-
tion need not be repeated in Burma. We may accept, for instance, the
fact that a message is more effective if it is beamed at the audience
repetitively and through a number of media. We must, however,
ourselves study the relative impact of various media in Burma. No
one, for instance, knows accurately the characteristics of the Burmese
radio audience, or the listenership of specific radio programs. Similarly,
no one knows what people would read if material were available.
Reading material is so scarce and limited in coverage that even figures
on "what people read" would be misleading.
Obviously, much of this needed basic research in Burma is descrip-
tive rather than experimental. We want to know more of the media
and audiences in Burma, and what happens when we send ideas through
these media to each of the various audiences. As we accumulate this
type of descriptive information, we can do more research of experimen-
tal nature, designed to discover the effect on the communication process
of the many variables involved. We can effectuate the high degree of
control necessary when we are testing individual variables, however,
only when we have considerable information as to the nature of the
variables involved.
In terms of printed materials, needed research in Burma must
first be concerned with the potential audience. Who can read what?
Who wants to read what? How can reading interests be motivated?
What is the vocabulary of the target audience? What are the felt needs
and interests of the various audiences?
Literacy statistics available so far in Burma are very misleading.
For example, ability to articulate written Burmese symbols does not
indicate functional literacy. Above illiteracy there are, no doubt, at
least, three levels of literacy; the oral reader who reads symbols aloud
one by one and who rarely reads silently or by phrase; the silent reader
who mentally reads aloud (usually identifiable by noticeable lip move-
ment), and the fully literate person reads silently and by groups of
symbols (an ability which requires extensive practice even in a phonetic
language). How many people in Burma can be classified in the last
category: the only class of literates that is capable of fully profitting
from ungraded reading material of extensive nature?
How many
"literates" belong in the less-than-fluent categories which make
simplified and graded materials necessary?

Second statistics are needed on all aspects of communication
media. Who is saying what to the people of Burma ? How can such
communication programs be coordinated? How can printed materials
be routed in greater quantity to target audiences?
Third, publishers, educators, information officers, and all who use
printed material in their programs must plan their publications as an
educational program. What do they want people to learn and to do
in the long stretch ahead? Subsequently, such printed material must
fit in with other media and field programs, thus achieving the repetitive
impact of which we have spoken.
And finally, all in charge of such communication programs must
be encouraged to evaluate their programs periodically to see what
changes in the target audiences have come about.
Fortunately, in terms of printed materials as a communication
medium, we can report two encouraging developments. The Burma
Translation Society has established a Communication Research Division
under Bo Ba Ko which will undertake audience surveys, readership
studies, pre-test and post-test studies of Society publications, and also
investigate Burmese vocabulary usage and readability. The Mass
Education Council has graciously offered assistance in carrying out
field work.
The second significant development is the Educational Publications
Study Group, an organization made up of representatives of education
and information services of government ministries and departments
and several private publishers. Convened early in 1955 by the Burma
Translation Society as a short-term seminar-type group, the participants
later formed a permanent organization which continues to meet
regularly.
The Study Group has recognized the need for collection of
statistics on book and pamphlet production and on library services in
Burma. Committees are presently studying ways of collecting such
figures and presenting them so that they are meaningful.
Neither the Burma Tranlation Society Research Division or the
Publication Study Group can do all that is needed themselves. As
I have emphasized, communication involves inter-disciplinary skill.
Communication research, then, requires inter-disciplinary cooperation.
It is hoped that this paper will have helped survey needs in this
area. Similarly, it is hoped that this paper will have clarified in a
small way the part that printed materials, as purveyors of ideas, and
more generally, the part that communication as a social process can
play in the long-term development of Burma.
