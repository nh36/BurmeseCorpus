Pig iron or cast iron is an alloy of iron and carbon. Iron con-
tains about four precent carbon. By volume this carbon amounts to
ten or eleven percent. In most of the grey irons used in the foundries
the carbon exists in the form of graphite flakes in the iron matrix. An
examination of a specimen under the microscope reveals that flakes of
graphite run along the iron matrix breaking the continuity of the
metallic matrix. This discontinuity in the iron matrix obviously affects
the strength of the material as the fibres of flake graphite are weak.
As ten percent of the volume is lost to weak graphite the mean tension
owing to a given total stress is increased on each point under stress and
the material fails earlier than it should in its natural course. Besides,
each small flake of graphite acts like a notch in the matrix decreasing
the strength of iron when subjected to load.
This weakness in the structure of grey irons was observed by
metallurgists long ago who found the possibility of improving the grey
irons in strength provided they could by any means disperse the flakes
of graphite, uniformly, into minute spheroids in the matrix. For several
decades they patiently tried to find a means by which they could trans-
form the graphite flakes into uniformly distributed spherulitic nodules
without success.
However success crowned their efforts at last and the first
intimation of a successful process for spheroidisation of graphite in
cast iron was made in the presidential address to the British Cast Iron
Research Association in December 1946 and the first description of the
process was published by Messrs. Morrough and Williams in March
1948. This dealt with the introduction of Metallic Cerium in molten
hypereutectic cast irons. This process was covered by a patent in July
1946 by the British Cast Iron Research Association though the
publication of the cerium process took place in 1948.
The International Mond Nikcle Company of U.S.A. and Canada
obtained patents for promotion of spheroidal graphite irons by treating
hyper-eutectic and hypo-eutectic cast irons with magnesium in March
1947.

The Mond Nickle Companies in India and other countries also

spheroidising the graphite flakes and distributing the graphite into
spherulitic nodules uniformly throughout the matrix. The tensile
strength of the iron has increased as expected from 13 tons to 40 tons

THE CERIUM PROCESS
The cerium process consists in adding Mischmetal to a molten
Hyper-eutectic iron of about 3.8 percent carbon, 2.5 percent silicon
with sulphur 0.02 percent and phosphorus less than 0.1 percent. The
sulphur specification is rather too low and not in the reach of ordinary
cupolas to make. If desulphurisation is attempted the ordinary
temperature of 1400. C. would go down considerably low rendering it
difficult to carry on with the process in the final stage. When cerium
dissolves in the iron it holds the carbon forming a
strong carbide. It retards further graphitisation though it has first
started the formation of the spheroidal nuclei. The addition of an
inoculant is therefore necessary to promote further graphitisation.
Silico manganese, zirconium alloy or ferrosilicon (75 percent silicon) are
used as inoculants to promote the graphitisation after the addition of
cerium. The amount of cerium to be added depended upon the section of
the casting poured; the larger the section of the casting the larger is the
amount added. The amount of cerium added varies from 0.22 to
0.44 percent. The process has not become popular on account of the
difficulty in getting low sulphur iron from our ordinary cupolas as well
as facilities to desulphurise the metal at the temperatures available.
MAGNESIUM PROCESS
Unlike cerium magnesium cannot be added as magnesium metal
since magnesium does not dissolve in the iron. Owing to the
explosive evolution of magnesium vapour which burns generating much
heat and light a violent reaction is caused on the surface of the molten
metal which may be thrown out. Magnesium is therefore dissolved in
molten nickle in which it dissolves quietly and the nickle magnesium
alloy is used as the reacting substance in place of the naked metal. The
normal reacting alloy contains from ten to twenty percent of
magnesium. Like cerium, magnesium is a very strong carbide forming
element which holds the carbon in combination as a carbide and does
not allow the separation of graphite as spheroids though at the initial

silicon enrichment depending upon the kind of matrix needed in the
casting. Owing to the ease in operation as well as the less stringent speci-
fications laid on the metal required for operation, this process is more
popular and has been adopted in several foundries making spheroidal
graphite iron.

We could not get the material we wanted, we tried the magnesium

scrap to lower the phosphorus.

THE MAGNESIUM PROCESS
The Rotary Furnace was installed as per instructions of the makers
and the furnace lined with the lining material the makers had sent. It
was sintered according to the standard procedure and kept ready for
operation.
The rotary furnace was started and heated for about an hour and
good acid slag 60 percent SiO 2 and 10 percent Fe0 and 10 percent Mn0.
and 10 per cent Ca0 was melted and a charge of 300 lbs. steel scrap and
400 lbs. Burn pig iron and 400 lbs. Duplex No. 2 was charged. When
the charge had melted fresh slag was given covering up the metal and
a sample was sent for determination of manganese and sulphur. The
manganese and silicon were corrected and the metal temperature was
gauged by an optical pyrometer; and when the flame temperature was
observed to be 1550. C. the metal was tapped into one teapot spout
ladle in which soda ash had been added. The slag was watery it was
thickened by small chips of limestone. Slag was removed and metal
poured into another teapot spout ladle in which soda ash was kept
ready at bottom. The first ladle was cleaned and the nickle magnesium
(weighed amount) was kept ready. The second ladle slag was
thickened and removed and the metal was poured back into the first
ladle. The nickel magnesium was added as the metal was being
poured into the first ladle. There was a brilliant reaction due to the
nickle magnesium. After the reaction subsided the slag was thickened
by limestone chips and slag rabbled out and the inoculant was added.
and stirred by a wooden rabble. The material was poured into the
moulds. Thus ladle after ladle was similarly treated and poured into
moulds,
The moulds were made by moulding machines using ordinary good
moulding sand with five percent bentonite. Match plates with
aluminium patterns were ready for each size of tees and bends. Steel
contraction was used for the patterns i.e. 3/16th to " per foot and
runners and risers were a little more liberal than for cast iron. After
each heat the runners and risers were examined and shrinkages correc-
ted and extra metal cut off whenever it was found unnecessary. About
six hundred to seven hundred bends and tees were poured in each heat
if they were small, and three hundred to four hundred if they were big.
When the temperature of the metal while tapping is maintained at
1500.C. which can easily be done with the rotary furnace the drop in
temperature during desulphurising operations is considerably little the
metal being highly fluid. The first ladle being attached to a spring

Magnesium immediately it is added starts the spheroidisation
nuclei but the metal when it comes into contact with carbon forms
the carbide and prevents the carbon from becoming free graphite
and gets spheroidised. In order to counteract this influence ferrosilicon
(75 percent) is inoculated to give an impetus for further graphitisation
and promote spheroidisation.
The metal magnesium has an effect of depressing the eutectic
temperature while it increases the nuclei for eutectic crystallisation.
The graphite particles assume a spheroidal form and each speroid is
enveloped by a coating of austenite.
The austenite further absorbs the spheroidised graphite produced
by the effect of inoculation and the spheroids grow without the
formation of flakes.
Since we are desirous of having a ductile iron we have maintained
the silicon at 2.5 percent and manganese at 0.35 percent to obtain a
'ferritic matrix which alone would give us elongation. The silicon given
as inoculant not only stimulates graphitisation but also helps to supply
the silicon deficiency if any to render the matrix ferritic
In a spheroidal graphite iron the final matrices may be ferritic,
pearlitic, acicular, martensitic or austenitic as desired depending upon
the use for which it is required. But here as we have gone into regular
production of pipe specials we have attempted to get a ductile
ferritic matrix by adjusting the composition to obtain a ferritic matrix.
I beg to draw your attention to the table where the analysis
shows the magnesium in the matrix. We are permitted to carry
magnesium from 0.06 to 0.12 percent. We have maintained the
magnesium on the lower side just to maintain the matrix ferritic which
would turn pearlitic or martensitic or acicular if magnesium were
allowed to increase being a strong carbide former.

HEAT TREATMENT
The heat treatment consists in heating the castings to about 900. C.
and holding them at that temperature for about 2 hours while the
massive cementite decomposes to form nodules of temper carbon and
cooling them to the pearlitic change point about 730. C. and maintain
there for about one hour. During this stage the carbon rejected from
solid solution is deposited as graphite on the existing nodules.

On the lathe the spheroidised castings hehaved very well. While
being cut on the lathe the cut fibres would continue in one piece ten to
fifteen feet long as though it was a thin wire issuing out. The whole
factory was overjoyed on the finding of a new experience and a new
metal which is not steel, which is not cast iron, but a metal with
characteristics midway between, which had the advantages of both.
Intricate castings can be teamed more easily and successfully than
in steel with strength and properties approaching steel. High duty cast
irons like niresist, nihard could be spheroidised and made stronger to
serve better. There is no doubt that this recent research in cast iron
will revolutionise the whole foundry industry in about twenty years.
You will see the limitations in Indian pig irons in all of which the
phosphorus is high. There is no brand which compares favourably
with Hematitic pig iron. This is a serious handicap in the steel
industry for recarburising purposes as well as in the spheroidal graphite
iron industry where low phosphorus irons are in demand.
In order to meet this demand for low phosphorus pig iron in
America and Europe they have developed the Metallurgical blast
cupola which converts hundred percent steel scrap into the finest low
phosphorus pig iron with low sulphur. Where there is abundance of
steel scrap and no power facilities for melting they have installed the
Metallurgical Blast cupola and converted this scrap into pig iron with
low phosphorus and low sulphur at a temperature of 1540. C. so as to suit
ideally a Bessemer Converter. With this furance they have installed
steel plants and rolling mills in America and in Europe. The
spheroidal gaphite iron manufacture will run under ideal conditions
with a Metallurgical Blast cupola.
