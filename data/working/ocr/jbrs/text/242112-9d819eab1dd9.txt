THE BRAHMANICAL GODS IN BURMA.
(A CHAPTER ON INDIAN ART AND ICONOGRAPHY)
By Mr. N. R. Ray, M.A., P.R.S. has been published by the University
of Calcutta, and on this the author was awarded the Griffith Memorial prize
for 1929, by the University of Calcutta. He has, for some years past, been
engaged in the study of Burmese art and architecture, and in this dissert-
ation he has embodied the results of his research on the Brahmanical images
so far discovered in Burma. This is a welcome publication, and is of special
interest to us as it deals with a subject relating to Burma's past, and also
because our knowledge of the subject does not extend much beyond the
publications of the Archæological department of Burma. Although the
materials are taken mainly from them the author has, for the first time,
made a systematic study of the Brahmanical images. He has not only
mentioned their importance, he has also discussed their bearing on early
Indo-Burmese history.
The work is made up of an introductory and 4 other chapters followed
by indexes and plates of images of Brahmanical gods and goddesses and of
some coins. At the end of each chapter a list of references is appended.
In the first chapter the author discusses at some length the question as
to why Brahmanism which affected the life of Burma's neighbours like Indo-
China and Java, did not make the same response here ? And he gives a
tentative suggestion. It was probably due to the fact that in Burma and in
Siam the Hinayana form of Buddhism prevailed where as in those countries
the Mahayana was the religion of the people. The comparative absence of
gods in the former and their abundance in the later group explains the
reason why Brahmanism with its hosts of divinities did not make its mark in
Burma. That is all the author could possibly say from a study of the
images. But before we accept his statement it is necessary to prove that
Hinayana was firmly established in Burma before the coming of Brahmanism.
Various other factors such as the genius of the people, the geographical
location of the place from which the religion came and its character must
have had their share in the choice of the religion in Burma.
The following chapters are devoted to the study of the various images,
and in the concluding chapter the author has tried to arrange the facts in a
chronological order. His conclusion is that Brahmanical influence was
confined from the 6th or 7th century to the 14th century. These centuries
he divides into three periods. To the first period which extends from the 6th
to the 9th century belong a few images of Arakan which were inspired by
the art of the later Gupta School. The artists of Arakan came by land
from Eastern India. The Arakanese Indian art was possibly fostered by the
line of Chandra Kings who ruled there from the 5th to the 10th century.
Some of the images of old Prome where the Vikrama Kings ruled have been
included under this period. Here the artists were possibly inspired by the
art of the Pallavas who came by sea.
To the second period belong some of the images of Thaton and Pagan.

images of Pagan resemble the art of the school of the Palas and Senas of
Bengal and Bihar. Thaton which was the land of Talaings hailing from
the region of Orissa produced some of the best specimens of Indian art in
Burma. During the Pagan period art reached the high water-mark. The art
of Pagan was rich in its variety and bears the influence of many lands,
especially of Orissa and Bengal.
After the fall of Pagan Brahmanical influence became dormant and was
confined to the delta districts. The last period was the decadent period
comprising the 14th and 15th centuries. To this period belong the images
of Tennaserim which resemble late South Indian icons. Images made after
the 14th century have not been discovered so far. The author finds the
reason for this in the fact that India was dominated by Mahommedans and
that the commercial expansion of Indians from the mainland ceased, with
the result that the source of inspiration was interrupted.
Considering the small number of finds such a classification may appear
to be a rather daring undertaking. But there can be no doubt that guilds
of artists had flourished who owed their inspiration to different Indian
schools and the attempt at arranging the images in a historical setting is
laudable.
Besides giving us a history of the images, the author has made an
artistic survey of some of the typical images. Most of the images are,
however, important for their conographical and historical value, and
only a small number have a high artistic worth. The author takes it for
granted where the images are of a high artistic order that they were made
by artists coming from India or skilled artists trained by Indian masters.
When the production is of a meagre artistic value it is done by a semi-
accomplished local craftsman. The author has shown many points of
departure from the orthodox iconographic rules, for example the standing
posture of the Vishnu image in Hmawza brought to light by the author. He
thinks the variations were due either to insufficient knowledge of the artistic
etiquette or that the sculptors followed a version unknown to the artists of
India. His explanation of the association of acquatic animals with Ganesa
is ingenious.
There were many images but so for one Hindu temple, the Nât hlaung
in Pagan is known. Vishnu was the most important of the Brahmanical
deities. Images both of Vishnu and his incarnations are the largest in
number. Next to Vishnu Siva was the important Brahmanical deity. It is
interesting to know that no early phallic symbol of Siva has been discovered.
There are only a few images of Brahma, Surya and Ganesh. The Bahmani-
cal images found in Buddhist shrines occury & subordinate post on in
relation to the position of the Buddha.

in spite of the fact that Ganesa, Mahapeinne has found a place in Burmese
mythology still the author does not exaggerate the influence of Brahmanism
which, according to him, was superficial. At the present state of our
knowledge, or rather of our ignorance the statement is more or less true.
The author has brought to light a few images and has identified one or
two for the first time. His identification of the image of Surya in the outer
niche of the Nât hlaung temple of Pagan seems to be correct, although
there may be doubt about his identification of the pavillion referred to in a
Tamil inscription. The Nat hlaung was a South Indian temple but in the
opinion of the author the images of the avataras in it were made by artists
trained in the Eastern School of the Palas and Senas. The surmise is
interesting, and further proofs will possibly be found in support of his
contention.
The book seems to have been hurriedly rushed through the Press for
certain irregularities of language remain unchecked in it. Certain expressions
occuring in it had better been avoided. There are some other inaccuracies
which are of a minor character, and probably due to the same reason.
On the whole we are pleased with the dissertation and would encourage
its author to publish more of the results of his researches into Burmese art
and architecture.
