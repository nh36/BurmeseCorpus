REVIEW
A PALI-BURMESE DICTIONARY
by
U Hoke Sein, B.A., B.L.
Dictionary-making is a late art for Burmans. The first Burmese-Burmese Dictionary is
being prepared by Professor U Wan and will soon be completed. The English-Burmese
Dictionary being prepared by Dr. Ba Han, who also helped to revise U Tun Nyein's earlier
English-Burmese Dictionary is nearing completion. Some time ago we came across parts
of a Pali Dictionary explained in Pali by the late Masoycin Sayadaw, but we do not know
if that learned work was even completed. The Concise Pali Dictionary by Ledi Pandita
is an enlarged Pali vocabulary explained in Burmese and is limited in scope. The Pali-
Burmese Dictionary by U Hoke Sein under review is a welcome addition to Pali
lexicography. It registers 50,000 words and runs into 1180 pages. It is an etymological
dictionary in the sense that for every individual Pali word a Sanskrit equivalent is given
with the derivation clearly shown wherever possible with roots, and prefixes and suffixes.
The importance of roots is stressed again in Appendix A where the roots in Pali and
Sanskrit are given alphabetically with their meanings. Appendix B gives the primary
suffixes, attached to roots to form bases of words with their meanings. Appendix C gives
the secondary suffixes attached to bases to form words with their meanings. Appendix D
gives a list of indeclinable words with their meanings. Appendix E gives a list of pre-
positions with their meanings. Appendix F gives the declensions of nouns and pronouns
and adjectives with their meanings. Appendix G gives the conjugations of verbs with
their meanings. Appendix H shows how Pali words are to be changed into Sanskrit
words, supplied by U Tha Myat. Perhaps the reverse process also should be shown, how
Sanskrit words are changed into Pali words. Thus all the basic requirements to acquire
a grammatical knowledge of the Pali language are supplied to the reader. And Burmans
have an opportunity to learn how differently Pali words are constructed from Burmese
words and how Pali and Sanskrit are related languages. The author's words on the rela-
tions of Pali and Sanskrit are worth quoting: "These are the advantages of giving Sanskrit
equivalents to Pali: Many but not all of the Pali and Sanskrit words which are alike in
form have the same meanings. Though some of them do not have the same meanings the
meanings of prepositions, roots and suffixes are the same. Therefore Sanskrit words
protect the meanings of the Pali words from changing. Since Sanskrit words in junction
are exceptionally clear, they not only clarify the meanings of Pali words but also help in
determining how Pali words are derived through prefixes, roots and suffixes. Thus
Sanskrit words are of special benefit in preventing the decay of Pali words and in getting
at their meanings". Those Burmans who are obsessed by the idea that Pali is the origin
of language will find it difficult to accept the above remarks.
The Dictionary is not consistent in the matter of enumerating certain things. Thus in
regard to sets of five, it tells us what are the five ornaments for kings, the five great
offences, the five Aggregates, the five Hindrances, the five Great Rivers. But it does not
tell us what are the five factors of novitiation, the five Precepts, the five Higher Know-
ledges, the five musical instruments or the five faculties. While it gives the derivation
of Patimokkha, it does not enter into philosophical discussions on Attä or nibbana.
The Dictionary has registered some words which are not in the Pali Text Society's
Dictionary; one would like to know where they come from, such as nalamina translated
by U Hoke Sein as snake-fish but by Ledi Pandita as lobster; and datyūha translated by
U Hoke Sein as cormorant and by Ledi Panditas as waterfowl, moza bird, cloud.
On the whole we would like to congratulate U Hoke Sein on producing single-
handed a work which gives so much information to Burmans about the Pali language.
