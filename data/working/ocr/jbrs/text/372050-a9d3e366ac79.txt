A. INTRODUCTION.
There are few records in the literature on passive immunisation
of laboratory animals against Past. septica. Most of the records
have referred to cattle. A very good review of the literature is given
by Schutze (1929).
Holmes (1910) recorded that the antiserum he had prepared pro-
tected bulls in 10.0 ml. doses and buffaloes in 25.0 ml. doses. But
his results were not consistent; smaller doses often giving protection
while the larger doses did not. Kleptzoff (1903) claimed that potent
antiserum could be produced by repeated injection of the bacilli and
that antiserum prepared against one type of organism also gave pro-
tection against other types. Tarantino (1911) claimed that the
hyperimmune serum prepared against barbone gave some but not
absolute immunity. Birch and Benner (1922-23) experimented with
hyperimmune serum prepared in pigs against Past. suiseptica and
found that the antiserum only prolonged the life of experimental
rabbits, but did not save them. Ochi (1934) studied the relationship
between the Past. septica. organisms isolated from various animals
and included passive immunisation and cross-immunisation tests. He
observed marked specific immunity but no cross-immunity. Khalifa
(1934) made a similar observation. Naidu (1933) stated that the
antiserum was of value for prophylaxis but not as therapeutic agent.
Jacotot (1938, 1940), tested the efficiency of hyperimmune serum
against artificial infection. He found that 10-20 ml. protected calves
against a subsequent inoculation of virulent culture which killed con-
trols in less than 36 hours. Inoculation of 20-30 ml. of serum either
at the same time as the virulent culture or 6-12 hours after infection
protected calves. 0.5 ml. of the antiserum given to the rabbits at the
same time as virulent culture gave considerable protection. Similarly,
the simultaneous inoculation of 1.5 ml. intravenously and 0.5 ml.
subcutaneously 6 hours after inoculation, gave protection.

It was considered by the writer that it would be advisable to
make studies on passive immunisation employing small experimental
subjects in order to obtain indications of their value before testing
them on large animals. Since Buddingh and Polk (1939) had em-
ployed chick embryos successfully in their study of passive immunisa-
tion with anti-meningococcus serum, it was decided to use chick
embryos.
B. EXPERIMENTS.
The experiments carried out may be divided into 3 groups, as
follows:-
(1) Immunisation experiments on chick embryos.
(2) Immunisation experiments on mice.
(3) Experiments to determine if there was a relationship
between the titre of the serum and the protective power.
(1) Experiments on chick embryos.
For this purpose, anti-sera to the strains P5 (Past. aviseptica), P24
(Past. suiseptica) and P1 (Past. boviseptica) were employed.
14 days old chick embryos were used. This series of experiments
may be divided into 2 sub-groups, viz., (a) preliminary experiments and
(b) main experiments.
(a) Preliminary Experiments.
Preliminary experiments were done using the anti-sera of the
strain P5 (F).
Technique.
A modified technique was used. 0.1. ml. of the undiluted and diluted
antiserum was introduced into the amniotic sac by means of a Pasteur
pipette which had been drawn to a very fine point, the terminal 1 inch
being bent at 135° and the level of 0.1 ml. marked on the pipette.
30 minutes after the inoculation of the antisera, 18 hours cultures of
P5 (F) grown on plain agar and suspended in plain broth and
standardised to equal opacity Brown's 1 and tenfold dilutions of it,
were dropped on to the chorion-allantoic membrane in 0.05 ml.
quantities.
In the first experiment, immune serum obtained from a rabbit
14 days after it had received 1.0 ml. of heat killed vaccine standardis
ed to Brown's 4 opacity was used. The rabbit had been given 0.1,

This experiment shows some evidence that P5 (F) anti-serum drawn
after the rabbit had been given live culture was protective against
homologous organisms.
The number of eggs employed in the experiment is far too small
to give the results any statistical significance. Nevertheless 0.05 ml.
of a final dilution of 1/10 of the serum may be taken as protecting
against 50 A.L.D. or, bringing this result to common terms which will
for convenience be used for all experiments, 1 c.c. may be said to have
protected against 5,000 A.L.D.
The above technique of mixing antiserum and culture appears to
yield better results than the technique of inoculating the antiserum
into amniotic sac. Therefore the same method was employed in an
experiment to find out if P5 (B) antiserum would give any protection
against P5 (F) organisms. It was found that no protection whatsoever
was given by P5 (B) antiserum.
Similar experiments were done with P24 (F) antiserum against
P24 (F) organisms. The antisera drawn from the immunised rabbit
before and after it had been given live cultures were both used. The
modified techniques in which a certain dilution of the serum was
mixed with the culture, in this case 1/5 and 1/2 dilutions respectively
of the antiserum, was used. The results showed no evidence of
protection.
Similar experiments on chick embryos were performed with Pl
antiserum. For the sake of convenience and orderliness, the experi-
ments and their results are given in sub-section 3.
(2) Experiments with mice.
Experiments similar to those with chick embryos were performed
with mice. Antisera of the same three strains P5 (F), P24 (F) and
P1 were used.
Experiment 1. Passive immunisation of mice with P5 (F) serum
against homologous organisms.
For this experiment the antiserum drawn after the rabbit was
given live culture of P5 (F) and which was used in the experiments
on chick embryos was used.
Technique.
1/10 dilution of the serum was given intraperitoneally in quantities
of 0.5 ml. per mouse. It was given the last thing in the evening and
the culture was given the first thing next morning. Multiple average
lethal doses were injected intraperitoneally, in a volume of 0.5 ml.,
batches of 3 mice being used. One A.L.D. was taken as 0.5 ml. of
10-7 dilution of a suspension equal to opacity Brown's I. The ex-
periment was kept under observation for 10 days.

Titration of the protective power of P1 antiserum was carried

The results of this experiment show that 0.5 ml. of 1/10 dilution
of the serum with a medium titre protected embryos against 500 A.L.D.,
but not against 1000 A.L.D., i.e., its protective power fell between
the low titre serum and the high titre serum obtained after the rabbit
was given live culture.
Thus some evidence was obtained that there is a certain amount
of relationship between the titre of the serum and its protective
power. But to what extent the relationship holds true in general
must be left to further experimentation.
C. DISCUSSION.
The above observations indicate that strains of Past Septica vary
in their antigenic properties and in their power of producing antisera
of prophylactic value. The strain P5 (F) appeared to produce an
antiserum of the lowest value, the strain P24 (F) produced a slightly
better antiserum, while the strain P1 produced the best of the three.
A parallel phenomenon was observed by Hadley (1912, 1913, 1914)
who found that only one of his 16 avirulent strains used in the form
of a living vaccine was able to afford immunity against virulent
cultures.
These observations also show that there was some evidence of
relationship between the titre of the serum and the protective power,
and that the agglutinin-producing power of a strain may be used as
an indication of its probable protective power.
The apparent confusion that still exists in the literature in con-
nection with the use of antiserum as a prophylactic may now be partly
explained by the fact that either unsuitable strains were used in the
preparation of the antiserum and/or that polyvalent antisera against
various antigenic groups of Past. septica were not employed.
D. SUMMARY.
(1) A review of the recent literature is given.
(2) Immunisation experiments on chick embryos and mice are
described.
(3) The antisera against P5 (F) appeared to be of least prophylactic
value, that of P24 (F) better and that of P1 best.
(4) There are indications that P24 (F) antiserum gave some protection
against heterologous organisms.
(5) There is some evidence that there is a relationship between the
titre of the antiserum and its protective power.
(6) A short discussion is given on the value of antiserum as
prophylaxis.
