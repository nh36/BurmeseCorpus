THE VARIOUS PROCESSES INVOLVED IN THE
ATTAINMENT OF LOKIYASAMADHI
THROUGH SAMATHA 1
by

The Buddha's teachings can be divided into 5 Nikayas or 3 pitakas.
But when one is asked what is the main aim of all the teachings of the
Buddha, the answer in short would be that the main aim of the
Buddha's teachings is to realise Nibbana, i.e., the cessation of all
kinds of sufferings.
It is believed that we are tied down to these 31 lokas because of
the ten fetters (samyojanas). They are: Self-illusion (sakkaya-ditthi),
Scepticism (vicikiccha), Attachment to mere Rule and Ritual (silabba-
taparamasa), Sensual Lust (Kama-raga), Ill-wiil (vyapada), Craving for
fine-material existence (rupa-raga), Craving for Immaterial existence
(arupa-raga), Conceit (mana), Restlessness (Uddhacca) and Ignorance
(avijja). If we can cut off these samyojanas then we will be liberated.
The way to do so is given in the Kammatthana practices. So the main
teaching centralises itself in the magga, the path. The path is known
as the Eight Fold Noble Path and can be divided into three stages of
Sila, Samadhi and Panna.
In my thesis I have taken up samadhi through Samatha. Sama-
tha is the practice of concentration to gain tranquillity of the mind.
So those who gain Arahatship through Samatha and then Vipassana
are known as the "Samatha-vanaka, one who has taken tranquillity
(Samatha) as his vehicle (yana).”
There are altogether forty kinds of Kamatthanas. They are:
the ten circles (kasinas), the ten stages of a dead body (asubhas), the
ten objects for reflection (anussatis), the four illimitables (appamannas),
the idea of the unpleasantness of material food (sanna), the contem-
plation over the four Essential qualities of which the body is com-
posed (vavatthana) and the four formless objects of meditation (arupa
kammatthanas). These forty kinds of objects of concentration are
alloted to individuals in accordance with their predominant characters.
According to the Buddha's Philosophy, there are six types of funda-
mentally different characters. They are; The passionate type (raga
carita), the malevolent type (dosa carita), the muddle headed type

Then what is the procedure for such a practice? Here I cannot
go into detail, taking up comparison with the exercises given in the
Applied Psychology", "Hypnotism and Suggestion" and the "Yogi-
exercises
though I have done so to some extent elsewhere. The
comparison is very interesting. But let me repeat again, the highest
mind development can only be attained through Samatha and nothing
else. Of that I am very confident.
The procedure is: Take up any one of the Kasinas out of the
forty. Let us take an earth Kasina. That Kasina will be placed at
a proper distance before the eyes in such a way that the eyes can gaze
at the centre of the disc without strain. The devotee will sit (in any
convenient posture, especially legs folded), before the disc and he has
to gaze at it in a normal way. He can repeat in the form of whisper
or he can say to himself mentally "Earth, Earth". This repetition
will enable the mind to be fixed on one thing only. Sometimes he
may close his eyes but if his concentration becomes complete then
though he closes his eyes he will be able to see the exact image of
the disc in his inward eyes. Once he has acquired that stage and
once he is in a position to reproduce that disc in his "mind's eyes",
he can dispense with the actual disc and instead of gazing at the ordi-
nary disc he now reproduces the disc from his mind and gazes at it
as before.
Here, this reproduction seems very easy. But it requires a very
strong will power to retain the image, without its disappearing.
Then, the devotee continues his practice. Slowly his inner mind
becomes purer and at last at a certain moment, all the 5 Nivaranas,
namely, Lust (kamacchanda), Ill-will (vyapada), Torpor and Dullness.
(thina-middha), Restlessness and Mental Worry (uddaacca-kukkucca),
Doubt (vicikiccha), suddenly disappear and rays and lights shoot out
of the after-image of the disc. This happens just for one thought-
moment, but as the impact is great, the impression left by it cannot

What is a Jhanic stage? This is a state in which the concentra-
tion is so strong and complete that the person looks like a rock, obli-
vious to all the surrounding sights and sounds but conscious of only
one thing i.e., the glittering after-image of the disc.
When a person enters the first Jhana, the mental concomitants of
that Jhanic consciousness are five. First of all he has to reproduce
the image (vitakka), then he has to retain it (vicara), then there is ex-
pectation that the disc will glisten up (piti), and when it actually
glistens up, there is pleasure (sukha) and all the time there is one-
pointedness of the mind (ekaggata). So with these five factors, one
enters into the first Jhana. One can, if one so desires, go up to
Vipassana from this stage of the first Jhana or one can proceed to the
higher Jhana. In the case of the second Jhana the devotee reduces
one factor i.e., vitakka. The reason is that the devotee knowing the
disadvantages of the lower factors tries to get rid of them by intensi-
fying the practice; in due course when he becomes proficient with the
first Jhana, the reproduction of the after-image becomes automatic.
That is the reason why one has to practice mastery over the Jhana
in 5 ways (1) mastery over attending to it; (2) mastery over entering
into it (3) mastery over maintaining it; (4) mastery over getting up
from it (5) mastery over retrospecting the same. This last mastery
is a very important one. On this last type of mastery depends the
practice of the Vipassana especially for the one who is going up to
Vipassana from Samatha. From the second Jhana one can go up to the
third Jhana by eliminating retention altogether. The disc, the lumi-
nious disc, now appears at will. Then the fourth Jhana can again be
reached by eliminating another factor i.e., piti. As the concentration
becomes continuous, there need be no expectancy, but actual experi-
ence of the ecstasy which is sukha. But on entering the fifth Jhana,
sukha also is dispensed with and in its place equanimity is substituted.
So in the last stage of the Rupa Jhana, there are two factors only.
namely, Upekkha and Ekaggata.
As I have already stated, one can go up to Vipassana from any
stage of Jhana. For practical purposes only these five stages of
Jhanas (i.e., rupa Jhanas) are necessary. There is no need to go up
to a still higher Arupa Jhana. From these Arupa Jhanas one cannot
proceed to Vipassana; for the three characteristics namely transitori-
ness, suffering and unsubstantiality cannot be practised, as the recog-
nition here is more of mind than matter. For many years I was
wondering how it can be possible to proceed from Samatha to Vipas-
sana. In Lokuttara cittas, in short form it is given eight and in full
it is given forty. I cannot understand it before. There are also three
stages of the intensity of concentration; (1) the preliminary stage of
concentration; (2) the accessory stage of concentration and (3) the
ecstatic stage of concentration. It is said that for the practice of
Vipassana one has to come down to upacara samadhi. Appana sama-
dhi is the Jhanic state. So it is clear that in Jhana there can be no
practice of Vipassana. If it is so, the question as to how one can

Now it is quite clear. When one enters the Jhanic state, one is ex-
periencing therein all the constituents either of the first Jhana or the
second Jhana or the third Jhana and so on. When one rises from
the Jhana these factors or experiences, which are so real in the Jhana,
disappear. So one takes up any one of these factors and meditate
on the transitoriness of such a factor. While thus meditating, suddenly
the experience of the particular factor ceases, leaving a very balming
effect, the like of which one has never before experienced in this life,
no, not even in the whole of the series of lives in the Samsara itself.
This is nothing but the entering into the magga and realizing the
phala. If such a thing happens for the first time, then one is a Sota-
panna of the first Jhana as the realization is based on the first Jhana.
If that happens after the second Jhana, then that person will be a
sotapanna of the second Jhana. The same process holds true also for
the other stages of sanctification. Now the question comes. Who
is a sotapanna?". Earlier in this paper I mentioned the ten samyo-
jans that bind us to the world of births and rebirths. A sotapanna
is the one who has entered the stream that leads one to Nibbana. So,
he is the hearer of the true law and thus he is a stream winner. (He
will have seven more births at the most in this 31 lokas). On reali-
zation of this sotapannaship, one can cut off the three samyojanas
namely, Self-illusion (sakkaya-ditthi), Scepticism (vicikiccha), and At-
tachment to mere Rule and Ritual (silabbata-paramasa). But if one again
realizes Nibbana for the second time then he becomes a sakadagamin.
He has removed the grosser elements of another two samyojanas namely,
the Sensual lust (kamaraga) and Ill-will (vyagada). But for a saka-
dagamin, there are still 5 fetters left. There is not much difference
between a sotapanna and a sakadagamin, for both of them have
seven fetters still left to be cut off. The only difference is that whereas
a sotapanna has kamaraga and vyapada in their full form, a sakada-
gamin has them in finer form only. For this reason he is a once-
returner. He cannot be reborn again in the kama loka more than
once. When one realises Nibbana for the third time he becomes an
Anagamin, a never-returner to this kama loka as he has totally got
rid of the two fetters, kamaraga and vyapada. But there are still left
with him these five fetters, namely, the craving for fine material exis-
tence (rupa-raga), the craving for immaterial existence (arupa-raga),
conceit (mana), restlessness (uddhacca) and ignorance (avijja). An
Anagamin can be born in any one of the Suddhavasa bhumi (arupa-
brahmalokas), if he does not realise arahatship in the present life.
But if one so desires and on proceeding with the practice, if the devotee
realises Nibbana for the fourth time, then he becomes an arahat, a
holy-one who has totally cut off ail the fetters that bind one to these
31 lokas. The magga, the eight fold noble path, can be divided into
three stages, namely Sila, Samadhi and Panna. Though one can be
very moral, yet morality is not an end in itself. It is just the first
step towards panna, and it is only the foundation on which Samadhi
has to be built. Again, though one has attained all these 8 or 9 stages
of Jhana, one cannot claim that he has gained panna. Panna i.e., true
knowledge or insight, cannot be gained until and unless one has taken
up Vipassana. So samadhi, though powerful in itself is not sufficient
enough for the realization of Panna until and unless one goes up to

If Vipassana is the only way for the realization of Nibbana, then,
why should one practice Jhanas at all? Here, the explanation I can
offer is that there are different kinds of arahats. There are ordinary
arahata with no mind power at all. There are arahats endowed with
mind powers (these may be those who go up to Vipassana through
Samatha or who practice Samatha only after the attainment of arahat-
ship). There are also eta aggas (the specialists). There are pecceka
Buddhas and the Samasambuddhas. In the case of the pacceka Buddhas,
they must be able to enter into the Jhanas and in the case of the
Buddhas also. For to enter into Nirodha Samapatti, one must have the
mastery over all the Jhanas, rupa or the arupa. I have mentioned
that for practical purposes of proceeding to Vipassana, it is not neces-
sary to go up to the arupa Jhanas. But if one intends to do so, one
In going up to the higher arupa Jhanas, one first concentrates
on the patibhaganimitta of the kasina and one tries to enlarge it in
such a way that the luminous disc now covers the whole sky i.e., akasa.
So, instead of concentrating on the luminous disc, the devotee now
turns his concentration to the infinite akasa and thus enters into the
Jhana of "the Infinity of space" known as Akasanancayatana. Then
one can go up to a still higher Jhana. One asks oneself. "Who is
conscious of the "Infinity of space"? The vinnana i.e., the conscious-
ness is conscious of the infinity of space. He thus changes his object
of concentration from that of the "Infinity of space" to that of the
"Infinity of consciousness." Then again, by denying that there is any-
thing, one can still go up to another higher stage of the Jhana on to
"the realm of Nothingness". After repeated practice, perception be-
comes so subtle that, one can neither affirm nor deny the existence of
perception. Thus one enters the "realm of neither perception nor non-
perception," the highest arupa Jhana that one can have in these 31
planes of existence. This is the highest Jhana of the mundane lokas
corresponding to the highest arupa Brahma plane.
Once a person has attained such a stage and if he is also an
anagamin or an arahat, then he is in a position to enter the Nirodhasa-
mapathi "the Jhana on the cessation of perception and sensation". In
this connection it is interesting to note the process actually gone through
by the Buddha just before His mahaparinibbana, after addressing the
priests thus: "And now, O priests, I take my leave of you; all the consti
tuents of beings are transitory; work out your salvation with diligence".
And this was the last word of the Tathagata. There upon the Blessed

In this connection one may ask why one cannot go up directly to
the Nirodhasamapatti? The reason is that cessation of Kamma-
actions, cannot be done all on a sudden though it can be performed
almost in a twinkling of an eye in the case of highly proficient Arahats,
Pacceka Buddhas and the Buddha Himself. In the Samyutta Nikaya,
it is said thus:--“And moreover, O priest, I have taught the gradual
cessation of kamma. Of one who has entered the first Jhana the voice
has ceased; of one who has entered the second Jhana reasoning and
reflection have ceased; of one who has entered the third Jhana joy
has ceased; of one who has entered the fourth Jhana the inspirations
and the expirations have ceased; of one who has entered the realm
of the infinity of space, the perception of form has ceased; of one who
has entered the infinity of consciousness the perception of the realm
of the infinity of space has ceased; of one who has entered the realm
of nothingness the perception of the realm of the infinity of conscious-
ness has ceased; of one who has entered the realm of neither percep-
tion nor yet non-perception, the perception of the realm of nothingness
has ceased; of one who has entered the cessation of perception and

With regard to the performance of “Abhinnas", it is as follows:
If the devotee wants to perform any miracle, first of all he has to enter
the fourth Jhana on any kasina. On arising from Jhana, he determines
what miracle should take place and he again enters the Jhana of that
particular miracle. On arising from that he wills that the miracle
takes place. Then only the miracle does take place. So far the per-
formance of any miracle, the person (whether he is a putthuijana or an
ariya) must be the master of the fourth Jhana; otherwise, nothing can
be done. To me all such phenomena seem to be normal; the only
thing why we take them to be supernormal is because of our limitation;
we have not reached that stage as yet. But gradually all of us, if we
so desire, determine and persevere, can become Arahats, Ekaggata,
Panaka Buddhas and Sammasambhuddha ourselves.
