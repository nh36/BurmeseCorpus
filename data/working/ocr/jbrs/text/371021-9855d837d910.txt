A. INTRODUCTION
There is a vast literature on this subject and the reports of the
results are conflicting and confusing. Schutze (1929) gave a very able
review of the literature up to the time of his writing. He remarked
It is difficult to form an idea as to the real prophylactic value of
vaccination against bovine pasteurellosis. Most opinions on the subject
are the result of field experiments without adequate control............In
this way a false impression may often be given that treatment by
vaccination has lowered the morbidity rate and brought the epizootic
to a standstill". The same lack of convincing evidence is seen in
regard to active immunisation against Past. suiseptica infection. In
fowl cholera there is certain substantial evidence of success but there
are also many other reports to the contrary.
It will be pointless to review the literature so ably covered by
Schutze. Therefore it is proposed here just to emphasize certain points
which have a direct bearing on the following experiments and to review
the literature not covered by Schutze.
Since Pasteur (1880) successfully produced immunity in fowls by
the use of an attenuated culture of Past, aviseptica, there had been many
attempts to immunise against Past. septica from various animals by
killed, attenuated and live avirulent vaccines. Attempts to confer
protection have been made in various ways. Generally speaking, with
Past. septica, killed vaccines have not been satisfactory. Vaccination
with attenuated cultures has given variable results and sometimes cause
the death of the vaccinated animals. Quite a few observations have
been made using live avirulent vaccine and the reports on the whole
have been encouraging. Hadley (1911, 1913) produced solid immunity
in rabbits again Past. aviseptica by using an avirulent strain as a live
vaccine. Manninger (1919) obtained evidence of a certain degree of
protection against virulent Past. avtseptica from the inoculation of a
living avirulent strain. De Kruif (1921) observed that a single injection
of a living avirulent variant of Past. lepiseptica conferred a high
degree of immunity on rabbits against virulent strains of Past.

Past. aviseptica using pigeons as experimental animals. They studied
the relative immunising value of a killed virulent vaccine,
a killed avirulent vaccine and a living avirulent vaccine. The vaccines were
given intravenously. They found no appreciable differences between
the three vaccines. Anderson et. al (1929) made observations on
similar lines. They used (a) killed “S” type, (b) killed "R" type,
(c) mixed killed "S" and "R" types and (d) living "R" vaccines.
These four types of vaccines were tested for their relative immunising
power on pigeons against virulent "S" type and killed doses of "R"
type. These workers observed that there was no evidence of any
protection being given against killing doses of "R" type by any of the
vaccines except by "mixed killed "S" and "R" type vaccine which
conferred some immunity. But against virulent "S" type all vaccines
gave variable degree of immunity and they could be placed in ascending
order as follows:-(a) killed “S” type, (b) killed “R” type, (c)
mixed "S" and "R" type, and (d) living "R" type vaccine.
Priestley (1936) carried out immunising experiments against a
strain of Past. aviseptica using mice. He compared the immunising
power of a heat killed virulent vaccine with the organisms still
possessing a capsule, a heat killed avirulent vaccine and a heat killed
avirulent vaccine heated to such a temperature that the capsules
were destroyed. He found that against very virulent organisms, the
latter two vaccines gave no protection while the first did give a
considerable degree. But against a strain of low virulence, the
avirulent vaccine afforded some protection.

C DISCUSSION
The successful use of living avirulent vaccine had been recorded by
workers in various fields. The records in regard to Past. septica. infec-
tion were given in the introduction to this report. The results reported.

here are contrary to those observed by Priestley (1936). It would seem
that the resistance to heat of the capsules of different strains varies
considerably; thus the mice inoculated with a virulent, capsulated
suspension of P5 (F) organisms heated at 60°C for 45 minutes in these
experiments died. It seems therefore, impossible for any hard and fast
rule to be laid down regarding the temperature and the duration
required to kill the organisms without destroying the capsule.
Anderson et al (1929), in their extensive studies of the comparative
prophylactic value of Past. aviseptica, prepared their vaccines by heating
at 56°C for 30 minutes and carbolising to 0-5 percent workers and by
the writer himself, avirulent strains, either killed or living used as
vaccines appear to be at least as efficient as killed virulent vaccines. Since
heating or preservatives might conceivably destroy the antigenic proper-
ties of of the organisms in some way, it seems better to use living
organisms for immunisation. The great objection to this of course, is
that the strain might regain its virulence. But there is no evidence that
a completely avirulent strain has spontaneously regained its virulence.
In the same way as Brucell abortus strains of modified virulence are
now being used on a large scale, it may also be possible in the near
future to make use of living avirulent Past. septica for the control of
pasteurellosis on a large scale. The successful use of living cultures in
the control of haemorrhagic septicaemia by Hardenbergh and Boesner
(1916-1917) gives encouragement to follow up this line of research.
D. SUMMARY
(1) A review of the recent literature is given.
(2) "Blue" variants can be used successfully as live vaccine to
confer active immunity on mice against virulent "fluorescent"
types.
(3) The capsule of strain P5(F) appeared to be fairly resistant
to heat.
(4) A short discussion is given on the use of living, avirulent
vaccine.
