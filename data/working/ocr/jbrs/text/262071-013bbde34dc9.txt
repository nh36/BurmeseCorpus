Chapter I. The Appointment of the Envoys and their arrival and stay in
Calcutta, October 1830-October 1831.
The Kings of the Alaungpaya Dynasty of Burma, right up to 1826, had
looked upon all neighbouring countries, except China, with contempt. British
agents, and later envoys such as Symes, Cox and Canning had been received
at Court, but as suppliants come to crave for favours at the Golden Feet.
Indeed, Bagyidaw, the sixth King, fought the first War with the British
labouring under the delusion of Burmese military superiority over all powers.
Mahabandoola's historic march from Arakan to Rangoon, during the rainy
season, was undoubtedly a spectacular feat, but when he met with his first
experience of a British shell exploding in his presence he was amazed. The
war proved to be a great eye-opener. The King lost not only large slices of
territory but had to agee to pay an indemnity of One Crore of rupees and to
entertain at his capital a British Resident. The Ruler, his Ministers and the
people in general realized that it was hopeless to attempt to recover the lost
territory by force of arms. The victorious new neighbour could no longer be
despised, at least not officially. In keeping with this changed outlook the
Burmese Government, in the reign of Bagyidaw, sent two missions to the
Governor-General of India--one in 18271 and the other in 1830. The subject
matter of the present thesis deals with the Second Mission, 1830-1833.
The 7th Article of the Treaty of Yandabo (1826) had provided for the
cultivation and improvement of the relations of amity and peace between the
Burmese and British governments by means of accredited ministers or
residents from each residing at the capital city of the other. John Crawfurd
was the first Envoy and Resident Minister deputed by the Governor-General
to the Court of Ava in conformity with the above treaty. He was in Ava
from the 30th September to the 12th December 1826. The Burmese Govern-
ment also deputed two envoys to Calcutta in March 1827. They went first to
Calcutta thence to Moulmein, and returned to Ava in June 1827. The object
of the Mission was threefold in character:- First, they requested that the
payment of the third and fourth instalments of the Indemnity (the 5th Article
of the Treaty) be delayed by three or four years because of the poverty of
the Government as well as of the people. Second, they claimed that the Kubo
Valley to the east of the state of Manipur was a Burmese possession and
should be returned to the King. The British Government, at the end of the
First Anglo-Burmese War (1824-1826) had recognized this Valley to be the
possession of Gumbheer Singh, Raja of Manipur, since as an ally of the
Company he had occupied it during the War and had also claimed it as a

After the return of Crawfurd for over two years there was no British
Resident in Burma, neither did the Burmese monarch station his representa-
tive in Calcutta. On the 24th April 1830, however, Major Henry Burney
arrived in Ava as British Resident the object of the Governor-General being
to establish and maintain diplomatic relations with Burma on a permanant
footing. Bagyidaw and his Government looked upon the Residency as an
eye-sore: it was one of the direct fruits of the humiliating Treaty of Yandabo,
and tended, in their opinion, to entrench upon the independence and sove-
reignty of the King. Soon, however, on account of the charming personality
of Burney, the King and his Government realized that it was possible to
make use of the Resident profitably, for the purpose of the return of the
Kubo Valley to Burma and the re-adjustment of Indo Burmese boundaries
in favour of their country.
Within a few weeks of Burney's arrival in Ava the Burmese Government
also expressed a desire to send an ambassador to Calcutta. Burney encou
raged the Woongyees or Ministers of the King in this move, in hopes that
the measure would ensure at Ava much correct and useful information
regarding the power, resources and character of the English. Finally, in
August 1830 two men were selected as Senior and Junior Resident respec-
tively. The Senior Resident was Mengee Maha Tsee Thoo Maung Shwe who
was at the time employed in the Palace as Twen-then-Woon or Superinten-
dent of attendants and had in the previous reign served as Mye Dain or
Land Measurer and Myeet Ye Woon or Superintendent of a Special River
Police. The Junior Envoy was Mentha Nanda gyau den Maung Byo a Tsare-
daugyee or clerk to the Ministers. Both were looked upon by their country-
men as men of learning and particularly conversant with Burmese history
and geography.3

Governor-General without date according to custom, the pronoun cautiously
omitted, and possessing the general character of a friendly communication
to an equal. The Royal presents for the Governor-General consisted of
rubies, sapphires, Burmese silk cloths and lacquered boxes.
The period of the sojourn of the Burmese Mission in Bengal Presidency
was from December 1830 to June 1833. The main source of the history of

On the 9th October 1830, the Burmese Mission, consisting of the two
Envoys, their families, servants and bodyguards numbering in all 84 men and
women and accompanied by Capt. Geo. Burney and his sepoys and servants,
left Ava by water and arrived at Mimbu on the 18th October. From Mimbu.
by land marches via Maphemyo and Nat-ye-gan they arrived at Ah or Aeng
on the 16th November, and Akyab on the 21st November. Instead of 43
days they could have completed the journey in about three week's time, but
the Envoys adopted dilatory methods. They stopped at almost every pagoda
they met with on their route down the river in order to pray for a safe return
from what they looked upon as a most hazardous service on which they were
going.
The Burmese King was smarting under the cession of territory entailed
by the Treaty of Yandabo, but he had not the power to recover it by force
of arms. The Envoys in their reports tried their best to ease the injured

After a stay of 12 days in Akyab the Envoys embarked on board the
steam vessel Irrawadi, belonging to the East India Company, on December 2,
and arrived off Babid Ghaut at Calcutta on December 6. On the following
day the Mission disembarked and took possession of a three storied house at
Ballygunge to the south of Calcutta. Capt. Burney occupied another house in
close neighbourhood: the Envoys looked upon him as a spy. This suspicion
was confirmed because 12 Indian soldiers and a durwan were also stationed
to guard the Envoys' residence. Twenty days after their arrival two carriages
each drawn by two horses came daily to the house to take the Envoys for an
airing. The Envoys received fixed salaries from their own Government for
themselves, followers and servants. The Indian Government paid per month
Rs 250 for house rent, Rs 200 for the carriages, and Rs 300 for bazaar
expenses. The Envoys complained to their Government that upon their
arrival in Calcutta, no person of rank came either to receive or visit them,
and that they received no table allowances as the Burmese ambassadors had
received formerly, and that their 80 followers were allowed only 6 copper
pice per day. Several of their followers died of cholera in Calcutta includ-
ing Maung Gyen the father-in-law of one of the envoys.5

When Raja Gumbheer Singh was informed of the Governor-General's
decision in connexion with the Kubo Valley he felt greatly vexed, but was
later persuaded to accept the new arrangement in good grace. His principal
objection was "the humiliation and shame he felt in agreeing to cede to the
hereditary oppressors of his country a part of the territories of his ancestors,
and the anxiety, apprehension and alarm the near neighbourhood of the
Burmese would be certain to create amongst the inhabitants of Munnipore".2

HISTORY OF THE BURMESE MISSION TO INDIA
The Valley was officially returned to Burma on January 9, 1834 and Raja
Gumbheer Singh died soon after. He was succeeded by Raja Chandra Kirtee,
an infant son. The Supreme Government, in order to compensate the Raja
for the loss of the Valley agreed to give him a monthly stipend of Rs 500
from the date of the surrender of this much disputed territory, with the
proviso that if Kubo ever reverted to Munnipore the stipend would cease.¹
Burney arrived in Ava on November 6, 1833. He was received by the
Ministers with much kindness. The King and Ministers recognized the part
played by him in behalf of Burma, but immediately after, the Ministers under
His Majesty's orders proposed that the British Residency be withdrawn. The
Ministers confessed to Burney that they themselves and the people at large
viewed the Residency as a public proof of the existence of amicable relations
between the two governments, but that their King was opposed to the pre-
sence of a permanent foreign Resident at his capital. 2 The Ministers even
offered to grant some facilities to British commerce and shipping at the port
of Rangoon in return for the removal of the Residency from Ava. The
Supreme Government, however, definitely refused to accede to the proposal
and instructed Burney not to encourage any proposition on the part of the
Burmese Government which had for its object the removal of the Residency
from Ava.3
The King failed to remove the British Residency from Burma by negoti-
ation; but when Tharrawaddy usurped the throne in 1837 he made the
situation so hot for Burney that he withdrew to Rangoon on June 17, 1837
and on October 12 he retired to Moulmein and thence to Calcutta. Bayfield
officiated as Resident in Rangoon until the arrival on July 13, 1838 of Col.
Richard Benson the new Resident. Benson was in Amarapoora, the new
capital, from October 1838 to March 1839. He was altogether neglected
both by the King and his Ministers. He withdrew after a stay of five months.
and placed his Assistant Mcleod in charge of the Residency. Mcleod fared
scarcely any better but held on till July 22, 1839 when he also retired to
Rangoon. He kept charge of the Residency in Rangoon up to January 7, 1840
when Auckland the Governor-General withdrew it altogether. From 1840
onwards, therefore, there was no British Residency in Burma. Meanwhile the
government of the country under incompetent monarchs and servile ministers
continued to decline. There was no British officer present either at the
capital or in Rangoon to protect British subjects, their property and their
interests in Burma; the result was the outbreak of the Second Burmese War
in 1852 which ended so disastrously for the King. Had a Resident been
present at the Burmese capital, it is possible, the war might have been
averted.
