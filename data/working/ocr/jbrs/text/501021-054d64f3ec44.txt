1. Cosmological tradition regarding marriage
Marriage is the fountain-head of the
family. Consequently in the study of the
ancient Burmese family life what is of car-
dinal importance is to discover first of all
the Burmese view of marriage not as an
abstract doctrine but as a lively faith that
affects the people's actions and their esti-
mate of their actions.
The Burmese cosmological tradition
regarding marriage provokes attention. It
says that the earliest prehistoric couples
began to build houses to hide from view
their conjugal relationship which was con-
sidered unsavoury by contemporary reckon-
ing..
'The males looked upon the females
and the females upon the males, and univer-
sally. Wise men reviled and opposed those
degrading practices. To be free from this,
and to conceal their bad deeds they built
houses, and lived within enclosures and
following each other's example, secured a
supply of food'.
2. Basic Burmese attitude towards marriage
This tradition gives the basic Burmese
attitude towards marriage. To remorseless
Buddhist orthodoxy, marriage strengthens
the chain that keeps man within the orbit
of the whirl of ceaseless existences (Samsara).
However that may be, we catch more than
a glimpse of the key-factors of the Burmese
concept of marriage as the voluntary union
of a man and a woman. The emphasis on
the free consent of the parties to be joined
in wedlock is clearly discernible in the three
ways of contracting a marriage under Bur-
mese Buddhist Law.
Amongst men there are only three ways
of becoming man and wife, which are as
follows:
‘1st, a man and woman given in mar-
riage by their parents, who live and eat
together;
“2nd. a man and woman brought by
the intervention of a go-between, and
who live and eat together;
‘3rd. a man and woman who come
together by mutual consent, and who live
and eat together."2

men as the life-partners of their grown-

3: Polygamy permitted but discounte-
nanced
Another noticeable feature in Bud-
dhist Marriage Law is that a man may
take to himself several women whom he
jealously guards from the rest of the
world and whose number is limited only
considerations of his material means. ‘A
man may marry as many as ten wives
if he can maintain them all.’4
This right is however exercisable sub-
ject to the first wife's consent. Any sub-
sequent union without the first wife's
consent gives her the right of divorce.*
At the present day however polygamy
is not countenanced by public opinion.
Even if there are more wives than one
only one is introduced at public functions.
She is known as Pwe-det Maya (ပွဲတက်
မယား) or Pwe-daing-maya. (ပွဲထိုင်မယား)
A contrast therefore poses itself bet-
ween a Buddhist marriage and a Christian
marriage which is 'the voluntary union
for life of one man and one woman to
the exclusion of all others'.7

: Status and rights conferred by marriage
The status that attaches to a marriage
and the rights that flow therefrom under
Burmese Buddhist Law challenge compari-
son with those under other legal systems.
The Buddhist husband and wife are
equal life partners, and they hold property
in common. One is the heir of the other.14
'Husband and wife own property equally,
that is to say, the husband is entitled to half
the property and so is the wife.'15 ‘The hus-
band inherits from the wife, and the wife
from the husband16 The wife owns the
husband's property.'17
One qualification must however be
mentioned. If on the death of either spouse,
there is the eldest child of the union who is
of the same sex as the deceased spouse and
The Burmese Family Life of Old
is competent to step into the shoes of the
deceased, such an one (known as the Orasa)
is entitled to a quarter share of the property
left at the death of the departed spouse. In
that eventuality the surviving spouse recei-
ves the remaining three-fourths.18
As a general principle, on the death of
both parents sons and daughters inherit
equally. This rule of equal distribution is
founded on a homely illustration. 'When a
hen brings food to her chickens, they all
have an equal share'.10
It must be mentioned that a Buddhist
has no power to will away his property.
During the subsistence of the marriage
each spouse has an interest in all the pro-
perties of the other including the property
brought to the marriage which becomes the
joint-property of the marriage. But in case
of property inherited by either spouse after
marriage, the other spouse has a vested
interest in one-third of such property. 20

What is pertinent to our purpose is to
indicate the unique social status achieved
by Burmese women as a result of the new-
found faith which came to them through
the Indian immigrants.
Every foreign visitor to the East must
be attracted by the grace and poise of Bur-
mese women with chignon-like hair-dos,
flimsy jackets and colourful skirts. Indeed
he cannot turn a blind eye to their charming
bonhomie, their intriguing individualism
and their keen business sense. It can be
confidently stated that the status of a Bur-
mese woman is unique in the East, if not in
the world. Our wonder grows when she is
viewed in relief against her sister in India
which is the cradle of her religion--
Buddhism.
In pre-Buddhist India women held a
very lowly if not degrading position in
society. The birth of a daughter was not a
matter of joy. Rather, it gave cause for
anxious concern. It was considered a social
stigma to have an unmarried daughter. And
to give away a daughter in marriage often
spelt financial ruin to the parents.
The wife was looked upon as the mere
child-bearer. She held little or no authority
at home and found no place in public func-
tions. Even after the suppression of the sut-
tee by the British which put an end to the
revolting practice of burning the widow on
her husband's funeral pyre, the widow's
position was still unenviable. She could
only return to her father or her son if she
had borne one and sink into insignificance.

The Buddhist teaching regarding sex

in the view regarding the dignity and worth
of womanhood. It is worthy of note that
there is a
sharp difference between the
Buddhist outlook on the position of women
and that of the men of the chivalric age in
Europe. To the Buddhist a woman is man's
equal comrade, while during the age of
chivalry she was looked upon as the weaker
vessel that had a claim upon man's help.
In Buddhism therefore women leapt to
a position undreamt of before. The near if
not actual equality with men came to be
increasingly recognised. With this growing
emancipation of women the mouldy doc-
trine of man's supremacy gradually faded
away.
