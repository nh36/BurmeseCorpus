The story of the Text Publication
Sub-Committee should be told against
the background of Burmese studies in my
school days. I was a student at the
Rangoon Government High School from
1896 to 1905, thus finishing the nine-year
school-course in 9 years. I retain vivid
memories of the emphasis laid on the
teaching of Burmese grammar. In 1899
Mr. A. W. Lonsdale of the Education
Department published a Burmese grammar
to inform scholars that Burmese, being
language constructed on scientific
principles, should be treated from
scientific standpoint. In the preparation
of his work, the author has generally
followed the plan of English grammar.
The book bristles with English grammatical
terms and their Pali equivalents. A Middle
School Anglo-Vernacular Burmese student,
struggling with his English and with no
knowledge of Pali would be unable to
grasp the full meaning of many a gramma-
tical term such as the transitive verb of
incomplete predication. He would find its
equivalent ဝါစကမစုံ ကတ္တု သကမ္မ ကြိယာ just as
difficult to understand, so being the only
Burmese in that string of Pali terms.
A reflexive Personal pronoun is called

One year, however, the two paper setters,

When I returned from Oxford in 1924
after 4 years of study there, I found that
the 4 year-old University of Rangoon had
been allowing Intermediate students each to
offer his own vernacular in the Composition
paper. Thus the Burmans offered Burmese
and the Indians offered their several Indian
vernaculars under the supervision of Mr.
Cassim of the Pali Department. Yet not all
Burmans offered Burmese, because those
Burmans who came to the University from
the European High Schools were allowed to
offer Alternative English instead of Burmese
in the Intermediate examination. Thus
compulsory Burmese composition in the
Intermediate was compulsory only in name.
To change this state of affairs, motion after
motion was debated at the Senate until it
was resolved that Burmese be made com-
pulsory like English for all Intermediate
students, thereby requiring Burmans to offer
the ordinary standard Burmese and non-
Burmans to offer a lower standard Burmese.
The ordinary standard Burmese was intend-
ed for Burmans who had taken the Anglo-
vernacular High School Burmese, and the
lower standard Burmese was intended for
369
non-Burmans who had taken the European
High School Burmese. The former was of
course higher than the latter. In practice
the lower standard Burmese was divided
into A and B standards. Standard A was
offered by non-Burmans who came to the
University from the European High Schools
with Burmese as one of their subjects, and
standard B was offered by non-Burmans
who knew no Burmese. In order to encour-
age the standard B class of beginners, I my-
self took the class for 2 years. I found that
the students, all non-Burmans of course,
were glad to have this opportunity to study
Burmese, the more so when they learnt that
a knowledge of Burmese, even by Standard
B, was essential for entry into government
service.
The students who had a complaint to
make about the Senate resolution were those
Burmans who came from the European
High Schools, where they had studied, as
pointed out above, a course of Burmese
lower than that taught in the Anglo-
vernacular High Schools. As Burmans they
had to take the Ordinary Standard Burmese
in the Intermediate. But these Burmans
from the European High Schools were
bright students and gamely agreed to im-
prove their Burmese by following the
ordinary standard Burmese in the Interme-
diate. If any of them failed in the Inter-
mediate examination, it was not because of
their failure in compulsory Burmese alone.
