The Memorial in 33rd Street*
33rd Street is not, perhaps, a part of Rangoon which any of us are in
the habit of frequenting, but it is not quite so unattractive a neighbourhood
as it was a few years ago and one may walk down it nowadays without
incurring any loss of reputation; and if any of you should care to go down
33rd Street from Montgomery Street you will see about fifty yards down on
the left-hand side a brick plinth supporting an iron cross which was originally
intended to carry a lamp; the whole is nine or ten feet in height. In the face
of the plinth is inset a brass plate bearing the following inscription :-

Six of those named are members of one family, the Crisps, and a fair
amount of information is available about them.
In the interim between the First and the Second Burmese Wars there
was in Rangoon a firm of merchants, Messrs Trill and Crisp, dealers in book-
muslin, cotton piece.goods, and timber. An extant "Rough Sketch (from
Memory) of the Old Town of Rangoon as it existed between the years 1836
and 1849", dated 1852, marks "the late Mr. Trill's house and Captain
Crisp's residence", which is described elsewhere as being a large, two
storeyed, brick building, standing approximately on the site where Messrs
Balthazar's offices now are. There were several Crisps in Rangoon at the
period. The senior member of the family was Captain May Flower Crisp ;
he was born in England in December 1788, and became a sailor; for some
years he was an impressed seaman on a naval vessel during the French wars,
and after the wars he came to India as a ship's captain and ultimately set
up as a merchant in Burma. It would appear probable that he came to
Burma first at the close of the war of 1824-26, when prospects of increased
trade seemed good, for he is not mentioned as a resident in the country
before that date. He entered into partnership with R. J. Trill, who was
already established in business in Rangoon and who was one of the British
subjects released from imprisonment when the town was occupied by Sir
Archibald Campbell in 1824. Trill, it would seem, did not long survive the
War, but the firm seems to have flourished under Crisp's management, and
was able to add ship-building to its trading activities. Crisp was joined by
his Charles Malcolm Crisp, who is said to have come out from England
son,
in command of one of his father's vessels; he married and then settled down
on shore to help his father. Two of the elder Crisp's brothers, Joseph and
William, were also commanders of ships and made periodic visits to Rangoon
at this time.
M. F. Crisp was a man of active mind. He took a keen interest in
marine architecture and experimented in different forms of ship-building at
the firm's timber and ship-building yard which lay on the foreshore some-
where near the end of the present Godwin Road. It happened that the rules
of the Rangoon port allowed vessels of sixteen feet deck exemption from the
anchorage dues, which normally amounted to about Rs. 200 ; Captain Crisp
therefore built a vessel of three hundred tons which conformed to the normal
model of ships to within three or four feet of the deck, whence the sides
sloped inwards so that the deck should measure only sixteen feet. The
vessel, which in addition to her peculiar shape was also long in proportion to
her width, was confidently expected by other residents in Rangoon to break
her back at the first storm; but evidently the construction was sound, for she

The firm of Trill and Crisp was often in trouble. In the 1840's there was
some complaint on the part of the Burmese officials about contraband goods,
and a still more serious complaint, apparently due to false information laid
by a rival firm, that the Crisps had been making a chart of the Rangoon
River for the use of British warships. The firm made several appeals to the
British authorities in Tenasserim and to the Government of India about acts.
of oppression which they stated had been inflicted on them, but received
only cold comfort, the policy of Government being that British subjects traded
in Burma at their own risk. The elder Crisp, moreover, was often at logger-
heads with the Burmese authorities, and his refusal to shikho when he passed
the Myowun in the street, more than once landed him in jail. The upshot
was that he removed to Moulmein, leaving his son, C. M. Crisp, to carry on
the business in Rangoon. While in Tenasserim he formulated somewhat
novel views on politics; a strictly religious man who was, what was rare in
those days, a teetotaler, he held that religion should be the basis of govern-
ment; he was also an advocate of the elective principle in the making of
official appointments, and would have applied adult suffrage, not merely male
suffrage, to this purpose.
He evidently returned to Rangoon in 1852, for on the 14th May of that
year a memorial signed by him in common with other British inhabitants of
the town, was submitted to the Governor-General, asking for the creation of
the post of civil magistrate, Crisp himself being designated as the most
suitable occupant of the post.* The proposal was received coldly; the firm
of Crisp was viewed with considerable suspicion by the Governor-General
since it was accused of having "sold powder, muskets, and military stores to
the Burmese after the probability of hostilities had appeared," and the refusal
of the request for the magistracy was accompanied by a warning that he
should abstain from all interference with the inhabitants of Pegu on pain of
deportation.
The action of the firm in the matter of the stores is somewhat surprising,
since in December 1851 and in January and March 1852, C. M. Crisp had
sent to the Government of India secret information about Burmese affairs,
and in particular about the extent of the defences and armaments of
the new town; but perhaps the firm believed in insuring against all contin-
gencies. However, when the invading force landed in April 1852, C. M. Crisp
pointed out the way on to the Pagoda platform by which the troops were
able to carry without any serious loss what would otherwise have been a very

The firm was hard-hit by the War, however. Their building in the old
town had been destroyed at the outbreak of hostilities and their goods
confiscated, as was, quite naturally, all British property. They submitted a
claim to the Government of India for Rs 41,490 compensation, but on account
of the matter of the supplies sold to the Burmese all compensation was
refused. Moreover, though some of their timber had apparently been re-
covered by the military, the latter had sold it as prize of war and no com-
pensation could be obtained for this either. M. F. Črisp went to England and
his grievances were taken up by a Member of Parliament, but without result.
He returned to Burma, and died in Moulmein at the age of eighty-five. Some
years before his death he had published locally a pamphlet attacking the
economic policy of Government after the Second Burmese War.
Of the children named in the memorial, Sarah, Susan Eliza, Josephine,
and William, were the children of C. M. Crisp, afterwards Postmaster. C. M.
Crisp had also three other children, Julia Primrose born 6th August 1852,
Godwin William born 5th February 1855, and Edwin Henry born 6th June
1856. These three births are recorded in the registers of the Cantonment
Church.
Malcolm Crisp who died in 1840 aged seven years, is given on the
Memorial as the son of M. F. Crisp, but this statement is said to be incorrect;
he was really the son of either Joseph or William Crisp and therefore the
nephew of M. F. Crisp.
The Captain William Crisp of the Memorial is doubtless one of these
brothers of M. F. Crisp..
There was another member of the family in Rangoon also, known as
Captain Crisp Junior. He was a cousin of C. M. Crisp, and commanded one
of the firm's ships for a time. After the Second Burmese War he became a
pilot. The Cantonment Church registers show the birth on the 2nd June 1861
of Amy Florence, daughter of "William Crisp Pilot and Hosannah Crisp".
Mr Trill, the date of whose death is not specified on the Memorial,
was clearly R. J. Trill, the senior partner of the firm, whose name appears
in the official records of 1824.
Mr Spiers Mate of the Navy was almost certainly William Speirs or
Spears, a half-pay naval lieutenant, who was a merchant and owned a pukka
house and godown in Rangoon; he made a claim of Rs 10,000 for loss of
property incurred in 1852. He is not to be confused with Thomas Spears,
Phayre's correspondent at the Burmese Court.
Mr Hugh Speirs was possibly a connection of William Speirs; he is
described as "a British merchant from Umarapoora."
Captain Brown may have been the Captain Brown who commanded
the Ayrshire, a ship built at Rangoon by Lieutenant Speirs.
Mr Brown Postmaster was F. Browne, merchant and postmaster at
Rangoon in the '40's, whose house also figures in the "Rough Sketch." His
office as postmaster was unofficial so far as the Burmese authorities were

There are certain peculiarities about the Memorial. The reconstruction
of the town was taken in hand immediately after the Second Burmese War,
and a map dated 1853 showing the new lay-out of the streets indicates a
good many of the plots bordering 33rd Street as being already allotted. It is
therefore somewhat surprising that one of the burials recorded should have
taken place as late as the February of 1854.
It is also odd that though the Cantonment Chaplain was able to record
the birth of Julia Primrose Crisp which occurred in 1852, yet he made no
entry of the death of Susan Eliza which also occurred in 1852, and, still more
notably, no entry of the birth and death of William which took place as late
as February 1854 by which date the registers were being regularly kept.
The coincidence of the dates of birth and death of Sarah and William,
both of whom are given as being born on the 22nd February and having died
on the 28th February in 1842 and 1854 respectively, is also remarkable.
All these circumstances, together with the absence of details about some
of the deaths recorded and the error in the parentage of Malcolm Crisp, tend
to cast a measure of doubt on the accuracy of the information given in the
Memorial. The Memorial was evidently erected some considerable time after
the deaths which it records, for the reference to M. F. Crisp as "the late"
Captain Crisp shows that it was not erected till after his death in 1873;
thus the dates were entered from memory of fairly remote events. While
there is no reason to doubt that the burials occurred, the dates given must be
accepted with caution.

It is clear that on the land where 33rd Street now lies there was once
an extensive burial ground. The origin of this burial ground is a matter of
some interest, apart from any question of the identity of those buried in it.
It is hoped to show with some degree of plausibility that this burial ground
was originally attached to the first Baptist Mission House in Rangoon.
The site of the original Baptist Mission House has never been deter-
mined; the House was abandoned after the First Burmese War, and even
if it had not been, the reconstruction of the town after 1852 was so thorough
that it swept away almost all existing buildings. There is indeed so little
relation between Rangoon as it was a hundred years ago and Rangoon as it
is now, that it is extraordinarily difficult to establish any identification of

and Maps in Rangoon and the India Office.** On this map appears a group

Sule Pagoda. Comparison between the 1824 map and maps of modern Ran-
goon is not easy, but certain landmarks shown in the older map still remain,
notably the Shwe Dagon and the Sule Pagodas. If, making use of these land-
marks, the older map be superimposed on the modern map, it will be found
that the main building of the Mission House compound falls on or about
Barr Street not far from Montgomery Street. While the scale of the 1824
map may not be entirely accurate, it is certainly not very far wrong;
the
Mission House must have been, therefore, if not actually on Barr Street,
then in the immediate neighbourhood. Further, there is a very useful map
dated 1853 showing the new lay-out of the town and also marking the old
Burmese roads; though the old buildings are not marked, the position of the
Mission House can be judged from its relation to the roads in the 1824 map;
and this confirms the suggestion that the House was somewhere near Barr
Street.
The evidence of the maps is supported by written evidence. The English
Baptists from Serampore who built the House in 1808-09 state that it lay
about five minutes' walk from the town in the direction of the Shwe Dagon
Pagoda. Mrs Judson says that it lay about half a mile from the town. As
the town then lay entirely between the river and the Sule Pagoda, the evid-
ence of the missionaries is consistent with the argument drawn from the
maps.
Attached to the Mission House was a cemetery. Here were buried,
among others, Richard Thomas Burney of the East India Company's service,
half brother of the novelist Fanny Burney (1808), Jonathan Couch Brain of
the London Missionary Society (1810), and the infant Roger Judson (1816).
No trace of their graves can be found in Rangoon to-day. The first two
were wrecked as early as 1812 at the time of the Amboyna trouble and there
is no evidence that they were ever restored; but the removal of the Mission
House into the town after 1826 did not leave the grave of Roger Judson
uncared for, for Judson records a visit to it as late as 1847, and no doubt it
would have continued in a state of preservation if circumstances had permit-
ted. Evidently the reconstruction of the town after 1852 swept it away just
as it swept away the graves of the Crisp children and others. There is thus
evidence that the Baptist burial ground existed as late as 1847 and probably
as late as 1852, i.e. at the time when most of the burials recorded on the
Memorial took place. It is also known that there was an English burial ground
in Rangoon. On the face of it, it is highly probable that the English burial

then naturally any Europeans who died in Rangoon would be buried there.
Moreover, the "Rough Sketch" of Rangoon between 1836 and 1849 marks

Certain objections arise, however. The Mission House, it seems, was
approximately on Barr Street, whereas the Memorial is in 23rd Street. But
this really presents no difficulty, for, apart from the possible lack of precise
accuracy in the early maps, the compound of the Mission House is described
as comprising about two acres of ground.
A more serious objection is that the English burial ground of the 1840's
is said to have been secured by purchase from the Burmese authorities by
Captain Speirs; but the Baptists' claim to the land no doubt lapsed after they
abandoned the original Mission site, and it would be natural for Speirs to
arrange for burials to continue in the old cemetery since it still existed, and
equally the Burmase authorities would be the readier to make the grant of
land if the site was already in use for this same purpose. Speirs's purchase
was probably undertaken as a means of preserving the existing burial ground
rather than as a means of establishing a new cemetery.
There is another apparent but not real objection. It has been commonly
said that the Mission House lay close to the execution ground,* and the
execution ground is known to have been on or about the site of the Suratee
Bazaar. This would appear to dispose of the suggestion that the Mission
House lay near Barr Street. But is there in fact any evidence that the
Mission House did actually lie near the execution ground? So for as I am
aware (though I may, of course, have missed some of the available.
information), there is no contemporary evidence to this effect. The letters of
the English Baptists who built the House certainly make no reference to the
nearness of the execution ground; and indeed it is highly improbable that the
Mission House would have been built in such a position. The objection
therefore falls to the ground.
Taking all the evidence into account, then, one may not unreasonably
conclude that the original Baptist Mission House lay on a site not far from
the north end of the present Barr Street, and that the Memorial in 33rd
Street marks the site of the original Baptist burial ground.
