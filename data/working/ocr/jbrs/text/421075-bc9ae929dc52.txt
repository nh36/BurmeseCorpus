deepened. I propose today, with your permission, to develop them at
length.

Why did the Burmans take to the plains?-Here, again, the reasons
are clear.-Primarily, it was to escape from Nanchao. The Wang-chü.
to the east, between the Salween and the Mekhong, were first conquered
by Nan-chao Shêng-lo-p'i soon after 713 A.D. 16 In the middle of that
century Ko-lo-fêng's campaigns, which took him to P'u-t'ao and the
borders of Tibet and Assam, must have been a grave threat to the
proto-Burmans. In 794 his grandson, I-mou-hsün, is said to have
attacked the Mang tribes. 17 In all Nanchao's continual wars, from
Ch'êng-tu to Hanoi, these peoples were levied and usually fought in the
vanguard. Fan Ch'o shows how stern the discipline was. 18 Valuable
as was the training, they must have feared and hated their Nanchao
masters.
The only way to escape was to take to the plains. For there, except
for a cold weather raid, Nanchao durst not follow them. But it came
as far as it dared. On the Mo-ling and Ch'i-hsien mountains, over-
looking the plains of the Irawady, Ko-lo-fêng or his successors built
a fortress-city. Its out post was Shên-lung stockade, on the western

The strength of the Pyū kingdom had lain along the Irawady valley
to the west; that of the Mons along the 'railway valley' to the east,
where they were nearest to their relatives, then masters of Old Siam.
From 835 there was a double vacuum in the plains of Burma. This,
I think, was the supreme moment in the history of the Burmans, when
they descended en masse upon the plains of Kyaukse.
Why Kyaukse?-Why not Mandalay in the north, or Yamèthin
or Tonngoo in the south, still the highroad of descent for many Karen
hillmen? The answer will lie in a historical map of Old Kyaukse.
Professor Daw Thin Kyi, who has done a great amount of fieldwork in
the district, is now engaged in making the map, but it is not yet finished.
The plains area of Kyaukse is only 563 square miles. I have far more
than that number of slips for entering on the map. She won't, of
course, be able to enter more than a fraction. The information is often
meagre; and many, probably most, of these place-names are now
forgotten. But all the names were once places inKyaukse. And here let
me interject a warning. When Burmans spread from Kyaukse over
Burma, the same names were repeated in district after district. True
Burmese names nearly always mean something, often something
botanical; and you will find a Lakpaṁ rwā, a Ñonpańkr īand a Poktaw
pretty well everywhere in the plains. But card-index the names in
inscriptions, and you will find that Kyaukse is the progenitor.
I speak
from experience and after many mistakes. It is no good looking to
modern Pyinmana, or Toungoo, or Myingyan, or Tada-u, or Ngazun,
Nyaungyan, or Wayindok etc. for the oldest places of those names.
After laborious card-indexing you will trace them all back to Kyaukse

This, and other evidence of Mon prominence in Kyaukse, led me
to the conclusion that the first Burmans, fleeing from Nanchao,
probably crossed the Nam Tu at Hsipaw, taking their horses with them:
that they approached the plains via Lawksawk and Ye-ngan: and
suddenly descending the Nat-hteik Pass in the south-east corner of
the district, rapidly overran Kyaukse before many of the Mons could
get away to their relations in the south.
During the Historical Commission's recent tour, we visited Khan-
dáw village and Kinda weir at the foot of the Nat-hteik Pass, where
the Panlaung river emerges from the hills, and the first canal, the Nga
Laingzin, takes off a northerly direction. It is one off the grandest
views in Burma, doubly moving to the historian. We made careful,
enquiries and found that the pass is still in common use by the hillmen,
chiefly Danu and Shan traders, who come down early on bazaar-dayss
returning the same afternoon. The path is often rough; but hill-ponie-
and pack-bullocks can manage it: and if the double journey is hard,
going for one day, there is the convenient plateau of Kinlè half way up
where one can rest for the night without much risk of malaria.

The earliest extant Kyaukse inscription is the one in Old Mon,
No. XI, at the N.W. corner of Kyaukse Hill 94. It is engraved on a
special stone, not found at Pagan nor in Lower Burma, quarried from
Wébu Hill, near Kyaukse. Dr. Tha Hla tells me it is a dark mica schist;
and I hope he will tell us a little more about it at the conclusion of my
paper. It is a rough, brown-grey, glinting, laminated stone, which tends
to flake, seems never to take a smooth surface, and is always difficult to
read and (one would think) toe ngrave or carve. The Kyaukse Burmans
followed the Mons in using this stone for nearly all their earliest inscrip-
tions. Of the 44 faces previous to the fall of Pagan, 26 are on Wébu
stone, mostly dating before 600s. Nearly all the 18 others fall between
600 and 650s. The stone is quite distinctive, even in a photograph; and
it must have interested Bodawpaya's collectors: for they removed 20
out of the extant 30 Wébu stones to Amarapura, often, thank Heaven,
with a note of provenance scratched on them. They all come from
Kyaukse. They are usually of one face, pointed and not arched at the
top, with a triangular apex, and plain lines or bead panels above the
top of the inscription, sometimes also below it. There may be a leaf or
lotus border at top or bottom. The writing is generally in large square
letters, chipped or scratchy in effect, with constant tendency to flake.
Considering the difficulty of carving such a stone, one is surprised to
see how often attempts were made to do so.
Passing from Kyaukse to Pagan, from difficult Webu to easy
sandstone, one is all the more suprised to note how rarely design, apart
from lettering, is found on a Pagan slab. Was it that the Pagan

KYANZITTHA'S
Supplementary Note on
POSSIBLE CONNECTION WITH KYAUKSE
Kyanzittha's origins are notoriously obscure. The Mahārājavaň krī ;
(Vol. I, p. 236; Glass Palace Chronicle-tr., pp. 66-7) places his birth
at Parimma on the east bank of the Lower Chindwin, almost outside the
borders of Tambadipa. Its authority is the Parimma Htilaingshin
inscription (List 494, Sel. Inscr. Pagan, p. 3): mimi phwā rā Parim prañ
nhuik Dhiluin syań mankri ceti tañ e'. It is dated 469s./1107 A.D.,
within Kyanzittha's own reign (fl. 1084-1113 A D.), earlier than any
dated Burmese inscription. But both style and orthography show that it
belongs to the early Ava period, about 300 years later.
The Taungbyôn Hlèdauk inscription (List 50 a' Sel. Inscr. Pagan,
p. 4) makes him "a beloved son of King Noratha" mankri Norathā
sā khyac ta mū lañ phlac tha so Thiluin asyan Kalancacsă. The only
legible year-date is 472s./1110 A.D.: but there were probably others
on the flaked base, and kings Turukple and Tryāphyā are mentioned
(List 50 b 8-11 ); and once again the early Ava period is indicated by
style and spelling. Neither inscription (in my opinion) is valid for
determining Kyanzittha's origins. To get at these, we must confine our
attention (i) to his own inscriptions in Old Mon (and Pali); and (ii)
to the few references to him and his family in the early inscriptions in
Burmese.
He makes two mentions of his parentage, in Pali and in Mon: see
Epig. Birm. I, ii, pp. 162,167 (Inser. VIII B 16-Pali) and pp. 150-1
(Inscr. VI 23-24-Mon). His father was of the Sun family (ādiccavaṁsa ).
His mother was “born of the Bael-fruit stock” (veļubahaṇṇajā ), and
perhaps (the Mon passage is none too clear) "of the Egg (Kumbāy)
family, dwelling in the Womb of Bamboo (b.ñ dūñ)”. He appears to
hint at a higher legitimacy on the father's side, and miraculous conception
on the mother's. Historically, the essential fact is that he makes no
overt reference whatever to Aniruddha, and never mentions even his own
war-like exploits in his service, previous to his crushing of Ngaraman-
kan's rebellion. It was not on any connection with Aniruddha's young
line of parvenu kings that Kyanzittha based his claim to legitimacy :
but on an alleged prophecy of the Buddha, making him an incarnation of
Vishnu, the divine founder of the Pyu kingdom of S'ri Kṣetra. His reaction
against the autocratic ways of his great predecessor is very likely seen in

Kyanzittha's quarrel with Aniruddha was plainly very deep. There
is evidence enough, no doubt, in his inscriptions to show that he was
susceptible to feminine charms: but his love-affair with the gift-princess,
whom Aniruddha had not even seen, seems hardly sufficient reason for
the implacable feud between them. Was its root really, one wonders, feud
between Kyaukse and Pagan?-early Burmese chiefs who conquered
Kyaukse (we have Fan Ch'o's word for it), were called man. They
would hardly have given up their rights without a struggle. Was
Kyanzittha the last of the Kyaukse man to maintain his title against the
monarchical pretensions of Pagan? - If so, there would be strong reason
for the feud. If Thiluin was but a minor kingship buried in the large
kharuin, this would only make his struggle the more heroic. And if one
adds the feeling of ingratitude, which Kyanzittha must have felt
towards a king whose battles he had fought, from Nan-chao to the
Isthmus of Kra, one can understand his refusal either to compromise
or to forgive. Loyal he remained to the end: he was willing to risk his
life to save Aniruddha's son, but not to bate one jot of his ancestral
rights. One can understand also his resolve, that shines through his
inscriptions, to unite Burma by consent rather than by force.
There is much non-proven in the above suggestions; but perhaps.
they merit consideration.
