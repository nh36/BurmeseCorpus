1. COSMOGONIC MYTHS.
Dr. Ba Han **
To primitive man, nature wore a
mysterious aspect. His strange surroun-
dings and the sky above lit by heavenly
bodies must have provoked massive
feelings of wonder and awe. The question
of his survival was at once a challenge
and a dilemma. To begin with he was
at the periphery of thought and under-
standing. With the gradual growth of
his power of observation there were
stirrings of interest about the nature of
the universe and his position in it. And
questions began to crowd into his mind:
Why things were as they were? And why
they occured? As answers to these ques-
tions, cosmogonic myths arose regarding
the origin of the universe. They are
gropings towards the meaning of existence
and they attempt to explain the formation,
of the world from the fewest possible
elements.
Amidst the spectrum of cosmogonic
speculations of the ancient world, Hindu
thought stands out shiningly. It boldly
asserted the concept of creation ex nihilo.
In a Vedic hymn¹ occurs this striking
statement:
“In the primal age of the gods being
was born of non-being." (Italics mine.) This
is a palmary contribution to the theory of
the origin of the universe?
The prose section of the Vedas known
as the Brahmanas which expounds the
Vedic hymns intruded into the realm of
cosmogonic and cosmological myths. The
Upanishads which grew side by side with
the Brahmanas developed theories of the
world and the soul going far beyond
the teaching of the Vedas³. While
retaining a great deal of the Vedic imagery
and conceptions, they taught that 'the
world not merely originated from ONE:
it is one. It is Brahma, and Brahma
is self 4.
In the entire universe there is a
fundamental unity of substance. The
divine pervades everywhere. God is the
source of light that animates everythings.
Salvation consists in the realisation that
the individual self is identical with
Brahma®.

It would therefore be well to pause

Buddhism does not look upon the cosmos
as something created and upheld by the
power of the Primal Cause. It contents

orderly sequence of things (dhammata) in

as the eternal process (niyama) of the

The past is beyond ken. There is no
attempt to probe further with a view to
discover the design of the Creator. Thought
shrinks from tracing things to their very
origin. The reason advanced is this.
Knowledge of such matters does not con-
duce to progress in sanctification. Nor
does it lead to enlightenment.

4. BUDDHIST COSMOLOGY.
In regard to cosmology, Buddhism has
developed its own system with the primary
object of undermining the Hindu world
theory which has as its pivot Brahma, the
One, the Unborn who is above all and is
present in all created things.
The universe of Burmese cosmology is
made up of innumerable worlds or spheres
(setkyavala) each of which has its own
earth, sun, moon, heaven and hell. These
worlds are separated from one another by
hells.
The centre of our world is Mount
Meru, where the gods (devas) dwell. The
sun circles round it. Seven concentric
mountain ranges girdle the earth, and seven
great rivers (sita) intervene between these
ranges. Beyond them lies the vast ocean
with four great islands named after the
huge trees that grow on them as their
insignia. These islands are UTTARA-
KURU in the north, ZAMBUDĪPA in the
south, PUBBAVIDEHA in the east and

6. DESTRUCTION AND
REAPPEARANCE OF
THE WORLD.
The entire universe is never destroyed.
A periodical destruction of the world up to
the heavenly realms of the Brahmas takes
place. The destruction is sometimes by
water, at times by fire and at other times by
wind. A new cycle then emerges phoenix-
like out of the ruins of the old world.
Buddhist cosmogony proceeds to narrate
how our present world appeared as the
latest cycle out of the chaos which
punctuates recurrent cycles and how some
of the denizens (Brahmas) of the formless
world (arupaloka) came to people the new
cycle.
