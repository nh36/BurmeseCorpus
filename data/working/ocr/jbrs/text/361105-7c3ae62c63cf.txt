TO THE YOUNGER
GENERATION
66
For two years I have had the honour of being
President of this Society, and it gave me rather a shock
the other day to hear that one of the younger genera-
tion had referred to it as dead and gone". I suppose
he regarded us as a set of old fogeys. Well, I admit
being an old fogey. And I want to take the privilege
of an old fogey to say a few words to the young. I
dont want to say much about what we have been doing.
You will find that in the Annual Report which the
Secretary will read. But I want to explain what we
are trying to do. That is why I want to speak to
the young, because it is the young who will have to
do it. And I want to make at least a few of the young
generation realize that it is worth doing.
We are trying to do two things. We are trying
to make a useful contribution to the cultural, economic
and political progress of Burma. That is one thing.
And we are trying to help Burma to assume a due
place in the international community of the modern
world. That is our second object. But they both go
together. We cannot achieve our second object unless
we achieve the first. And if we achieve the first we
shall in the same process achieve the second.
I say we hope to contribute to the cultural,
economic and political progress of Burma, and I wish
to stress the word political. How was it that formerly
Burma lost its independence? It is easy to lay the
blame on imperialists and capitalists. But they could
not have succeeded if Burma had been able to stand
up for itself. It could not stand up for itself because
its rulers would not face the challenge of the outside
world. It lost its independence because it was not
capable of independence. Now Burma is once again
independent. But one condition of its independence
is that it shall keep up with the modern world. It

For this it must be equipped with the machinery of
social progress. And at present, owing to the circum-

.
In all aspects of life we are faced with innu-
merable difficult problems. The progress of Burma,
and even its survival as an independent nation, depends
on our finding satisfactory solutions for these problems.
There is an urgent need and infinite scope for research.
Many of the problems resemble those found elsewhere,
especially those in neighbouring lands. But almost all
of them, except so far as they are purely technical-and
very few are purely technical have features that are
peculiar to Burma. I believe in getting all the help
we can from foreign experts. But in most problems,
whether in agriculture or education or anything else,
the chief practical difficulty consists in applying general
principles to local conditions and the local people. Here
foreign experts can do little to help us for lack of
local knowledge. Only too often they are likely to
mislead us. We must look to Burmans for the solution
of Burma's problems. And unfortunately we have at
present very few Burmans capable of tackling them.
new
We have, of course, the university. Before the
war however this was cramped by the economic envi-
ronment. Now it is taking advantage of its
opportunities and turning out in increasing numbers
graduates competent to undertake research. And we
are sending an increasing number of men abroad who
should come back better able to undertake research.
But few people will undertake research without some
prospect of publishing their results. We are trying to
encourage research and to provide facilities for publica-
tion either in our Journal or in an annual Conference
of which the first was held last year. That is what
we are trying to do. So far as we succeed we shall
contribute to social progress in Burma and at the same
time put Burma on the map in the world of modern
learning. We aim at doing something to equip Burma
with the machinery of social progress.

Can we do it? For that we must look to the
young generation. May I venture on a little personal
history. It is forty five years since a chance reading
of a Journal of the Siam Society inspired me with the
idea of founding a similar society in Burma. I was then
just thirty, which now seems to me very young. It is
to the young men of about thirty, especiully to those
who have returned from foreign study that we must look
if the Society is to do what it might do for the
advancement of Burma. And one further point. It
was almost at the same time that I brought back with
me from Singapore some notes on the Raffles Library
and Museum in the hope that they might come in
useful in building up a similar institution in Burma.
Now at last they have come in useful after more
than forty years. Now we have at least the name of
such an institution. That is my second point. You
will see from the list of members whom we are recom-
mending for election to our Committee that we hope
to strengthen it by representatives of the younger
generation. To them in particular I would say "Do
not be weary in well-doing”. If nothing comes of
it, you will at least have the satisfaction of having
tried to do something. I hope you will try. And
I hope you will succeed in making our Society an
instrument of social progress.
-
Now I will ask the Secretry to read our annual
report for the past year.
10 March, 1953.
J. S. Furnivall.
