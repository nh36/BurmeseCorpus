The Most Up-to-date and Costly Book
1. Of all the books on Burma ever
written and published in English, the most
recent, the most up-to-date, the most
comprehensive, the most enlightening, the
most informative and interesting, the most
complete and, indeed the most costly, is
Dr. Luce's Magnum Opus. It is a big
book of three giant volumes published in
1969 for Artibus Asiae and the Institute of
Fine Arts, New York University. Old
Burma-Early Pagan is it's name and it's
author is Gordon H. Luce (M.A., D. Litt.,
C.B.E. and I E.S. Retired).
Author's Qualification & Experience
2. As one closely connected with the
author himself for nearly half a century
I can sincerely say with intimate knowledge
if not with absolute authority that the
history of Burma, with reference to South
East Asia and the Far East, is the author's
life-long study. Dr. Luce was lecturer in
English language and literature in Rangoon
College and Rangoon University from
1912 to 1924; lecturer in Burmese History
and Far Eastern History at the Rangoon
University from 1924 to 1942 and Professor
of Burmese History and Far Eastern
History in the same University from 1946
to 1958; a compiler of Early Burmese
History in the Burma Historical Com-
mission from 1958 to 1965. All these
appointments held by him facilitated him
to devote full time and he did exert all his
energy to undertake extensive and intensive
research to compile a
comprehensive
history of Burma, with reference to other
parts of Asia, particularly, India, China,
Indo-China, Malaysia, Indonesia and
Ceylon. A worthy writer on the suitable
subject has produced a worthy work-a
veritable encyclopaedia of things Burmese.
The reader will find in Dr. Luce's Master-
piece the origin and development of art
and architecture, sculpture and painting,
language and literature, iconography and
museology, numismatics and ceremics,
flora and fauna and last but not the least
palaeography and or hography in old
Burma, particularly during the Pagan
period. On the production of such an
excellent publication enta ling enormous
expenditure, the publishers and financiers
also are to be very heartily congratulated
besides the author himself:
Reason for this Review
3. The book is sure to be adequately
appreciated by all sincere scholars of history
and revered by all old pupils of the author.
It would certainly run into subsequent
editions. Necessary it is, therefore, that
constructive criticisms be given so that
improvements can be made by the author
in his next edition. My pains-taking perusal
of the text volume has revealed to me that
his big book, brimful of great and good
virtues, is sadly fraught with quite a few
faults, particularly, in connection with a
major episode in history; leave alone
mistakes of minor measure.

Môn, the pioneers in Civilisation
6. "The pioneers in civilization,
both in Old Burma and Old Siam", states
Dr. Luce in his Foreward, "were the
Môns. Strongest in the deltas near the
coast, where they grew their irrigated rice,
they had contacts with India from very
early times. Their language, which is akin
to Khmer and the pre-Vietnamese dialects
of the Annamite Chain, is distinctly allied
to Indonesian and quite different from
Burmese and Pyu, which are Tibeto-Bur-
man.'

Precedent regarding Repairs of ruins
79. Once they had gained glory, our
mighty monarchs longed for longevity of
life. From the Theravada Pāli piṭakas,
they all know that repairs made to ruined
religious structures strengthen their physi-

therefore, was to repair such of his
predeccessor's pagodas as were in precari-
ous condition at Siripaccaya. Having
done that merit-making act of mainte-
nance to the two monuments close to his
capital city at the 'primary port of Pagán”,
he thought of doing the same in the
south of his domain, at an old capital
city of the local Môns. Hence, the
restoration of the Maungdi monument
should be dated later than the year he
did similarly to the two Hpetleik temples.
It was the self-same motive that makes
the same Sovereign to repair and restore
the ruined Nwatalè Solid Stupas of brick
at Nga-O village, on the N. bank of the
mid waters of the Shweli river. At least
