SOME PAPAL CORRESPONDENCE WITH THE KINGS OF BURMA
Translated from the Latin and commented by
Vivian Ba ( A Tavoyan in Paris )
In the 18th Century, King Taninganwe
sent the Catholic Priest, Father Vittoni to
the Pope in Rome with token presents of
Burmese precious gems, requesting the Pope
to send technicians for the advancement
of his Kingdom. Mon and Burmese
monarchs helped the Catholic Priests and
gave them grants of royal land for building
schools and Churches. It was during the
reign of King Mindon that very close
diplomatic contacts were made with Rome
and the several Embassies to the Roman
pontiff, the letters in the Vatican Archives.
the Burmese royal gifts in the Vatican
Museum attest to this fact. I have been
able to procure copies of the Pope to
various Burmese Kings and the replies of
Burmese Kings to them through the kind
help of Cardinal Marella whom I had known
in Paris as the Papal Nuncio. But it may be
useful to add here that the archives of the
reign of Pope Pius IX, the Pope with whom
King Mindon had close diplomatic contacts
are still under 'secret' and only at the end
of each pontificate is one part of the
archives opened to the public. We have not
yet reached the opening of the archives
pertaining to the period of Pius IX and
special permission must therefore be
obtained to consult the papers of that
Pope's reign. I am grateful also to the
Secretary of State of the Vatican who
allowed me to have copies of certain letters
which were carefully despatached to me
through the Papal Nunciature in Paris.
Latin is a language with very long
phrases and sentences and much is lost by
translation. Every word and phrase in the
papal letters is well thought out and with

Letters or Briefs of Popes to Kings are
classified after the introductory words in the
first paragraph. Thus the letter of Pope
Gregory XVI to King Tharrawaddy is called:
"Certiores Facti." Pope Pius IX wrote two
letters to King Mindon the "Summa
Quidem" and the "Imperialis Maiestatis.'
The Summa Quidem was sent to King
Mindon when Father Paul Abbona who had
come to Europe with the First Burmese
Embassy to France with General Maha d'
Orgoni returned to Burma in 1857. Fr Abbona
was a trusted confidant of King Mindon. The
King always asked him for advice in diploma-
tic matters. Several British envoys mention
him. He always believed that the heart and
soul of Burma should be where the King was
(in Mandalay and not in Rangoon where the
British were.) He was often criticised as
being too extremely pro-Mindon. Anquetil
writing in “Le Monde" Friday 23 August,
1867 goes so far as to say that the British
calumniated this missionary seeing how much
weight the King gave to his advice. The same
writer extols the priest's holiness. He accom-
panied several embassies of King Mindon to
Europe, took Burmese state scholars to Italy.
Father Abbona played a great part in the
Burmese Convention with Italy in 1871.
Phayre also mentions Abbona and so does
Dalhousie.4

The first European to write a detailed
life of the Buddha with His teachings
according to Burmese Theravada Buddhism
was Bishop Bigandet. In his Buddhistic
researches he was encouraged by King Min-
don who also helped him materially in
building churches and schools, lent him royal
launches, elephants, and other transport
during his missionary tours and also used
his good services in diplomatic missions to
the British Commissioner of Rangoon, to
the French Government and the Pope. Bishop
Bigandet says of King Mindon in his History,
"It is well to consign in these pages a
fact which does honour to the King of
Burmah. Since his accession to the throne
in 1852, the reigning sovereign has shewn
a real disposstion in assisting the missionary
resident in the Royal city...etc. When the
Bishop came to visit the Mission, the King
liberally gave him money to defray his
travelling expenses, and supplied him with
elephants when he had to travel by land, and
boats when he had to go by river. He
likewise gave him a few fine pieces of timber
for rebuilding the tower of the Church of
Rangoon. When some missionaries came for
the benefit of their health to the royal city

Bishop Bigandet is known in Burma as
a Cathoic prelate, philantoropist and scholar.
His masterly works on religion, anthropo-
logy, customs and folklore of Burma and
Malaya are indeed very learned works.
During his Golden Jubilee celebrations he
said:
"Now I reap a hundredfold the fruit
that has grown from the handfuls of seed
that I have planted during the fifty years of
my pilgrimage in these parts, which have
become my home. I feel quite happy to
have exchanged the West for the East,
Europe for Asia, France for Burma. After
half a century I can state without hesitation
that my choice has truly been a good one
and a source of pleasurable reminiscience.”
When Bishop Bigandet died on the 19th
March, 1894 in the 18 year of his age and
38th year of his episcopate, twenty thousand
people accompanied him to his last resting
place on Burmese soil.³
