THE ROLE OF LITERATURE IN NATION BUILDING *
(With special reference to Burma)

Literature is one of the major con-
tributory factors in the building of a nation.
Its roles are many and varied. The three
main roles it often plays among people
with a common tradition may be mention-
ed: (1) as a cultural binding force;
(2) as an evocative medium to boost up
the moral of a people in the time of
crisis; (3) as a means of arousing the
patriotism of the people under a foreign
rule. My paper deals with the role that
comes under the third category.
Nation building is a long and slow
process in which there are bound to be
many ups and downs. It is generally
accepted that the Burmese people emerged
as a nation sometime in the 11th century
A.D. They had their own state, their
own king. They had to struggle for the
preservation of their sovereignity. They
had their moments of glory as well as
humility, and peace as well as strife in
the course of their history. Their darkest
hours seem to be during the period be-
tween A.D. 1886 and 1948.
After three successive Anglo-Burmese
wars, in 1824-26, 1852 and 1885-86 the
Burmese monarchy came to an end, and
the whole of Burma passed into the hands
of the British. In 1942 during the Second
World War, the country was overrun by
the Japanese, and at the end of the war
in 1945, it became a British possession
again. But Burma regained her Indepen-
dence in 1948.
I propose to present here the aspect
of the literature-political writing-that ¡pla-

Background before the 1st World Wer
From the beginning of the history
until the end of the independent Burmese
kingdom in 1885, the Burmese people
knew no kind of political adminis ration
except the rule of an absolute monarch.
Therefore although there has been Bur-
mese writing since the twelfth century,
there were, to my knowledge, no substan-
tial political writings of any sort until
Saya Lun's time. The king's power was
so absolute and effective that the question
of political rights does not seem to have
exercised many people's minds. Apart

of five traditional great enemies of man-
water, fire, king, thief and a disliked per-
son¹-so it is no wonder that philosophis-
ing on the nature of the kingship was
avoided and political awareness was neg-
ligible.

Literature and the Press
The earliest Burmese writings appeared
in a simple prose form on stone inscrip-
tions recording dedications in the Pagan
Period (1044-1288 A D. ). Apart from
inscriptions, the earliest extant specimens
of Burmese literature date from the 15th
century, 4 having originated under the
auspices of the king and his faith, that
is, Buddhism. Like most of the early
21
literatures, it looked to the court for
appreciation and patronage and wrote
mostly eulogies of the reigning monarch.
Its distinguishing qualities were therefore
puritanism of thought and courtly refine-
ment of language. Another feature was
its heavy borrowing form the Jataka, the
stories of the Buddha's previous existences,
mostly in the from of Pyo (Pyui.).5 In
addition, her were some accounts of
national events and deeds of the king's
ancestors in verse, mostly in two different
forms, that is, Mawgun (Môkwan:)7 and
E-gyin (E:khyan:), together with shorter
nature and love poems, that is, mainly in
the form of Yadu (Ratu)³.
This pattern remained unchanged up
to the end of the 18th century, when closer
relations were formed between Burma and
the outside world. Thus with greater
contact growing intimacy with other
countri´s, Burma began to emerge from
her isolation. One of the results can be
seen in the field of literature. Romances
and plays which were brought thence to
Burma from another country gave an
impetus to the writing of secular literature.
For example, the Ramayana and other
similar stories were treated was in Burmese
verse in a more light-hearted way than had
been possible with the Jataka10. Then in
the first half of the 19th century there
were the playwrights U Kyin U and

Court.11 Their plays were produc d as

1914-1920
When World War 1 broke out in
1914 its direct impact on the develop-
ment of political consciousness in Burma
was relatively slight. Nevertheless, the
indirect effect of the war was important,
for it did much to break down Burma's
provincialism and to extend the political
horizons of many of its people. It de-
monstrated, among other things, that
British power was not invincible.17 The
Burmese people also heard about Allied
war aims; they took cognisance of Presi-
dent Wilson's statements about self-
determination. They became aware of
political developments in India; and at
the end of war, Burma found itself carried
along in the wake of a major revolu-
tionary upheaval and constitutional reform
programme centering in India. Thus,
World War 1 clearly constituted a divid-
ing point in Burma's political history.

The introduction of the printing

them in his message. If it had not
