FIRST ITALIAN CONTACTS WITH BURMA
Several travellers from that peninsula we now
call Italy have through the centuries either visited,
lived or passed through the kingdoms of Ava and
Pegu. Some lived only a short time in Burma but
their lengthy accounts of the Land of Pagodas and
Elephants and sparkling gems are travel books
translated today into many languages. Several of
them befriended the learned sayadaws and studied
Pali and Burmese in order to read the chronicles
and monastery manuscripts. Most of these learned
Italians who went to the very sources of Burmese
history and traditions were of the learned society
of the Barnabites and the Oblates of Mary
Immaculate. After them came the Milan
missionaries but none has yet surpassed a Percoto
or a Sangermano; not forgetting of course
Mantega, Cortenovis, d'Amato. Gallo, Balma,
Ceretti, Abbona and a galaxy of others.
One by one, the names of Marco Polo, Nicolo
da conti, Girolamo Adorno, Girolamo de San
Stefano, Gasparo Balbi, Ludovico da Varthema,
learned philosophers; geographers. linguists,
naturalists, mathematicians from the teaching
society of the Barnabites and the equally learned
congregation of the Pinerolo Oblates. All these
Italians served King Mindon faithfully. Then
follow names of Italian military, naval and medical
officers who served King Thibaw.
But in the diplomatic history of King
Mindon's reign none has left so lasting and imprint
as the Piedmontese Paolo Abbona and his friend in
the Sardinian Foreign Office, Cristoforo Negri,
who though he had never set foot on Burmese soil,
did all he could through the services of his
countryman Father Paolo Abbona to strengthen the
ties of friendship and mutual respect and
reciprocity by treaty. The first attempts of these
two patriots bore fruit only under the Italy of
Victor Emmanuel. Negri being the founder
president of the Italian Geographical Society, it
was only natural that he opened his archives,
library and documentation section to Burmese
collections and the gathering of data on Burma.
The letters and reports of the Barnabites and the
Oblates are a mine of information on Burmese
affairs of the 17th, 18th, and 19th centuries and
though some of then have been studied by fellow

But it already seems that we have contributed
to the opening of a way for commerce between
Burma and West China. Transiting through Burma
on the Irrawaddy we would shorten greatly the
trade route of the Indo-Europeans with West
China. Rangoon would become the great port of
that trade and the Irrawaddy would be the way for

Paolo ABBONA
In several other papers and articles I have
termed Paolo Abbona: The most Burmese minded
of the Catholic missionaries.
This Oblate of Pinerolo completely
assimilated his person with Burmese social life
and politics. He entered himself to the King by
chewing beetle with him and squaring on the floor
while interpreting and writing letters for him. He
often excused the defects he found in those who
surrounded him and if European historians termed
the Burmese as 'gentiles' or 'uneducated' it was he
who took it upon himself to prove the contrary. He
was even against some pages of Sangermano and
his own oblate Gallo for some chapters which
looked at the Burmese through European eyes and
judged them according to European ethics. Even
though he was imprisoned during Pagan Min's
reign he found reasons to excuse the step taken by
the Burmese police. He blames his fellow
missionary prisoners for feasting too noisily thus
attracting the notice of other townspeople who
considered the Christian New Year festivities to be
exclamations of joy at the arrival of the English in
Rangoon. For his fidelity and Burmese
mindedness he was made adviser to the King, had
free access to his presence, was paid monthly by
the King's treasury, was sent on several diplomatic
missions to the British and to Europe, was
entrusted with Royal pages for education in Italy
and during his stay in Europe received a regular
allowance from the Burmese court.

In the Treaty concluded on the 3rd March
1871 of the Christian era between their Majesties
the Sovereigns of Italy and of Burma, articles 10
and 11 of the version Italian was not sufficiently
clear. It is specified there in that Italian subjects
who come to trade in Burma cannot sell neither

articles 10 and 11 refer only arms and munitions
of war. His Excellency having asked me to
formulate and give him in writing my verbal
explanations here is my replay:
In the text of the Treaty in the Burmese
language the prohibition contained in articles 10 and
11 is said not to be extended other than to arms and
munitions of war; therefore with the excepting of
arms and munitions of war of any kind, the subjects
of both nations can sell buy all the merchandises
which they want without asking any permission
whatsoever.
