Description of Nissaya
Meaning of Nissaya
In exploring the literary field of Burma, whether Pali or Burmese, the influence of
the former in the growth of the latter cannot be overlooked. Since the advent of the
Theravada form of Buddhism in Burma, or rather, since the settlement of the Burmans in
the country where Theravada Buddhism had already flourished, the people had been
familiar not only with its teaching but also with its medium of propagation - Pali. It is
only natural therefore that they have borrowed and used Pali words, some in their original
forms and some modified to express certain religious and philosophical ideas that are
embodied in the religion which they have so fondly adopted. Their own language therefore
abounds in terms that are closely connected with Buddhism. In the course of time these
“loan words" acquired new meanings in addition to their original ones. "We may say,"
said Dr. M. Bode, the author of 'Pali Literature of Burma', "that the essential Indian
genius, the psychological subtleties, and high thoughts of Buddhism have forced the
Burmese language to grow, deepen and expand continually.” To this, we may add that
along with the Burmese language a literature developed that contains, among others, a
considerable amount of Pali words, derivatives, idioms and ideas.
Like Pali, Sanskrit also has had its impact felt in Burma although to a lesser degree.
This ancient Indian language, according to scholars, was introduced into Burma even
before Pali. 2 That it found most enthusiastic students in ancient Burma for sometime
even after the introduction and well establishment of Theravāda, is irrefutable. Later,
however, its attraction to the people became smaller; the chief reason probably is that the
majority of scholars, as devout followers of Theravāda, had been absorbed in Pali studies.
Sanskrit learning thus has been confined only to the few who realise that the knowledge
of Sanskrit is essential to the studying of Pali. What we want to mention
here is the impact of Sanskrit which is evident by "the fact of existence in
the Burmese language of a number of Sanskrit words, both derived and
naturalised, importing not only terms in religion and mythology but also those
relating to social life."3 We find in the Burmese language "loan words" from Sanskrit and
Pali both denoting the same meaning. In some cases, Sanskrit derivatives or naturalised
words are preferred to their Pali counterparts.

It is not surprising therefore that we have the word nissaya in Burmese a direct
borrowing from Pali, as well as the word nissaraññ: whose form suggests that it was
derived from Sanskrit nisraya. Its meaning however is that of the Pali nissaya. It means:
(1) 'that on which anything depends', as in nei? Jǝ`yi shaya (nissayācariya), 'a teacher
cn whom a pupil depends', and
(2) ‘help, support, guidance or tutelage' as in nei” fə`yi yuði (nissayaṁ gaṇhāti), ‘to
take help', or nei? Ja`yi `peði (nissayam dadāti) 'to give help'.
The Burmese equivalent of the word nissaya or nissaraññ: is amhi which generally means
“anything to lean on, a thing that affords help'.
Since its introduction into Burmese the word nissaya has acquired new meanings.
They are:
(1) method (Pali naya) as in `ni na nei? ŋəya' (nissaya),
(2) meaning (Pali attha) as in pa? ənɛ? for pa? nei? ŋəya' (Pāṭha nissaya), and
(3) translation as in nei? ŋəya' pyan sho ði // ənɛ? faŋbaŋði.
It is in this last meaning that we shall discuss the word in its many aspects.
Now there are two kinds of translation in Burmese, namely, (a) direct rendering,
and (b) Nissaya rendering. Direct rendering is what is understood by the word translation
in its proper sense, i.e. a continuous, unbroken change of words from one language to
another. As such it needs no comment, and we propose to deal with the other, i.e.
nissaya translation.
Burmese scholars do not seem to have a clear conception of what a nissaya rendering
means. They have a vague notion that any form of translation which has Pali text
interspersed with Burmese translation is a nissaya. The usual feature is Pali words or
phrases, each followed by its Burmese equivalent. The translation may be faithful´ or
elaborate or free and in some cases it verges on paraphrasing. To give an idea of this
kind of translation, here is a couple of lines in Pali from the Dhammapada' followed by
its nissaya rendering in Burmese².
Sukaram sadhunā sādhum, sādhum papena dukkaram;
Papam papena sukaram,papamariyehi dukkaram.

This kind of translation is not peculiar to the countries of the Theravada school.
Although Tibetan or Chinese nissaya is not known to scholars, there are examples of the
type among the “Tocharian” fragments, from a much earlier period. Similar works in
Khotanese also have been found by scholars, and these belong to the Mahāyāna School.
It seems, however, that the Theravādins are more acquainted with this type of translation.
As regards the Buṛmese, they simply feel at home with it. It is not difficult for them to
read through and understand the nissaya without being distracted by the presence of Pali
text.
In Burmese, there are synonyms, apart from nissaraññ: for the word nissaya. The
name amhi (aid) is as old as nissaraññ: as they are mentioned together in the Taungdwin
Governor's Inscription dated A.F. 1442. But it is now rarely used. A more common
word, anak (meaning) is used for translation as well as for the title of nissaya. For the
Abhidhamma works we have a special name, akauk or ayakauk (abstract). A general term
for the nissaya, however, is naṁ sa or nam̃ saung (glossary); thus naṁ kyin is a concise
translation and naṁ kye an elaborate one.
Style of Nissaya
The nissaya can be roughly divided into 4 categories according to their style, viz.,
(1) Verbatim translation,
(2) Free translation,
(3) Ornate translation, and
(4) Translation with short notes.
(1) Verbatim translation: This is often known as word for word translation. Most
probably it is the earliest form of nissaya. In this translation a Pali word is followed by
its exact equivalent in Burmese, devoid of all its trimmings. It is the nissaya in its
simplest form, giving the impression of a glossary. Most of the Abhidhamma texts,
especially the Abhidhammatthasangaha, and the Vinaya works that are full of technical
terms, are translated in this style. The following is an extract from the Kankhävitarani
nissava, translated into English.

four, Nissagyiyavatthūni Nissaggiyavatthus, iti such as, suvanṇa-gold, rajatam=
silver, kahāpaṇo: money, māsako= coins. Navaṭṭanti- It is not proper,

(2) Free translation: The next step in translating Pali texts, perhaps, is this style.
Fundamentally the difference between this and the first is in degree rather than in kind.
The translator of this style does not adhere to the exact meaning of the original words.
Instead he takes liberty with the words. He translates in elaborate style mainly to show
his proficiency of the language and his knowledge of the subject. Sometimes the style is
so flowery that the exact meaning of the original word becomes obscure, and one cannot
see the tree for the wood. This style is usually adopted in translating popular suttas and
prayers. To illustrate the points we have made, we have appended two translations, the

Many an attentive reader of this nissaya cannot help detecting that certain
explanations given by Ariyālaṁkāra bear a very close resemblence to those in the
Sumangalavilāsinī, the commentary on the Digha Nikaya by Buddhaghosa. It is true
that in many places Ariyālankara's expositions are fuller and in some much shorter,
and in others Ariyālaṁkāra even deals with those Pali words which Buddhaghosa has
left unexplained. And in some cases Ariyālaṁkāra even expresses his disagreemet
with the interpretations given by Buddhaghosa on certain words or phrases. Neverthe-
less, the similarity is significant. It is also to be noted that Ariyālaṁkāra himself was
the author of a nissaya on the Sumangalavilāsinī.
A brief appraisal of Ariyālaṁkāra as a nissaya writer may be made here. There
is ample evidence that he believes in brevity and clarity of style, which he employs most
effectively in this nissaya, and that he is making an attempt to avoid the use of Pali loan
words, a phenomenon often seen in nissaya works in Burma.
This nissaya, which has been looked upon as a learned work, is not however free
from a few blemishes. To mention only two: one is too faithful a rendering of the
Pali into Burmese; this often gives rise to ambiguity. The other is inconsistencies in
rendering certain Pali words or expressions into Burmese. The explanation for the
'slip of the style' may be found in Burmese chronicles which asserts that he was one of
those writers who put down what he wanted to say on palm-leaves without first drafting
on Parabeik (folded paper book) with a steatite pencil.
