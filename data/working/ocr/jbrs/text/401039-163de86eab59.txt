Introduction
1
Two years ago, as a student of Taxonomic Botany, under the
advice, guidance and constructive criticism of Professor Ko Ko Lay,
I became so much interested in the subject that a great desire to know
more compelled me to collect specimens of family Verbenaceae found
within Rangoon-Insein Area. In addition to the collections I made,
several hundred sheets of specimens in the Herbarium of the Forest
Department enabled me to obtain the indispensable information about
these plants. I have used standard taxonomic method in preparing
this study.
Morphology
The species of Clerodendron are a diverse group of woody plants
comprising of trees, shrubs, undershrubs, or climbers with either thick
or slender stems. C. griffithianum is a tree, C. thomsonae and its
hybrid C. speciosum are climbers and the others are shrubs or under-
shrubs.
Younger stems are usually quadrangular, with either a shallow or
deep longitudinal groove along each surface. The older ones are more
or less cylindrical, or tending to be slightly angled near the node.
Small, white or large and prominent brown lenticels are usually present
on the bark.
The leaves are simple and unicostate or multicostate. They are
mostly opposite, except in C. siphonanthus, C. neriifolium, and
C. serratum where they are whorled. In rare cases, the arrangement
of leaves may vary even in plants of the same species such as
C. siphonanthus and C. serratum where they are mostly whorled and
occasionally opposite. They are sessile or sub-sessile as in C. sipho-
nanthus and C. macrosiphon, while the rest are petiolate. C. infortu-
natum, C. villosum, C. colebrookianum, C. lasiocephalum, C. fragrans

less than 9 cm. wide possess short and slender ones. The petioles
usually have a longitudinal groove along the adaxial surface.
The leaf shape, lobing and size are good definitive characters for
most of the species, as they show so little variation. The margins are
entire or irregularly toothed. In C. colebrookianum the lower leaves
are serrated and the upper ones are entire. C. macrosiphon has both
entire and coarsely toothed leaves irregularly mixed on the same plant.
In some species the leaves are glandular. C. paniculatum and C. squa-
matum have small rounded glands scattered all over the lower surface,
while in C. colebrookianum and C. fragrans and its var. pleniflorum,
4-6 myrmecophylous glands are found at the base of the lamina.
Because of their extreme constancy they offer an easy means of
identifying the species.
The leaves are either densely tomentose with stiff hairs as in
C. infortunatum. or villose as in C. villosum, or glabrous as in
C. inerme, or densely ferruginous-pubescent as in C. lasiocephalum.
The nature of hariness is a good primary character in certain species.
However, the use of this character requires great judgement as the
pubescence changes considerably during the course of development.
Younger leaves may be covered rather densely with minute soft hairs
while the mature ones on the same plant are devoid of them as in
C. colebrookianum.
The inflorescences are mostly terminal; the exception is in the
case of C. inerme where it consists of an axillary 3-flowered cyme
borne at the tip of the primary peduncle. The terminal inflorescences
of most of the species are made of compound paniculate cymes. The
lateral cyme usually arise by dichasial branching and each in turn may
branch once or twice and pass into either typical dichasium as in
C. infortunatum, or a cincinus as in C. thomsonae, or a bostryx as in
C. villosum, The primary peduncle may either be clongate as in
C. paniculatum, or condense as in C. lasiocephalum. The inflorescence
as a whole is rather large and spreading. The size of the inflorescence
and the number of pairs of secondary peduncles are diagnostic for
most species.
The bracts on primary peduncles are usually prominent and
follacious. Those in C. griffithianum and C. calamitosum are large

and leaf-like at the base, but their size gradually decrease towards the
tip of the peduncle. The bracts on secondary and tertiary peduncles,
except in C. speciosum, are usually small and non-foliac ous.
A considerable range in the size of the flower is noticed among
the species. At about anthesis, the floral buds of C. siphonanthus and
C. macrosiphon are as large as 8-12 cm. in length, while those of
C. serratum rarely exceed 2 cm. The size of the flower is generally
a good character for most species.
The floral buds of C. serratum and C. macrosiphon are markedly
circinate, while the majority of the remaining species have erect or
slightly curved ones.
The calyx affords a good specific character. The length of the
calyx is generally constant for a given species. C. lasiocephalum and
C. fragrans and its var. pleniflorum possess the longest, usually 2.2-2.4
cm. long, while C. serratum, C. paniculatum and C. squamatum have
less than 0.5 cm. in length. They are either deeply lobed as in
C. calamitosum or truncate or minutely toothed as C. serratum and
C. neriifolium. The presence of glands on sepals may also be used as a
primary character for identifying C. infortunatum, C. villosum,
C. colebrookianum C. lasiocephalum, and C. fragrans and its var.
pleniflorum. As with the pubescense of the leaves, its use in the identi-
fication of the species needs caution.
The corolla is tubular and the tube is slightly dilated towards the
throat. In the two climbers, C. thomsonae and C. speciosum the
bases of the tubes are also dilated. The tube of C. colebrookianum is
densely hairy within. There is a slight posterior slit on the tube at the
throat. Just about anthesis, the slit, because of resupination becomes
anterior in position. The lobes are mostly equal or sub-equal and
tend to make the flower bilabiate. This irregularity offers a good
distinguishing character for certain species like C. macrosiphon and
C. serratum.
The species have didynamous stamens. The length of the free
filaments vary very little from species to species. Therefore, it is
relatively useless as a definitive character. The filaments are attached
on the corolla tube slightly below the throat. They are long exserted
and slightly recurved. The anthers are 2-thecous, dorsifixed with the
lobes united only towards the tip.

The ovaries may be smooth as in C. infortunatum, or shallowly
grooved as in C. inerme, or more deeply so as in C. thomsonae.
The ovary is 2-carpellate and 4-celled with one anatropous ovule in
each. The style is long and slender, and usually exserted except in
C. colebrookianum. The stigma is 2-fid and the stigmatic branches are
acicular.
The fruits are berries or fleshy capsules usually borne on accres-
cent sepals with two or three seeds due to abortion. They may be
spherical as in C. infortunatum, or obovoid as in C. inerme. Some
seperate into four pyrenes at maturity as in C. thomsonae. The seeds
are usually slightly flattened and irregular in shape.

Generic Descriptions
Trees, shrubs, under-shrubs, or climbers. Leaves mostly opposite,
sometimes whorled, sessile or petiolate, exstipulate, lamina undivided
or 5 to 7 lobed, the venation pinnate or palmate, 1- to 7 - costate at
the base. Inflorescences usually terminal (axillary in one species),
consisting of paniculate dichasium, occasionally ending in a monocha-
sial cyme. Flowers bracteate, pedicellate, bracteolate, complete,
zygomorphic, bisporangiate, mostly perfect, rarely sterile, hypogynous;
sepals 5, gamosepalous, truncate, toothed, or deeply lobed, valvate,
glandular or eglandular; petals 5, gamopetalous, tubular, with the tube
slightly dilated towards the throat and slit slightly posteriorly, slender,
the lobes equal or unequal, tending to be slightly bilabiate, glabrescent
or hairy; stamens 4, didynamous, alternating with the corolla lobes,
the posterior stamen absent, epipetalous, attached slightly below the
throat, filaments free, long exserted and slightly recurved, filiform,
anthers 2-thecous, 4-celled, united only towards the tip, introrse,
dehiscing longitudinally, glabrous; ovary wholly superior, spherical or
ovoid, smooth or 4- grooved longitudinally, 2- carpellate and 4- celled,
with one anatropous ovule in each, the style filiform, long exserted or
inserted, the stigma briefly 2-fid, acicular. Fruits berries or fleshy
capsules, usually borne on accrescent sepals, with 2 or 3 seeds due to
abortion, spherical or obovoid, sometimes seperating into four pyrenes
at maturity; seeds slightly flattened and irregular, non-endospermic,

VAR. semiserrata
Very similar to Clerodendron siphonanthus except for the serrate
nature of the leaves towards the apex, the inflorescences not fully
developed on both sides, and smaller flowers without exserted stamens.
Clarke believed it to be a "possibly only short imperfect flower
branches rapidly developed from a normal plant due to being cut
back by hot-weather jungle fire; for the calyx and corolla are exactly
as of C. siphonanthus” In my opinion this is definitely a variety
as ] have seen both it and the typical C. siphonanthus
sprouting from cut-back stumps, and producing their own
characteristic branches and inflorescences respectively.
This plant runs wild all over Burma at elevation from near sea-
level to 1000 ft. altitude. It grows along the banks of small rivers,
streams, lakes, and more frequently along the well-watered edges of
paddy fields especially in Lower Burma. Sometimes it can be
encountered near roadsides and railway lines, where there is frequent
water course. Being ubiquitius plant especially of Middle Burma,
large inflorescence bearing white or cream coloured flowers can be
seen from September to November, and most abundantly in October.
Fruits mature in December.

at high altitudes. It occurs in colonies of two to ten individuals on
moist soil in shady places. The white flowers are seen throughout the
year, but bears no fruit.
It is one of the most interesting species of the genus, because of
its double flowered nature. The flowers are like roses and its
fragrance is rather like that of jasmine. Being ornamental, it is mostly
