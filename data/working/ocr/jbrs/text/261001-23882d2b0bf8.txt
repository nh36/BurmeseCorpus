Studies in Dutch Relations with Arakan
1. DUTCH RELATIONS WITH KING THIRITHUDAMMA OF
ARAKAN (1622-1638).
(A paper read before the Judson College Historical Association)
on June 13th 1934
The successful rebellion of the Dutch Netherlands against Spain forms
one of the most romantic episodes in modern history. Its chief importance to
the scientific historian, however, lies in the fact that it both rendered possible.
and stimulated the great movement of Dutch commercial and imperial
expansion, which gave the death blow to Portuguese supremacy in the East
and placed in Dutch hands for most of the seventeenth century the greatest
share of the rich trade of the Indian Ocean. The story of Dutch enterprise
during that interesting century has been told with a fair degree of fullness by
the Dutch historians; but a good deal of work still remains to be done on the
original records which time, and an age more interested in such things, are
bringing to light. For some time before I went on furlough last year I had
become increasingly aware that there has long been a tendency among Eng-
lish historians to neglect the very important work done by modern Dutch
investigators and the magnificent historical material contained in the various
Dutch archives. Encouraged therefore by Sir William Foster, late historio-
grapher to the India Office, and W. H. Moreland, the best English authority
on seventeenth century Dutch sources, I decided to find out what either the
Dutch historians or their records, or perchance both, had to tell about Dutch
enterprise on the mainland of Further India. I started upon the Daghregister of
Batavia. I soon discovered that it abounds with references to Arakan, Burma
and Siam, and that these have been largely neglected by modern Dutch
writers. That is why I have chosen a short period of Dutch relations with
Arakan as the subject of my address to you this evening. And I believe that
most of the facts which I am about to lay before you represent entirely new
knowledge.
In the third quarter of the 16th century, while in the throes of their
struggle for independence, the Dutch acted as the distributors from Lisbon
to the rest of Europe of the goods brought by the Portuguese from the East.
But in 1580 the crown of Portugal passed into the hands of their great enemy
Philip II of Spain, and although he was never able to oust the Dutch comple-
tely from this lucrative commerce, nevertheless they found their distributing
trade seriously threatened and hindered, and they naturally began to dream
dreams of going themselves to the East for the spices which were in such
great demand in Europe. The destruction of the Spanish Armada by the
English in 1588 acted as a great incentive to both English and Dutch
merchants to enter the spice trade on their own account. Probably, however,
what did more than anything else to spur on the Dutch was the publication
of Linschoten's books, wherein he not only gave a first-rate account of the

trade and navigation of the Indian Ocean, largely from observations made on
the spot, but also exposed the utter weakness of the Portuguese there. Hence
before the close of the century the Dutch had established trading settlements-
'factories' as they were called-in the islands of Java and Sumatra. When
in 1602 the various competing groups of merchants were combined into the
single Dutch East India Company, wealthy, powerful and in fact almost a
department of government, the Dutch were in a far better position than any
other European power for successful expansion in the East.
From the beginning Java was the centre of the Dutch commercial
activities in the East, and there they built their capital Batavia. They soon
discovered, however, that the only way to obtain spices in the Archipelago
was to barter Indian piece-goods for them. This necessitated the planting of
factories on the Coromandel Coast. Thus in 1605 one was founded at
Masulipatam, the chief seaport of the Kingdom of Golkonda, in 1608 one at
Tegnapatam, and shortly after a third at Pulicat which became the head-
quarters of the Dutch Company on the Coromandel Coast. Now for centuries
there had been a close trading connection between that coast and the ports
of Arakan, Pegu and Tenasserim, and in the first half of the seventeenth
century the merchants of Golconda possessed a major share of the trade.
An acute observer, William Methwold, for some time chief of the English
factory at Masulipatam, in his 'Relations of the Kingdom of Golchonda',
recently published by the Hakluyt Society under the editorship of Mr.
Moreland, described the trade thus:
"To Arrecan they send store of tobacco, some iron, and few sorts of
painted clothes, and returne from thence some gold and gumme lack, but
most part rice ... To Pegu they export much silver in rials of eight, cotton
yarne, and beethyles dyed red, with several sorts of paintings; and bring
from thence the perfect rubies and sapphires which are dispersed through the
world, much gold, the best gum-lack, with some tin and quick silver.
Naturally therefore the Dutch factories on the Coromandel Coast were soon
drawn into this trade.
"
In the early part of the seventeenth century kings of Arakan, aware of
the naval strength of the Dutch, sought their aid as allies against the Great
Mogul, with whom the Arakanese were in a continual state of war. But the
Dutch wanted trade rather than war, and strove to keep clear of the King of
Arakan's schemes. From time to time they set up a factory in Arakan, but it
was only a temporary affair and little trade was done, save by private
traders. As time went on, however, their hostility for the Portuguese gra-
dually brought them into closer contact with Arakan. In 1608 Minyazagyi,
engaged in a struggle with the Portuguese, who had settled at Chittagong,
offered the Dutch free trade and permission to build forts in his dominions in
return for their support against these dangerous freebooters. But they had
refused: they were too busy elsewhere. The depredations of the notorious
Sebastain Gonzales Tibão, who had established himself on the island of
Sandwip, at last brought the Dutch in. Tibão made a great attack on
Mrohaung in 1615, and Dutch ships aided Minhkamaung, Minyazagyi's
successor, to beat it off. This timely assistance enabled the Arakanese to

gain the upper hand, and Tibão was ejected from Sandwip in 1617. The
Portuguese of Chittagong then united with the Arakanese, and turned their
arms against the Mogul power in Bengal. Every year devastating raids, often
as far as Dacca and Murshedabad, were carried out and vast numbers of
captives carried off to Mrohaung, where the King, after selecting all the
artisans for his own service, sold the rest to foreign traders at a few rupees
a head. As there was a constant demand for slaves in the Dutch factories in
the Archipelago, Dutch merchants soon became the King's chief customers
for these unhappy human beings. They also came to Arakan for rice. Their
factories in the spice-growing districts were in constant need of food supplies,
and their agents were constantly busy wherever rice was to be had, and
especially in Siam and Arakan. Such was the situation when the great
Daghregister of Batavia begins to throw light upɔn Dutch relations with
Arakan.
This magnificent record begins in the year 1624 and continues, with
gaps, until 1807. Naturally most of the gaps occur in the earlier years, and
unfortunately they are so frequent in the period with which we are now deal-
ing, that it is impossible to tell a connected story. There is, however, hope
that this defect in our knowledge may be made good. Much material
supplementary to the Daghregister undoubtedly exists in the Dutch archives,
both in Holland and at Batavia. There is, I am sure, a great field for the
researcher into the history of the Dutch connection with the lands on the
eastern side of the Bay of Bengal. It will yield a rich harvest. As yet I have
seen little more than the Daghregister itself, but I hope to show, in this
account of Dutch commerce with Arakan in the days of King Thirithudamma
how valuable is that source, and to give some idea of the importance of
Dutch sources to students of the economic history of Further India.
But before I deal with the evidence of the Daghregister let me say just
a word or two about its nature. In the first place it records the arrivals and
departures of all ships putting into or leaving Batavia, with detailed lists of
their cargoes together with the value of each cargo in Dutch florins. Then
careful abstracts of all letters received from the subordinate factories are
included, as also the more important decisions of the Council and the chief
points in the letters despatched outwards. Some of the correspondence
received is given in full, notably letters from foreign potentates, including, of
course, those received from the Kings of Arakan and Burma.
The first entry relating to Arakan is dated 3 September 1624 and deals
with the measures taken to reopen the Dutch factory at Mrohaung, which,
by the way, was always referred to by Europeans at this period as 'the city
of Arakan'. On that date, we are told with a tantalising lack of detail, that a
Dutch expedition under Anthonij Caen went forth in search of Portuguese
vessels and also with letters from the Dutch Governor- General to the King
of Arakan authorising Caen to make a trading agreement for the import of
rice and slaves to Batavia and for measures against 'our common enemy',
i.e. the Portuguese. This was followed up by the despatch of the yacht Arent
with 4000 reals in coin for the purchase of rice and slaves. Storms and
contrary winds, however, prevented her from making Arakan, and she

return cargo of 153 bales of piece-goods, 450 viss of opium, 90 lasts of rice
and 35 Arakanese slaves, the whole being valued at 56,459 florins. I must
here attempt some explanation of these terms. From another entry in the
Daghregister we learn that the last was equivalent to 3000 Dutch lbs., and it
must be remembered that the Dutch lb. was slightly heavier than the English
lb., being equal to 1.09 English lbs. The real of eight, or silver dollar, coined
by both the English and Dutch East India Companies for their trade in South
India and used widely in the East, was equal to four shillings and sixpence in
the English currency of that time. Later on in this paper I shall refer to the
Mughal coin, the tanga, used apparently as the standard coin for trading
operations in Arakan in the first half of the seventeenth century. This was
the forerunner of the rupee and was worth two shillings, and its name is still
the word used in Burma and Arakan for rupee, though its pronunciation
has been slightly altered in the course of time. The Dutch florin referred to
here was worth, so far as I have been able to estimate, almost exactly one
shilling and eight pence. If the Arent's cargo valued at 56,459 florins was
purchased for 4000 reals, a very high profit indeed was realised on her
voyage.
The next entry is not until June 1628 when the Edam leaves for Arakan
'with the requisite capital for collecting a good quantity of rice'. How she
fared we do not learn. No Daghregister for the years 1629 and 1630 is now
extant, but from 1631 onwards we begin to get much fuller information
about the Arakan trade. There is a resident Dutch factor at Mrohaung,
Cornelis van Houten, by name, who maintains a trading connection not only
with Batavia but also with the Dutch factories on the Coromandel Coast. At
the end of that year he reported that trade there was almost at a standstill
through the terrible famine and pestilence in the country. Both people and
cattle were dying of starvation, and men had even eaten each other. By trad-
ing directly with the King, however, he had managed to send away cargoes
of rice on three ships. It would appear that he was withdrawn shortly after-
wards, and that for some time the trade was left to private merchants and
isolated voyages from the Coromandel Coast.
This condition of affairs was regarded by King Thirithudamma as most
unsatisfactory. Like his two predecessors already mentioned he was keen to
develop the external trade of his country, keen enough on it indeed to fit out
his own ship for a trading voyage to the Coromandel Coast in 1634, and to
send an envoy by a Dutch ship to Pulicat asking for the loan of a Dutch pilot
to conduct his ship across the Bay of Bengal. And he was so anxious to have
a permanently established Dutch factory at his capital that in 1633 he sent
two ambassadors to Batavia to negotiate a renewal of settled trade. At the
time the Dutch were busy with the blockade of the important Portuguese
fortress of Malacca, which they had just commenced, and with sundry minor
struggles with states in the spice islands. Food supplies therefore were of
great moment to them. Hence in the following year the Arakanese envoys
were escorted home by two Dutch ships with cargoes of copper, iron, steel,
nutmeg, mace, pepper and cocoanuts, together with 2000 reals of eight in

In the next year we find a resident factor once more installed at Mro-
haung, by name Adam van der Mandere. The Daghregister for 1636 con-
tains some of his earliest reports. In a letter dated Jan. 5th of that year he
wrote a pretty full statement of trading conditions there. The King, he said,
had expressed himself as highly delighted at the re-establishment of the
Dutch factory and had promised-according to his offer made through his
ambassadors to build a suitable stone building for the occupation of the
Company's merchants and the security of its business. It was, however,
impossible as yet to buy much rice, because the recent famine had been so
severe that it had cost 10 tangas the carra of 440 lbs., and was still not to
be had for less than five tangas. And they had only managed to obtain it at
that price through the good offices of a Danish free merhant there. In fact,
the King's agent, Tosia, had done his best to keep up the price against the
Dutch, and had raised every possible obstacle to prevent them from obtaining
it at a reasonable price.
He further reported that he had procured 290 slaves and 164 maunds of
indigo, whereof 66 had been obtained at Chittagong at 1½ tangas the maund
and the rest in Arakan at 3 tangas. I may mention here that the exact
weight denoted by the word 'maund' varied greatly in the East in those days.
The Golchonda maund early in the seventeenth century was equal to 24 or
25 lb. avoirdupois. Later the English merchants tried to standardise the
Madras maund at 25 lb. and the Bombay maund at 28 lb. But here the
southern maund connot be referred to, for the Daghregister gives it as 42
Dutch lb. I suspect that Arakan got its weights, like its currency, from
Bengal.
The time to buy lac was not until May or June, continued van der
Mandere's report, but he hoped to procure a good supply at 41 tangas the
maund. He had personally been up to Chittagong, and had found it cheaper
there than in Arakan. Hence his whole outlay on that commodity averaged
no more than 3 tangas the carra. He would therefore have a good stock
collected against the arrival of ships from Batavia. There was said to be
abundance of rice in Tavoy, also tin at 18 reals of eight the bahar of 320 lb.
But the King of Arakan was the sworn enemy of the rulers of Pegu and
Tavoy, and would never permit a deputation to be sent to Tavoy for those
commodities. Moreover, the Tavoy river was dangerous to all but ships of
the lightest burthen, and all ships entering the port must deliver up their
cannon and warlike stores to the government until such time as they were
allowed to depart.

the Bommel on 14 March 1636, and was accompanied by one from King
Thirithudamma himself which the Daghregister quotes in full. I will deal
with the royal missive first. It is the earliest example of a letter from a King
of Arakan to a European power that I have so far come across. The King
styles himself the invincible ruler of Arakan and of a multitude of great

Van der Mandere's letter did not paint so rosy a picture of the royal
favour to the Dutch. Not a stone, he said, had been laid to the house that
the King had promised to build for the Company. The delay was officially
explained as due to the need to strengthen the defences of Mrohaung against
an expected attack from Pegu. Moreover, although the King had received
the Dutch factors very kindly with many high courtesies and compliments,
very small effect had ensued. All his efforts to obtain permission to send to
Batavia the children of Dutchmen by Arkanese mothers had failed, and the
King had allowed a vexatious corner in rice to be formed against the Dutch
by his chief minister. Readers of my Early English Intercourse with
Burma will recognise here a reference to two customs similar to those in
Burma in early days. Foreign residents and even visitors to Burma and
Arakan were encouraged to form temporary alliances with the women of the
country, but on the strict understanding that when they left the country their
wives and children might not be taken away with them. This prohibition
often constituted a serious hardship in individual cases, and we find Europeans
resorting to all sorts of expedients to smuggle their families out of the
country. There were cases of wives being hidden in large martaban jars and •
smuggled on board ship. The pious Dutch Calvinists were also not a little
worried because their children left in Arakan were brought up to be Muslims.
But what caused van der Mandere the greatest embarrassment was the

King's sudden and unannounced grant of a monopoly of the rice trade to the
chief cappade, Lolcholij. No one else might sell rice to the Dutch on pain
of death. There is no doubt that the King realised that the Dutch warlike
operations against Malacca offered him a splendid opportunity for profit, for
van der Mandere reported that this move was the result of the Arakanese
ambassadors' visit to Batavia in 1633. On their return they had told the
King that the Dutch were so short of rice there that it cost 18 tangas the
carra. But this was not the only monopoly. A secret order had been issued
prohibiting anyone from buying the iron imported into the country by the
Dutch Company, and Lolcholij was sitting tight hoping in this way to buy up
the whole supply at a low price,
The sharp practices, however, were not all on one side. The harrassed
factor had to complain very sharply of the impertinent behaviour of the
captain and supercargo of the Bommel. They had, he said, so severely beaten
the people who came to deliver rice on board the ship that they had been
forced to yield great overmeasure, and the dealers had refused in future to
measure their rice on board. He could do nothing with the offenders, for
they had flatly requdiated his authority.
For the year 1636 the information in the Daghregister (that of 1637) is
more detailed and precise than in any previous year. Four Dutch ships
arrived in Arakan that year, the Wessaenen from Pulicat on July 17th, the
Oostcappelle and Ruttem from Batavia on October 6th and the Otter from
Batavia on November 2nd. The lastnamed on her way to Arakan captured a
Portuguese ship off Junkceylon with a most acceptable cargo consisting of 88
bales of piece-goods and 45-50 lasts of rice. Bad weather, however, had made
it necessary to abandon the captured vessel, and she had therefore been set
on fire. The Ruttem and Oostcappelle had left Batavia on 22 July 1636
with cargoes valued at 263,284 florins bound for Pulicat. They were
instructed to discharge their cargoes there and proceed with all haste to
Arakan, where they were to take on board all the rice, paddy, lac and
slaves that van der Mandere had in stock. They arrived in Pulicat on 24
August and after discharging took on fresh cargoes for disposal in Arakan-
pepper and other spices, sandalwood, sulphur, vermilion and tobacco with
also 2000 reals of eight to the total value of 13,495 florins. The Wessaenen,
despatched by the Governor of the Dutch stations, on the Coromandel
Coast, Carel Reijniersen, carried a cargo of piece-goods, iron and tobacco
valued at 11,717 florins and a letter of complaint to King Thirithudamma
about the monopolistic trading methods of his minister. The Governor had
spoken on the subject to the officers of the King's ship, which had come to
Pulicat for trade, and they had assured him that such practices were
without the King's knowledge. They had promised to speak to the King
about the matter and ask for redress.
The three ships from Batavia returned there in February and March
1637, bringing with them three letters from van der Mandere dated
respectively 10, 21, and 31 January. They contain much interesting
information. It is obvious that van der Mandere and the King had begun
to get on each others' nerves very badly. After the arrival of the ships he

In the meantime van der Mandere's difficulties real or fancied, thickened.
The rice monopoly continued as before in the hands of Losolosij, a new chief
minister. And the King, of course, was not ignorant of it, as his shippers had
tried to persuade Governor Carel Reijniersen at Pulicat. He had heard that
the Dutch were in dire straits for rice because of wars with Macassar, the
Mataram and the King of Bantam, and he was resolved to take full advantage
of the situation. Van der Mandere therefore made a counter-move to avoid.
the monopoly. He bought a small vessel from a Portuguese merchant, E.
Manuel Rodrigo Agrij, and naming it the Arakan sent it to Chittagong under
the charge of his assistant, Pieter Cocqu, to whom he entrusted the sum of
1500 tangas for the purchase of rice, slaves and black cloth. But when
Cocqu had advanced money to the private brokers for a supply of rice at 2
to 3 tangas the carra of 420 lb., the King had sent a vessel thither to enforce
the monopoly. And the King's agent bought up all the rice that was for sale
and passed it on to Cocqu at 4 to 4 tangas, having himself paid 2 tangas
for it. Van der Mandere had then to admit his defeat and to agree to buy all
his rice at the King's price. And as the Coromandel factories needed a
larger supply than could be carried on the Wessaenen, he fitted out the
Arakan to carry a supplementary cargo across the Bay.
To command her he appointed the gunner's mate of the Oostcappelle
one Heindrick Heindricksen of Hamburg, giving him the rank of second mate
and a salary of 26 guilders a month. The remainder of the crew consisted of
two Dutchmen and a number of the Company's slaves. For her voyage the
Arakan was equipped with 4 swivel guns, 100 lbs. biscuit in'two kegs, one set
of sails, six hooks with accessories, one strong rope and a quantity of tar,
and with such equipment, van der Mandere wrote to Batavia, he judged the
vessel "defensive against an enemy and fully provisioned for its journey."
Men were tough in those days!
He was no more successful in the matter of the children than in that of
the rice monopoly. Again and again he besought an audience of the King.
Not until the evening before the Oostcappelle's departure on Jan. 10th would
the King deign to notice him. Then at 10 o'clock of night van der Mandere
and a free burgher of Batavia, Hans Bisschop, were admitted into the palace
and the King appeared at a window. They begged him to allow the Dutch
half-cast children, and especially those of Hans Bisschop's deceased brother,
to depart on the ship the next day, and they also seized the opportunity to
ask for licence to collect the rice for which they had made contracts with
private brokers, and in general for the free trade of former days. The King's
sole reply was: "The Governor General has not written to me about the
children; what your General writes to me about, that will I permit." Then

With regard to other articles of commerce van der Mandere reported
disconsolately that he had bought some 200 slaves, but none either young
or robust. The King had monopolised the sale of lac; hence he could
obtain only five maunds at 3 tangas the maund. In future it would be
impossible to buy it at that price. He had sold Japanese iron at 4 tangas
the maund, but the iron that had been sent over from the Coromandel Coast
was unsaleable at that price, and he had no less than 48,000 lb. on his
hands. Fortunately the gloom was not unrelieved. There was plenty of
black cloth to be had both in Arakan and in Chittagong. In the former
place it cost two tangas the piece, in the latter only 1 tangas. The piece,
he stated, was eight hasta in length. Here I conclude that he was referring
to the hasta or covad of Masulipatam which was 18.7 inches long. The
piece then would measure three yards and 5.6 inches. Red dungerries also
could be purchased in abundance at one tanga each. Still better, an
ambassador from the Nawab of Dacca had arrived in the capital with a
present of six horses and some piece-goods for the purpose of negotiating
peace. The cessation of the chronic hostilities between Arakan and Bengal,
wrote van der Mandere, would promote trade and do some good to Dutch
business in Arakan.
The chief minister, Losolosij, was anxious to send two of his retainers
by the Dutch ships to Batavia. When he approached the Dutch factor in
matter, van der Mandere, presumably hoping thereby to improve his
relations with the King, was weak enough to grant the request, although
he must have been aware of the ulterior motive behind the visit. On
arrival at Batavia in February they presented the following letter of
introduction from their master: "God will that these present words find
Your Excellency in such perfect life, health, and prosperity as this your
friend desires for Your Excellency. So far as I am concerned I enjoy good
health. Two of my people go thither ready for your Excellency's service.
Will your Excellency be pleased to treat them as you would your own
affairs, and if they make any complaints, will your Excellency be pleased
to grant them a hearing as you would your own friend, and see that they
return satisfied by the first opportunity that occurs, so that I may obtain
news of my friend. As the ship is in a hurry to depart I cannot send your
Excellency more than one whole rampatij and a half piece of mamonij
together with five packages of fine rice, the which are unworthy of your
Excellency and are merely a remembrance." The letter is dated January
1637 and signed "Loscor ZUzil." The piecegoods mentioned as presents
were kinds of cheap muslin.

in Aracan for permitting the despatch of these two persons after the scurvy
treatment he had received at the hands of the King and chief minister.
They had come to the conclusion, rightly or wrongly, that he had not
maintained due dignity in his dealings with the Court. And this incident
made them decide that he must be withdrawn and a better man appointed

As luck would have it, just before the last ship left Arakan for Batavia
van der Mandere suddenly discovered a complete change of attitude on the
part of the King. Two days before the Ruttem was due to leave, another
of the ministers, called Chama, summoned van der Mandere to the palace
and besought him in the royal name to postpone the ship's departure for
five or six days to enable His Majesty to send the Governor General a
letter and a present of 50 carras of rice. Now at last the Dutchman stood

II. DUTCH TRADE WITH ARAKAN IN THE FIRST HALF OF THE
SEVENTEENTH CENTURY.
(A paper read before the Burma Economic Society on July 25th 1934.)
If you turn to the chapter of Harvey's History of Burma in which he
deals with Arakan, you will find a summary of what up to the present has
been known, or imagined, concerning the subject of my paper this evening.
The Dutch, he says, from about 1626 to 1683 had a factory a mile from
Mrohaung, the then capital of Arakan; "it was closed at times, because
they had so many commitments elsewhere; and in 1670 the whole staff was
massacred. But while it lasted they found the King of Arakan more business-
like than him of Burma, for under the articles of 1653 he admitted their
right to claim their own interpreter at audiences and to take away their
children by women of the country. Indeed, as might be expected of a people
dwelling amid the life of the seaports, the Arakanese were in several respects
less backward than the Burmese. Thus they permitted the export of rice,
under the control of an officer who regulated it so as to prevent a shortage.
And about 1660 they began to use coined currency in the ports, striking it
themselves."
These few facts were for the most part taken from a book written early
in the eighteenth century by the Dutch writer Valentyn and entitled "Oud en
Nieuw Oost-Indien ", which modern scholars have learnt to regard as highly
unreliable. Now with the exception of this writer Dutch historians have
maintained almost complete silence regarding the history of their trading
connection with Burma and Arakan. They seem to have preferred to write
about their more permanent settlements in the East and those operations
which played more important parts in the development of their imperial

As everybody knows, the Dutch appeared in the East in the last years
of the sixteenth century as competitors with and deadly enemies of, the
Portuguese. The writings of that excellent geographer Linschoten furnished
them with first-hand information about trade and navigation in the Indian
Ocean; and when in 1602 the various competing groups of Dutch merchants
were combined into the single Dutch East India Company, which was almost
a department of Government, they were in a far better position than any
other European power to undertake successful expansion in the East. In
particular they commanded both greater naval power and greater capital
resources than anyone else.
They soon came to regard Java as the best centre for a commercial
empire mainly concerned with the collection of spices for the European mar-
ket. There in 1611 the first Dutch Governor-General laid the foundations of
their future capital, Batavia, in Jacatra; and under their vigorous rule the
new settlement began to prosper exceedingly. A vast amount of European
capital flowed into the port, and some of the most industrious nations of Asia
were attracted thither. From Batavia the Dutch established contact with
Arakan by a double route. In the first place their ships sailed directly to
Arakan, Burma and Siam in search of supplies of rice for their garrisons and
fleets in the Spice Islands, and to Arakan to buy the slaves captured by the
Arakanese in their perennial raids into Bengal and neighbouring countries.
In the second place there was the less direct route via the Coromandel Coast.
Almost the only way to obtain spices in the Archipelago was by bartering
Indian piece-goods for them. This necessitated the planting of factories on
the Coromandel Coast, and by the end of the first decade of the seventeenth
century there were Dutch trading posts at Masulipatam, the chief seaport of
the Kingdom of Golkonda, at Tegnapatam and at Pulicat. And the last-named
became the seat of the Dutch Governor of the Coromandel factories.
Now for centuries the Coromandel ports had maintained a regular trade.
with the ports of Arakan, Pegu and Tenasserim. In the first half of the
seventeenth century the merchants of Golkonda were the chief participants in
this trade, exporting to Arakan tobacco, iron and coloured cotton stuffs, and
bringing back gold in small quantities, raw lac and rice. It was not long
before the Dutch Coromandel factories began to compete with the Indian
merchants in this trade. And kings of Arakan, aware of Dutch naval strength,
courted their alliance both against the Mughal power in Bengal, and also
against the rebellious Portuguese of Chittagong. But the Dutch were too
busy with their own struggles with the Portuguese and with various native
powers in the Archipelago to be willing to add to their commitments. Not

commodities and prices met with in the records of its trading operations, and
afterwards the conditions under which it carried on trade. The direct trade
between Batavia and Arakan was much greater in bulk than the indirect
trade via the Coromandel Coast. Spices of various kinds and Japanese iron
formed the major portion of the cargoes sent from Batavia to Mrohaung; but

of the necessary return cargoes of rice, lac and slaves, each ship sent there

different denominations, which were coined by the big European companies
in the East. The coin most used in the first half of the seventeenth century
was the 'real of eight' which was worth about four shillings and sixpence in
the English currency of the time.
To give some idea of the nature and value of Batavia's imports from
Arakan let me quote from the Daghregister some details of ships' cargoes.
But first of all I must explain the terms used to denote weights and
measures, etc. Rice was measured by the last or the carra, and sometimes
by both. Ther last was a measure of weight equivalent to 3000 Dutch lbs.,
and as the Dutch lb. was equal to 1.09 lbs. avoirdupois, the last works out
at 3270 lbs. avoirdupois. The carra varied in weight from 420 to 440 Dutch
lbs. I suspect it to have been the customary amount carried by a bullock
in two panniers. Then for smaller amounts we shall meet with the maund,
but we must beware of thinking of it in terms of the modern maund. In the
seventeenth century the maund varied greatly from district to district. The
Golkonda maund was equal to 24 or 25 lbs. avoirdupois. The Mughal
official maunds changed their weight with each emperor, and varied from as
much as 74 lbs. to as little as 37 lbs. In Arakan, however, the Dutch
used a maund described in the Daghregister as equal to 42 Dutch lbs. and
hence a little less than 46 lbs. avoirdupois. This was approximately the
size of the Bengal maund of that date, which was used at Chittagong by
the Portuguese. The values of all cargoes-i.e. their estimated net cost was
always given in Dutch florins, a coin worth then about one shilling and
eightpence, or a little more then a modern rupee. Prices in Arakan and at
Chittagong were always given in tangas, the offical Mughal currency before.
the rupee, and worth two shillings. And it is interesting to note that the
word is still the one used for 'rupee' in Burma and Arakan, though its
pronunciation has been slightly altered.

commodities were relatively unimportant.
Regarding prices some factors, notably Adam van der Mandere 1634-38,
provide us with useful details, while others completely ignore the subject in
their reports. Hence it is impossible to do much in the way of working out
changes in price levels and their causes. Rice was a royal monopoly; the
King's agent therefore fixed the price. In doing this he was influenced by
two main considerations: the amount of surplus available for export and the
needs of the Dutch. For instance, during the terrible famine of the years
1631-1635 the price of rice at Mrohaung rose to 10 tangas the carra of
440 lbs. When conditions began to ease the Dutch factor had a tremendous
struggle with the King's agent before he could get his rice at 5 tangas the
carra. In 1636, when there was a good surplus at Chittagong, the Dutch
factor tried to evade the King's agent by going there in person and advanc-
ing money to private brokers to supply his needs at 2-3 tangas the carra of
420 lbs. But the King's agent intervened and prohibited this transaction by
buying up all the rice at 2 tangas and refusing to let the Dutchman have it
at less than 4-4½ tangas. He knew that he could get his price because the
Arakanese ambassadors, who had returned from Batavia reported that the
Dutch were in great straits for rice supplies on account of their blockade of
Malacca and wars with various native states. And in the next year, to the
great disgust of the Dutch, he made an excuse to send more spies to Batavia
to keep him informed of conditions there. To prevent this sort of thing in
future a standing order was passed by the Council at Batavia prohibiting
factors at branches from giving permits to such people to travel to Batavia.
It is interesting to note that he had gauged the situation to a nicety, for the
Dutch Governor-General, on hearing of the price his factor at Arakan had
been forced to pay, wrote that the price was not unsatisfactory, since any
price within 5 tangas was reasonable. After this effort on the part of the

We must now pass to a consideration of the conditions under which
this trade was carried on. Naturally a great deal-in fact, practically every-
thing-depended upon the state of relations between the Chief of the Dutch
factory and the King. As we have already seen the relations between Adam
van der Mandere and King Thirithudamma were bad, particularly over the
question of the rice monopoly. Van der Mandere adopted the view that the
monopoly was merely an unfair means of preventing the Dutch from obtain-
ing their supplies at a fair price. He was ignorant of, or ignored, the real
cause of it, i.e. the prevention of a shortage of a vital element of the people's
diet. And the need of such a measure is at once apparent when we read the
accounts of the terrible famine which had ravaged not only Arakan, but
other countries also, during the period 1631-1634. The Dutchman, of course,
was only concerned with the problem of freighting his ships with adequate
cargoes at the cheapest possible cost, and he knew that the King was trading
upon the urgency of the Dutch need for food supplies. But in this respect
the King's commercial morality was no lower than that of his customers.
His obtuseness in the avoidance of direct relations with van der Mandere
was held by Governor-General van Diemen to have been rather the latter's
fault. Van Diemen held that the factor had not behaved with becoming
dignity, and in ordering his recall said that in future men of proper bearing
and not 'slovens' should be appointed.

ill to take any interest in the matter. Meanwhile van der Graeff and his
chief officer died in prison, and there was nothing for it but to carry out the
Governor-General's threat. There is no doubt that the King was very loath to
see the Dutch depart. He tried to pacify them with the present of a young
elephant and a grant of free trade in slaves. But he remained obdurate on the
subject of the captured ship, and the Dutch factory was withdrawn.
When in 1653 the Dutch returned to Mrohaung to negotiate the reopen-
ing of their factory with King Sandathudamma, their previous experience of
trade in Arakan led them to formulate a set of trading conditions which their
new factor, Joan Goessens, presented to the King for acceptance. Their
demands form an illuminating commentary upon trading conditions in that
country. The first article laid down the general principle that byegones were
to be byegones. The second explained the first. The six captive Dutchmen,
the survivors of van der Graeff's ill-fated crew, were to be released in exch-
ange for 123 Araknese prisoners captured by the Dutch in reprisal after the
closing of the factory. The other articles dealt more specifically with trading
matters. Dutch ships coming to the Arakan river were to be supplied with
experienced pilots to bring them into the harbour. Letters arriving for the
King from the Governor-General were not to be subjected to long delays be-
fore delivery. Dutch captains were to be allowed free access to the King so
that they might make their representations to him by word of mouth and not
have to write them out for the ministers to play about with. And they were
to be permitted to bring with them their own interpreter. This was an im-
portant point, for the official interpreter supplied by the Court invariably
edited, softened down or cut out portions of what was said by foreign merchants
at audiences, so as to keep anything disagreeable from the royal ears or
enhance their humility before the royal splendour. There was to be free
trade under the royal authority in all kinds of merchandise, and the Dutch
factory was not to be inconvenienced with spies or sentries. The Dutch were
to be permitted to carry on trade with all manner of people, and not to be

III. SHAH SHUJA AND THE DUTCH WITHDRAWAL IN 1665.
The romantic story of Shah Shuja has attracted the attention of many
writers. Schouten, Bernier, Manucci, Bowrey and Hamilton, in his own day or
shortly after, wrote accounts of his flight to Arakan and death there after
failing to seize the Mughal throne from his brother Aurangzeb in 1660. And
in more recent years this Journal has published studies of the incident from
the pens of Mr. G. E. Harvey and U San Baw U. Further treatment of the
subject here therefore might well seem superfluous, were it not that the
evidence of the Daghregister of Batavia does not appear to have been used
by these two writers, and the references to it in that great record are of
exceptional interest. Moreover the Daghregister shows us the affair in a new
light, as the first of a chain of events which led up to the withdrawal of the
Dutch factory from Mrohaung with dramatic suddenness in 1665.
Shah Shuja, the third son of the Mughal Emperor Shah Jehan, had been
made Governor of Bengal by his father. When the emperor died, a scramble
for the vacant throne on the part of his sons ensued. The final victory rested
with Aurangzeb, who accordingly became emperor. Shah Shuja was the last
of the brothers to succumb to his power. When all was lost, the defeated
prince fled first to his capital, Dacca, and thence (by land, says U San
Baw U) to Chittagong, where he obtained permission from the King of Ara-
kan to continue his journey to Mrohaung. At the capital he was hospitably
received by the King, who assigned him and his suite a residence on the
right bank of the Wathi Creek at the base of Bahbudaung Hill.
The King of Arakan promised ships to carry the fugitives to Mecca,
But when after eight months his promise was still unfulfilled, an incident
occurred which brought about the massacre of the prince and most of his

were in a secret letter. The decision to abandon the factory, he was told,
was made "chiefly in order to give some satisfaction to the Nabob in
Bengal, who had expressed such a desire to us, together with his master, the
Mogul, and in order to preserve our great trade in his kingdom, wherein the
Company was very well situated." What was said in the general letter
about sending the yacht Brouwershaven, he was informed, was intended
merely "to mislead the world, since the safety of the Company's servants
and property depends upon keeping this matter secret". He was to carry
out the withdrawal with the greatest care and secrecy, and when it was
accomplished, he was to get a message through to the King informing him
of his departure and explaining that it was not done as a hostile act, "but
only because of the important property which the Company has in the land
of the Mogul, and that there is no intention of assisting him against
Arakan."
To complete the deception the Governor-General in accordance with the
usual custom sent a complimentary letter to the King, in order, so the
Daghregister puts it, "to mislead him as much as possible, and make him
believe that we had no intention of removing the factory and leaving
Arakan."
