Now in Chinese and Tai the passage of old initial sonants into
surds, has had the result of doubling the Tone-system, by creating two
Series, High and Low (perhaps Original and Reduced would be a more
accurate description). Thus
(i) a Low Series - for words which have, or once had, an initial
sonant.
(ii) a High Series - for other words.
>
The same thing has usually happened, or is happening, in Karen.
When a dialect-Pwo or Sgaw, for example-has lost its initial sonants,
it has normally six tones. When it retains them-as in Western Bwè, it
has normally no more than three. In Pǎku, which is in transition, I
incline to the view that there are still only three tones, not six. In
Pǎ o, Northern and Southern, I think the six tones are pretty clearly
distinguishable, though only three are shewn in the missionary script.
Let us adopt Haudricourt's numbering of these various tones: H
1, 2 and 3 for the High Series, and L 1, 2 and 3 for the corresponding
Low (sonant or ex-sonant) Series. They are not arranged in order of
their modern pitch. L3, L2 and L 1 are just the reduced forms
respectively of H 3, H 2 and H 1. Note that the third tone, whether
H 3 or L 3, can never be nasalized, and is generally closed by a Glottal
Stop (?), indicative of a lost final consonant. Tones 1 and 2, whether
High or Low, may be open or nasalized. In the latter case there was
once usually a final nasal consonant. What the original tonal distinc-
tion was between 1 and 2, I cannot say; but it goes deep.
Final consonants have almost disappeared from modern Karen.
Labial plosives are the first to go; nasals the last. Some still survive in
Pă o pronunciation, especially in Northern Pă-o; and there in some
dialects more than in others. The trouble is that Northern Pǎ-o, while
still keeping its power to pronounce finals, is fast losing its Karen
vocabulary in favour of Shan or Burmese. I give a short specimen list

In tracing origins, it is these initials affecting both Low and High
tones which give one most trouble, especially R, X R., J and y. Here
comparison with the northern groups of Karen is particularly useful;
and on the strength of it I have sometimes come to conclusions different
from Haudricourt's. I think initial y in Karen usually goes back to a
Nasal; but I doubt if Haudricourt is right in restoring original as a
Nasal. Recently I believe he has modified his views, and now derives
it from initial h in high-toned words, and possibly alveolar in low-
toned words; for initial r, both in Pwo and Sgaw, is at present
confined to fairly modern loan words. But I do not feel at all certain
on these points; and still less on the original sounds of the letters which
I transcribe R, X" and f. They too straddle high and low tones: so
must in each case represent a modern merging of more than one
original sound. For Karen origins, a lot depends on finding the right
solution of these problems.
There is no doubt whatever, in my opinion, that Karen is basically
a Tibeto - Burman language, akin in origin to Burmese, though Burmans
and Karens have followed widely different routes to meet once again
in the plains of Burma. There is no time to analyse the basic connec-
tions of the two languages; but in my various Tables I have chosen
instances to illustrate the relationship.
The migration-route of the Karens will have to be traced by noting
the foreign elements in the Karen language. One such element is
certainly the Austric. It is mostly Austro-asiatic, especially Old Mon,
but also Riang, the pre-Shan language of the Central Shan Plateau. In
Table IX, "Karen Austric", I include only Karen words extending
beyond Southern Karen. Austric words connected with the sea, boats,
fish, etc., common especially in Pwo, are mostly modern borrowings.
But such borrowings as Leaf, Fruit, Husked Rice, Village, Fowl, etc.,
argue a long association of Karens with Mon-Khmer peoples, perhaps
before the entry of either into Burma.

They occur in Southern Chin; but they are not a normal feature of

languages, but not in northern. Contrariwise, they are an essential
element in the Northern Tai languages, especially the Chung - chia
dialects of Kueichou and Kuanghsi, but not in Southern Tai such as
Siamese. Except in Northern Pão, I have found in Karen only a few

basic words - most parts of the body, numerals, natural phenomena,
fauna, flora, material objects, common verbs. But if, judged by its
vocabulary, Karen is not a distant relative of Burmese, it is not a close

place before phonetic breakdown brought in tonal differentiation. And
there are big differences in Word-Order.
There is certainly a Mon-Riang element in Karen, but not very
ancient. The Shan element is modern and local. But the Tai element
in the broadest sense of the term, and the Austro-asiatic element in
general, may be more extensive and more ancient. And suspect that
there is another element, neither Mon-Khmer nor Tai, glimpsed
especially in words with glottal ɗ and 8 initials. Such words include
some of the less common names connected with the body and some
names for fauna, flora and common objects. These glottal words are
not basically Karen; but glottal sounds. now mostly confined to
Southern Karen and its neighbours, were once probably universal in
the language.
Here is my working hypothesis. Around the beginning of the
