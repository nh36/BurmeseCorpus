THE GENERAL PICTURE OF THE INDUSTRY.
There are about 45 hselaikkhons* (6:884) of various
sizes in Rangoon Town with not less than 45 different
brands of hsebawlaik, (culo88) manufactured within the
Rangoon Municipality. The accompanying list † showing
the number of hselaikkhons in Rangoon, does not claim to
be exhaustive, but it can confidently be said that it covers
a sufficiently high percentage of the industry to enable the
writer to make reliable estimates.
The sizes of hselaikkhons vary widely as can be
seen from the list, varying from khens having less than
5 workers to khons employing well-over 400. Roughly,
the industry can be divided into 3 groups. The first
group includes the 3 leading hselaikkhons, which, between
them, employ about 1000 workers. Their products are the
3 popular brands in Rangoon Town. The second group
includes those khons employing workers ranging from 40
to 80 workers in each. There are 4 khons in this group
and the total number of workers is about 200. The brands
used in this group are fairly popular. Group III contains
the rest--some of them employing 15 to 20 workers and
some employing less than 5 workers in each khon. This
group consists of about 40 small sized khons, employing
between them only about 250 workers. It can be seen
from the list that figures have not been investigated for
about half the number of khons in this group. Most of
them are new comers to the industry and all of them are
relatively unpopular brands, so that they have to confine
their trade to their respective limited areas. It is, there-
fore, very unlikely that any of the khons would employ
more than 10 to 15 workers, while many will be in the
'less than 5' class. They can therefore be safely included
in the 10-15 or less than 5 classes. On this assumption
250 is estimated as the total employment by all the khons
in this group.

But unfortunately this Department has not yet compiled

statistics for other Cottage Industries. Incidentally, then, the
estimate arrived at in this thesis can claim to be the
figure compiled for the first time in post-war Burma. It
may be proper here to mention the number of cheroot
workers for the whole of Burma. According to the Census
of India 1931, "workers engaged in the manufacture of
tobacco (mainly cheroot making) have increased from
14,468 in 1921 to 28,512 in 1931" It is unfortunate that
the 1941 Census is not available. But it is quite apparent
that the cheroot industry has been on the increasing trend,
having a great stimulus during the Japanese occupation
period, and probably maintaining its own, against competi-
tion from foreign imported cigarettes, in the post-war
period.
The 3 leading firms or the Big Three viz Ma Sein
Nyun, Myinbyan Ma Tint and Kyabyan Ma Kauk, employ
between them about 1000 cheroot rollers. They enjoy a
wide market because their brands are the most popular in
Rangoon. Their popularity can be attributed to various
causes viz (1) The product itself is superior in quality to
other brands. This is very likely because there are so many
grades and qualities in the raw materials so that the firm
in question uses the best quality of the raw materials. (2)
The popular firms have trade secrets, especially in the
preparation of the stuffing or filler for the hsebawlaik so
that its product conforms to the tastes of the general public.
(3) It may be due to 'buyers' inertia', that is to say, a pre-
ference for this particular brand to other brands. The
other brands may be equal or even better in quality to this
brand, but in the minds of the consumers they think this
particular brand suits them most and so they stick on
smoking it. Thus, for example, a man may have a parti-
cular liking for Gold Flakes and refuse to smoke other
brands like Players and Capstan which are the nearest

The popularity of a brand may be due to the long established
name and reputation of the firm. But there are two ex-
ceptions to this. In the first place, Ma Ta Po, a brand
which claims to be the oldest in Rangoon Town, being
established some 60 years ago, about 1888, has now dwin-
dled to a great extent, employing only about 3 or 4
workers. On the other hand, a recent new comer, Kya-
byan Ma Kauk, established in the post-war year of 1946,
had made a very good start. She has now under her
management, well over 150 workers and is said to be
still expanding.
The popularising of the brand is the chief task of all
producers Thus, it is not infrequent in the hsebawlaik
industry for some persons to buy the whole established firm
i.e., to change ownership, but to continue producing under
the same brand or for the descendents and relatives of
owners to continue producing the same brand. Any
brand, which has established its popularity will expand rapi-
dly. But unless the increase in demand arises from the
increase in number of new smokers, the increase in output
of particular brand will be at the expense of other brands.
Thus there will be other firms going out of business due to
dwindling away of demand for their brands. These firms
will not, however, go out
business immediately. Firstly,
when the demand for its product diminishes, it may work
under capacity i.e., each worker will now produce less than
before. They may even work on alternate days, or so many
days per week. This is the case of under-employment. When
it finds the reduced demand to be permanent, then it will
be forced to dismiss workers. When, finally, it finds that
even the diminished receipts would not cover working costs,
viz. cost of labour and raw materials, then the firm will be
forced to go out of business. But until and unless this stage
is reached, the firm will continue to stick on in business,
with reduced hands. Thus, there are may hselaikkons with
less than 5 workers. These firms may be termed 'firms on
the margin', because if the demand for its products tend to
fall below the existing level, they will have to go out of
business, since they cannot go on producing indefinitely at
a loss.

There are a string of small-sized hselaikkhons of 5-10
and less than 5 workers, as shown in the list. These small-
sized khons are able to exist because there are no heavy
overhead charges, so that they can continue in business,
with very small outputs. So long as they can cover working
expenses, which in the minimum consist of labour charges and
raw materials, they are satisfied with making small margins
of profit, The absence of fixed plants and overhead costs
also explains the ease with which new hselaikkhons can
enter the business. On the other hand, the presence of
preference on the part of group of buyers for particular brands
is the retarding factor against easy entrance. Many of the
small-sized khons are new comers to the industry. They come
into business at first, with few working hands and make a
bid for a share in the market. If they succeed in getting
a ring of consumers, then, they may stay in business.
But if they do not get a foothold, then, they quietly go out
of business. The shortage of working funds would not hinder
a khon fron expanding. For the expansion could work in
a cumulative manner, by rapid trade turnovers, which will
grease the wheels of business to turn round. The expan-
sion may be brought about either by working longer hours
or employing more hands, if the first alternative cannot
with the increased demand. Since there are no
cope up
such things as indivisible factors, instalment of new equip-
ment or 'bottle necks' in either raw materials or labour,
output can be expanded or contracted with great flexibility,
in response to fluctuating demand.
LABOUR.
It is apparent that the labour force of each hselaik-
khon depends upon the extent of demand the particular
brand commands. If it is a very popular brand a single
hselaikkhon will employ as many as 400 to 500 workers.
On the other extreme there are numerous small sized ones
with as few as 5 workers in each. The existence of
hselaikkhons with 20 to 40 workers and those with 60 to
80 workers is because their brands are fairly popular, in the
sense that they do command a fair proportion of the total
consumers' demand but not so big a proportion as that
enjoyed by the very popular brands.

The wages are paid in piece rates. This is universal
in the whole industry. Workers are paid according to the
amount of work they do per day. The earnings of workers
within a hselaikkhon do not vary so much. The dispersion
is also not great with the earning of workers in other khons.
The major portion of the wage-bill, of course, goes to the
cheroot rollers. All of them are women labourers. Other
kinds of work for which wages are paid are the manual
labour employed in the mixing and preparing of the stuffing
for the hsebawlaik. This process will be explained in a
later section. Here we are interested only in the division
of labour and their respective earnings. To compute the
earnings of each worker, we need to know, firstly, the
capacity of output per worker per day. This will vary
according as (1) the degree of skill possessed by the
worker (2) the size of the cheroot the worker rolls (3) the
number of hours worked per day. Thus the earnings of a
If

she is a skilful worker i. e., if she is slick in the work, she

the latter. Moreover, it is obvious that the longer hours she
works, the greater will be the output and the greater will be
her earnings. Lastly, the smaller the size of the cheroot,

in any hselaikkhon, the lesser the degree of dispersion of

The division of sizes into large, medium and small
do not apply to all the hselaikkhons. For there are as many
as 4 to 5 different sizes manufactured in certain firms.
Nevertheless, the above three sizes are the most common
and correspond also to the popular sizes demanded by the
consumers. In some hselaikkhons, the piece rate for the
smallest size is 6 annas per 100, for the medium size 8
annas and for the largest 12 annas per 100. But the piece
rate mentioned above can be said confidently to be uniform
amongst most of the firms. It can be seen from the above
figures that the average daily earnings of a cheroot roller
ranges from Rs. 2/8/- to Rs. 3/-. But it must be remem-
bered that this amount is earned only when she works
normally, from 8 to 10 hours a day. In many of the small sized
khons the working hours are usually less, i.e., working under
capacity and so accordingly the earnings are less. There-
fore, it would be on the safe side to say that the average
earnings of workers for the whole industry vary from Rs. 2
to Rs. 3 per day. This can be compared with the pre-war
average earnings of about 8 annas per day. The present
earnings come to about 4 times that of pre-war. This is also,
in line with the cost of living index today, which is 3 to 4
times above pre-war.
The period of training a new hand to become a worker
of average skill takes about 15 to 20 days. However, it
depends upon the degree of intelligence of the workers;
if she is a very smart girl, she may take to the trade in less
than 10 days. On the other hand, if the girl is a bit 'thick'
headed, it may take longer than 20 days to train her.
The maxim of 'practice makes perfect' can be applied to
the art of cheroot rolling. The more experienced a worker,

An interesting feature in the hsebawlaik industry is
that workers in each hselaikkhon are usaully drawn from
the neighbourhood in which the khon is situated. Thus,
supposing the hselaikkhon is in Sanchaung, the workers are
drawn mostly from the Sanchaung Quarter. It is either
due to the convenienc for the workers to come to their work
or it is due to the desire of the employer to give employment
to the womenfolk from the neighbourhood. This act of
generosity on the part of the employer is done partly to
get the goodwill of all these residents in that Quarter.
Thus, there are many instances where certain brands enjoy
a wide market in their respective areas, just because of the
'buyers' inertia' of the consumers residing in that Quarter.
To give some examples, Sanchaung Ma Tint, Kemmen-
dine Daw Tu, Kokkine Yin Yin are brands which are
fairly popular in their respective localities but otherwise
unknown in other parts of the city, where they cannot
compete with the popular brands. This inertia on the part
of the consumers residing in a particular locality is a pheno-
mena worth mentioning because on it depends the existence
of some hselaikkhons.
There is much division of labour in the manufacture
of hsebawlaik. Firstly the mixing of the ingredients, accor-
ding to formula, is a work requiring manual labour. It
is usual to employ Indian labourers to do this job. The
job of mixing and preparing the stuffing is done under the
strict supervision of the entreprenour, for much depends on
this mixture. The stuffing or the filler when prepared is
brought to the khon and is placed on round bamboo trays
where two workers sit opposite each other. They are the
rollers. Their skill is judged not only by the amount of
cheroots they can roll but also in the correct manner of
rolling. If it is rolled in a haphazard way e.g., by not taking
the right amount of the filler then the chercot may either be
too tightly or too loosely rolled. If the cheroot burns evenly
when lighted then it is an evidence that the hsebawlaik
has been rolled in the correct manner. A good hsebawlaik
should, therefore, be neatly and evenly rolled. The sorting

As regards working conditions it appears to the
casual observer that in most of the hselaikkhons the work-
ers are crowded in relatively small areas. This unsanitary
working condition would have bad effect on their health.
Moreover, the strong smell of tobacco, to an unaccustomed
person is a nauseating one, often leading to giddiness. Even
to the accustomed workers, the constant smell of tobacco
would tell on their health in the long run. Then again
sitting for 10 hours with only a small break is not a healthy
condition at all, more so when the job is a monotonous
one. But in spite of all these unsatisfactory working con-
ditions, workers on the whole seem to be happy and con-
tented, often singing and joking among themselves while
they work.
The bargaining strength of the workers against the
employers is weak, because the workers are not organized
under any Trade Union. Hence there is practically no
labour trouble. There was only one instance during the pre-
war years when the workers of Ma Sein Nyun hselaik-
khon went on strike in sympathy with the general labour
strike. Since then, there have been no strikes whatsoever,
in any of the hselaihkhons.

the Shan States may not be close to the railways so there
is again the additional cost of transport. Hence it is more
convenient and economical to bring down the goods in
motor rucks.

Apart from the common sizes of 4 inches to 5 inches
in length, there are also extra-large size hsebawlaik of 7
to 8 inches in length and about 1 inch in diameter. These
big sized hsebawlaiks are demanded on great occasions
like marriage ceromonies, the Shinbyu and ear-boring
ceromonies and other charitable occasions. They are also
demanded to a small extent by elderly people, who smoke
these big cheroots at leisure in their homes. Business is
brisk when festivals are in great height, which falls during
the months of November, December and January and
extending to March and April i.e., a total of 6 months in
a year. In the districts this period coincides with the harvest
season and is the period when cultivators have much money
in their pockets, to spend in charities (AH-HLUS) 200
and at Pwes 8. It is on these occasions that the demand
for hsebawlaik is at its highest. The slack season for the
hsebawlaik trade is said to be the rainy season.
We shall now deal with the problem of standardisa-
tion and the scope for improvement in the quality of the
product. The primary advantage of standardisation is
reduced cost.
The advocates of standardisation, therefore, based
their arguments on the grounds that standardisation will
lead to (1) increase output (2) economy in costs of produc-
tion (3) prevention of such wastages as competetive adver-
tisement (4) and subsequent reduction in commodity prices,
leading to consumers' benefit. Thus, in war time Britain,
we hear of such forms of standardisation as utility cloth,

utility wool, utility furniture etc. On the other hand the

therefore be a blow to the consumer. Moreover, the
prevailing prices of hsebawlaik cannot be said to be very
high, they are well within reach of the masses or 'Ludu'.
So, there would not be much gain from standardisa-
tion-it can only do harm by restricting consumers' choice.
This does not mean to say, however, that there is
no scope for improvement in the quality of the product.
The present procedure in the manufacturing process needs
reform, especially from the health point of view. The
practice of drying the hse and hse-yo out on the pavements
and streets is not at all hygienic. Dust and other foreign
particles can easily get into it, and this may cause irritation
to the throats of smokers. The drying of filter tips out in
the open air calls for similar objection.
The primitive methods of ripening, curing and flavour-
ing of tobacco need also serious attention. This calls for
scientific treatment of tobacco, newhat on Western lines,
so as to get the best out of the tobacco. The authorities
should step into this matter so as to promote the quality
of Burma tobacco to be on par with the best in the world
and with the view to making Burma tobacco an exportable
cash crop.
What is needed then is standardisation to
establish standards of excellence and quality in raw materi-
als. The improvement in the quality of tobacco would bring
good results to the quality of hsebawlaik. Lastly, the size
of the hsebawlaik should be standardised to a more con-
venient size which should be neither too bulky nor
too long.
Given these improvements both in the fields of
treatment of tobacco and in the manufacturing process,
there is ample scope for the hsebawlaik industry to im-
prove its position in the future to be one of the most
thriving and paying Cottage Industry of Burma.
