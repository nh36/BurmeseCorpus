URBAN STUDY OF THE SITTANG VALLEY*
by
KHIN MA LAY✰✰
This paper, mainly based on aerial photographs of 1953, confines solely to the four
major towns of the Sittang Valley', namely, Pegu, Toungoo, Yamethin and Pyinmana. The
first three are district headquarters while the last, though not a district headquarters, is an
important town and in fact surpasses its corresponding district headquarters, Yamethin, in
economic importance.
In Burma, the size or density of population and the method of local government
adopted are relevant factors in considering what constitutes a town. In general settlements
with not less than 5,000 inhabitants are primarily regarded as towns. But Census towns
of Burma also include all municipalities and cantonments even though with less than
5,000 inhabitants.2 Thus according to the above definition Pegu, Toungoo, Yamethin
and Pyinmana are towns.
They are mainly agricultural and distributive towns acting as collecting, marketing
and exporting centres of the agricultural produce of the country around, and in return.
import goods not produced locally and distribute these to the countryside. Generally they
occupy an intermediate position between the industrial units on the one hand, and the
purely agricultural or rural communities, on the other. Further they perform functions of
political, educational, social and religious character. They are administrative centres and
contain centralised institutions such as schools and hospitals, and offer employment to the
rural population in public administration, police forces, or in factories, such as rice and
saw mills, and sugar refinery of Pyinmana.

examine the effect of urbanisation on the use of the land in the vicinity of the towns.3

1. PEGU (17° 20' N, 96° 29' E)
Pegu, situated some 47 miles northeast of Rangoon, stands on both the banks of
the Pegu river and is about 20 miles west of the Sittang. Its Municipality was formed in
1883 and by 1885 a large area to the west of the town, Zaingganaing, was added to the
Municipality.2
History of Pegu
The town, within the walls, the ruins of which are still traceable, lies on the left
bank of the Pegu river. To the east lies the original town of Pegu which was founded in
573 A.D. by the Talaings from Thaton.³ The present town was believed to have been the
work of Bayinnaung but lately the Historical Commission has found out that it was the
work of Dammasedi.
Pegu originally derived its importance from the fact that it was an important port.
It was accessible from all parts of the alluvial plain around it. Pegu itself was not a
seaport, but it had regular commercial relations with India since very early times through
Martaban its chief seaport, and it was often referred to by travellers as a flourishing city.*
After the sixteenth century Pegu gradually lost its importance as a capital. Physio-
graphic as well as political changes contributed to this tragic decline of a once prosperous
capital. Due to the silting of the Sittang, Pegu was no longer accessible to merchant ships.
Seaports such as Syriam and Rangoon received most of its exports and Pegu became an
insignificant country town.

Pegu had been a scene of numerous wars fought between the Talaings and the
Burmese and when the latter overran and conquered Pegu in the eighteenth century, they
tried every means to efface all traces of Talaing nationality and ravaged and devastated
the town and dispersed its inhabitants, many of whom fled to Siam and other parts of
Lower Burma. It was therefore not surprising that Pegu on its annexation by the British
in 1853 was a wilderness. The British Government was very much concerned with the
sparsity of its population and various remedial measures such as the bunding of the
Sittang, construction of the railway and road, were adopted. Uptil the 1911 census we can
find a stream of immigrants into Pegu and other parts of Lower Burma, and after that the
rise in population was due more to the natural increase than to immigration.

Sex Ratio for Pegu in 1931 was 82. For indigenous races there were 108 females
to 100 males, while the figures for the Chinese and Indians were 46 and 42 respectively.1
This disparity in sex ratio is largely ascribed to the existence of large foreign elements in
which the males preponderate. Many Indians, who came to Burma, did not stay long
but returned to their country after they had accumulated enough money, and thus did not
bring their women with them. However, by about 1930 or so, there was a tendency for
this sex disparity to be decreasing, probably because seasonal immigration from India
slackened. This disparity in sex ratio also applies to the other towns.
Functional Areas of Pegu²
The two distinct functional areas of Pegu town are the commercial area and the
residential area.
Between the walls of the old town and the Pegu river is the market together with
other commercial buildings. It is here that shops and stores are clustered together and
that urban functions are concentrated. This commercial or business centre is easily distin-
guishable from the residential area by the kind of buildings and their spacing. In the
residential areas the buildings are likely to be farther apart.
Other functions such as Government, education, religion, and recreation are not
segregated into areas of conspicuous size. Within the walls of the old town are the
Government offices, hospital, jail, police lines and schools. The Government quarters are
to be found to the north of the town along the main road to Toungoo. Here many new
buildings have sprung up recently. The station is on the other side of the river which is
crossed by a substantial iron double-girder bridge. The essentially Burmese quarter of
Zaing-ga-naing is also found on this side of the river.
The town does not possess any large industries, except a few rice mills and saw
mills, which are found mainly along the Pegu river, and a brick kiln north of the railway
station. Towards the outskirts of the town, the buildings and roads are more of the
rural type.

Religious buildings, such as pagodas and monastaries are numerous, thus showing
that religion has a very strong hold in the life of the Burmese people. Within the walls
of the old town of Pegu is the famous Shwemawdaw pagoda which had been damaged by
earthquakes but had been renovated in 1955 by the then Prime Minister U Nu.
Land Use
Around the town are vast stretches of paddy-fields while along the Pegu river there
are scattered small patches of riverine crops (Fig. 1). One peculiar feature of the town of
Pegu is that more than half of the area within the ancient walls is cultivated with paddy.
This area seems to have been residential in the early period, but after the devastation of
Pegu and later resettlement, people seemed to have clustered near the chief means of
communications, viz. the river, road and railway.
To the north and east of the town there are some patches of low vegetation. On
the low hills east of the Shwemawdaw pagoda, attempts are being made to clear some of
the scrubs, and vegetables have been planted in neat rows. Similar market gardening
strips are to be found to the north of the town. Out of the four towns under study, this
is the only place where a considerable influence of the town on the land use of its imme.
diate area is noticeable.
Communications
The town has good means of communications with other parts of the country. The
Rangoon-Mandalay road and railway pass through it. It is also the junction for the
railway branch line to Moulmein. Within the town, there are fairly broad streets,
well-raised and metalled. Further from the centre of the town, however, the roads are
not well kept and are in fact only slightly better than village roads.
Pegu is an important rice and timber centre. Logs from the forest reserves are
floated down the Pegu river and some of them are sawn into timber at the saw-mills along
the river bank, while others are sent to Rangoon.

The history of Toungoo dates back to 1510 A.D., when it was founded by King
Mingyinyo who made it his capital and was then known by its classic name Ketumadi.*
From the chronological records, it appears that there is not much significance attached to
its site. The King moved his capital from Dwayawadi,2 which is about a mile southeast
of the present town, to Toungoo chiefly because the former, situated on the Sittang river,
was being Eroded away.³ However, unlike Pegu, the historic importance of Toungoo as
a capital was short-lived, for Mingyinyo's successor, Tabinshweti, moved his seat of
government from Toungoo to Pegu in 15404 and from then onwards the fame of Toungoo
faded away.
The old town was roughly a square surrounded by a moat. Remains of both the
moat and wall can still be seen today. In the south-east corner and near the present
railway station, is a fort roughly about 400 ft. square. It was built by the British in 18645
when Toungoo was an important post on the frontier of British Burma.
Population
The population of Toungoo in 1872 was over 10,000 and it increased up to the
period 1891-1901 when there was a decrease due to removal of troops in 1893. Toungoo
has since been growing steadily. The rate of increase between 1872 and 1931 is over 116%
which again is mainly the result of the opening of the road and railway, followed by the
development of a wider market at Toungoo.

Toungoo has no important industries. Along the Sittang river, due east of the
jail, there are saw mills. A few rice mills are found towards the outskirts of the town
and are generally owned by foreigners, mainly Chinese.
To the east, south-east and south-west of the present town are extensive rural
residencies. East of the Sittang river is the Karen quarter.
Land Use
The rural land use, chiefly of paddy cultivation, comes right up to the fringes of the
town (Fig. 2). Riverine cultivation is carried on along the Sittang river, and here con.
siderable quantities of fruits and vegetables are raised to supply the people of Toungoo.
To the north of the town is a fairly large low area covered with grass and scrubs, which
during the rains collects water and becomes a vast marsh forming an ideal fishing ground.
Communications
The town is on the road and railway from Rangoon to Mandalay and is thus con-
nected with both Upper and Lower Burma. It is an important centre for the produce of
the Karen hills, mainly betel-nuts. Toungoo had since 1881 been the headquarters of the
well-known timber company, McGregor & Co.' It is also noted for Toungoo coffee
which is roasted and ground from berries brought from the plantations on the Karen hills.
Before the Second World War, it also handled the wolfram mined at Mawchi. There is a
road running eastward to the hill station of Thandaung. The Sittang river also serves as
a useful means of communication with Rangoon.
YAMETHIN (20° 25' N, 96° 9′ E).
Yamethin is on the water-shed between the Irrawaddy and Sittang rivers. The
Municipality was constituted in 1888,2
History of Yamethin
Yamethin is believed to have been founded by Anawratha in the eleventh century.3
Like Pegu its older town lies in the south-east corner, north of the Kyeni Tank. Traces
of the moat are still distinct on the aerial photographs, except in the south and west where
the moat had been filled up for settlements. Although fortified like Pegu and Toungoo,

It is possible that its site is largely responsible for the founding of Yamethin. For
centuries the town had been a centre for the caravan trade from the Shan States to Myin-
gyan on the Irrawaddy. This fact of being a junction for the age-old trade route between
the Shan States and the Irrawaddy might have been the main reason for the British to
choose this town as the district headquarters after the annexation of Upper Burma in 1885,
and thus provides an additional reason for the importance of the town today.
The town commands a suitable site occupying the highest point in the central plain
of the district which has been cultivated since very early times by means of irrigation from
the Kyeni Tank, situated on the eastern outskirts of the town. This is an old Burmese
work dating back to the eleventh century and has been remodelled by the British Govern-
ment towards the end of the last century.

PYINMANA (19° 44' N, 96° 14′ E)
About 50 miles south of Yamethin, on the Ngalaik chaung, is Pyinmana, the

History of Pyinmana
Unlike the other towns Pyinmana does not have a prominent historical background.
It is a fairly recent town, built in 1832 by a dacoit leader, Tha Dok Gyi.2 Uptil the time

a bridge built across the stream by Tha Dok Gyi to enable the people to cross over the

north and settled in the newly built village. At the time of annexation by the British the
town did not extend as far west as now, and also it was confined to the south of the
Ngalaik stream.

Having given a preliminary account of the history and land use of the four towns,
we shall now turn to the two main purposes which have been mentioned at the beginning
of this paper.
DIFFERENCES BETWEEN THE TOWNS
To begin with, it was proposed to assess the difference in the plans and development
between the two historical capitals and the other two towns. A careful study of the four
towns bring out an appreciable difference in the patterns of the towns. The ancient
capitals of Toungoo and Pegu have a definite rectangular pattern. The old town lies
within the walls while the newer town is built up around it. Even Yamethin, though it
does not possess such a remarkable historical record, seems to conform to this rectangular
pattern. Pyinmana, on the other hand, being built only in the early nineteenth century,
has no such pattern. It is a large straggling town.
Being old capitals, Toungoo and Pegu were fortified towns and the remains
of the historic walls and moats are still traceable today. Further the older
towns have much broader and well-laid out roads running more or less at right
angles to one another. In Pyinmana, roads, except near the commercial core,
radiate in all directions with a general lack of regularity and alignment.
Yamethin, being an old town dating back to the early eleventh century, also has well-laid
out roads but they are narrow, ill-kept and on the whole no better than village cart-tracts.
This is probably because it is just an overgrown village and that its importance is mainly
derived from its administrative functions. It still remains materially unimproved and in
fact in 1931, was not considered a major town of Burma.¹
Since both Pegu and Toungoo had played vital roles in Burmese history, they are
naturally rich in antiquities which are of tremendous value to the archaeologists and histo-
rians. Such evidences of the past are, however, very meagre in the case of Pyinmana.
Another remarkable difference concerns religion. Pegu and Toungoo contain
numerous pagodas and monasteries which had been built many hundreds of years ago, and
many of them are now in a deplorable state of decay. Even in Yamethin, there are fairly
widespread religious buildings. This clearly shows the strong impact of religion on the
people of Burma since very early times.
Pyinmana, on the other hand, has very few religious buildings and those that exist
are also not very remarkable for their architecture. Neither are they of great size and
antiquity nor are they notable centres of religious gatherings. The probable reason for
this comparative scarcity of pagodas and monasteries is that, as Pyinmana developed very

Lastly, the towns of Pyinmana and Yamethin have in addition to the regular daily

From this we can see that historical capitals were planned with well laid out roads.
Even towns like Yamethin which were important trading centres in the reign of Burmese
kings, also possess this quality. However, such towns like Pyinmana, which were mere
villages with no important functions before the arrival of the British and later developed
into towns, do not have this planned look but assume sprawling shapes. Most of the
towns and large villages in the lower Sittang Valley are of this type, because they developed
after the opening of the Valley by the British by means of the trunk road and railway.
They grew up very rapidly when the modern means of transport induced the people from
nearby villages or other parts of the country to settle at these new places. As they
progressed so quickly the authorities did not have time to plan the settlements, and thus
in these places roads radiate in all directions without any regularity and precision.
EFFECTS OF TOWNS ON LAND USE
In the second place it was our intention to examine whether the development of
these towns exert any influence on the land use in their neighbourhood, in the forms of
market-gardening, livestock and diary farming.
Urbanization entails congregation of people who are mainly engaged in trades
other than agriculture. Thus the urban population depends on others for the supply of
their requirements of perishable goods such as fruits, vegetables, and diary produce. It is,
therefore, natural to expect urban centres to exert striking influence on the use of the land
around them.
But in general urbanization in Burma does not have any notable effect on the land
in the immediate vicinity of the urban centres. There are, however, exceptions such as
Rangoon and Mandalay where distinct belts of land use have developed just outside the
limits of the towns. Around Rangoon is an extensive market-gardening belt. Outside
Mandalay, there are large fruit gardens particularly mangoes. The remaining towns of
Burma have rarely anything in the nature of an intermediate zone between the rural and
urban uses of the land. It is, therefore, not surprising to find that the four towns under
consideration, with the exception of Pegu, do not have any perceptible effect on land use.
Even in the case of Pegu, the development of a small market gardening area on its out-
skirts seems to be very recent.

Secondly, in the Burmese towns, the houses, except in the fairly congested business
sectors, which mainly occupy the geographical centres of the towns, have small plots of
land around them. In most cases these plots of land are planted with flowers for decora-
tive purposes or with some fruits and vegetables for domestic requirements. Thus these
small gardens around most of the houses supply a portion of the domestic requirements of
the people.
Poorer sections of the urban population, occupying the outskirts of the towns, or
people in nearby villages, may grow surplus fruits and vegetables in the compounds around
their dwellings and sell them to the towns. But the extent of their cultivation is too small
to be seen from the aerial photographs of the scale available. In any case it is doubtful
whether they should be treated as market-gardening.
Further, the existence of riverine cultivation along the streams near the towns,
retard to a great extent the development of a distinct market-gardening belt just outside
the towns. Fruits and vegetables grown on the alluvial soils along the banks of the
streams nearly supply all the requirements of the towns. Around Pyinmana especially
there is a fair amount of riverine cultivation along the Ngalaik stream. In Yamethin
where no riverine cultivation is carried on, the needs of its inhabitants are met by the
supplies from nearby villages, where there is almost always a surplus. It is also probable
that some of the riverine crops from Pyinmana area may find their way to Yamethin.
The above remarks will enable us to understand why out of the four towns under
examination, only Pegu shows some influence on the use of the land in its immediate
vicinity. Due to insurrection in the district, well-to-do people of the nearby villages
evacuated to Pegu and eventually settled there so that its population has grown very
rapidly since the 1941 Census. This rapid growth in its population provides an incentive
for the development of market gardening near the town. Moreover, it is probable that
with similar rapid growth in the population of Rangoon, due to the same reason, the
produce of riverine cultivation which formerly served Pegu is now sent by rail and trucks
to Rangoon for better prices. This also gives another impetus for the development of
market gardening outside Pegu so as to cater the needs of its population.
