AN ACOUSTICAL ANALYSIS OF THE VIBRATIONS OF
BAMBOO BARS USED IN PATTALARS*
MAUNG MAUNG KHA**
INTRODUCTION
About twenty five years ago a group of physicists in the physics laboratories of
Harvard University began to study the mechanical behaviour of violins, in the hope of
finding out how the best ones differ in their vibration from those of inferior kind. The
investigations in this field were made with the hope that the results might lead to improve-
ments in new violins. The first publication' included a variety of harmonic patterns
disclosed in violin tones, the response (or frequency) curves obtained from many old and
new violins, etc. Similar investigations were also made in the Physics Department of the
University of Chicago and some other institutions. From the results of numerous experi-
ments it is now established that with the help of science, violins can be produced with the
tones almost as rich as the old stradivarious violins.
About the same time as the Harvard group conducting the first scientific investiga-
tion on the mechanical behaviour of violins the frequencies of various notes produced in
"pattalars" (Burmese xylophones) were measured in the physics laboratories of the
University of Rangoon by Professor J. West and U Khin Zaw and in the Cavendish
Laboratory, Cambridge, by Mr. Furnivall. Their results were reported in the paper entitled
'Burmese Music', by U Khin Zaw in the Journal of the Burma Research Society². The
The present paper contains results of experiments designed to determine the frequency
spectra of various notes produced by bamboo bars of a number of 'pattalars' with a view
to finding their overtones or partials and possible effects of moisture on the frequency
of the notes emitted by bamboo bars.
THEORY
The transverse vibration of bars such as those of tuning-forks, 'pattalars' and
western xylophones follows laws quite different from those followed by the vibration of
strings such as those of the pianoforte, the violin, the harp, the guitar etc. In theory
several modes of transverse vibration are possible, but, there are only two cases which are
of interest from a musical point of view. The first of these is often referred to as a
'fixed-free' or 'clamped-free' condition, in which the bar or the rod is fastened at one end
and free at the other. The second case, called 'free-free' condition is that in which the
rod or the bar is free at both ends. Tuning-forks and reeds are examples of the first case

It is seen from above relations that higher frequency bars should be shorter than
the lower frequency bars, and that the length of a bar is 1.414 times the length of the bar
whose frequency is an octave higher than the frequency of the former.
The lengths of the first, eighth, fifteenth and twenty second bamboo bars, which give
the various C-notes, on a pattalar made by the late U Chit Maung are respectively found
to be 47 cm, 38.7 cm, 30.8 and 23.4 cm. Three other pattalars of the same make give
almost the same figures. The ratios of the first to the eighth, the eighth to the fifteenth and
the fifteenth to the twenty second bars are found to be 1.214, 1.256, and 1.312 respectively.
These values may be compared with the theoretical value of 1.414. However, it must be
remembered that the bamboo bars used in ‘pattalars' are not of the same thickness. Slight
tuning of the bars are usually made by shaving off the inner part of the bamboo, at the
centre for lowering the pitch and at the ends for raising the pitch. This process may be
compared with the changing of the pitch of a tuning-fork. If a tuning-fork is filed near the
stalk (clamped end), the pitch is lowered; and if it be filed near the free end the pitch is
raised. The effect of shaving at the centre of a bar is the same as lengthening of
the bar or loading at the free ends, which causes lowering of the pitch. On the other
hand, the effect of shaving at the ends corresponds to shortening of the bar or loading
at the centre, which raises the pitch.
When vibrating at its fundamental frequency, the "free-free" bar has two nodes,
which were found by Rayleigh to be at 0.224L from each end of the bar, L being the length
of the bar. For this reason, the bars of the pattalar or the xylophone should be
supported at the points 0.224 times the length of the bar from the two ends. Incidentally,
the bars in U Chit Maung's pattalars are usually supported at lengths ranging from 0.213L
to 0.217L from the ends, while those of a pattalar of an unknown make are supported
at lengths varying from 0.174L to 0.227L.

Experimental Investigation
The present experimental investigation consists of two parts. Since the presence
and strength of the partials which make up a musical note determine the quality of the
note, the partials revealed in the frequency spectra of the notes produced by the bamboo
bars of a number of 'pattalars' are studied in the first part. Effects of moisture content in
the surface layers of bamboo bars are determined in the second part of the investigation.

Effect of Resonator
The notes are also analysed for the pattalar with no resonator. With the sensitivity
at 100 Mv range, the effect of the resonator is not appreciably detected. When the
sensitivity is increased to 10 Mv range, it is observed that lower frequency tones lower than
the fundamental of the bars are detected. This is observed especially at the extreme bars,
namely No. 1 and No. 2. These two bars are nearest to the points on the resonator at which
the bars are fastened. Probably, striking at these bars causes more rigorous mechanical
vibrations of the resonator, and low frequency tones are probably the result of
these vibrations.
Fig. 36 shows the curves for No. 1 bar. The upper curve is obtained when the
resonator is used and the lower curve is for the case where the bars are supported by hand
and not by resonator. It can be observed that low frequency tones below the fundamental
appear and that the fundamental and the partials rise in intensity (becomes louder) when
the resonator is used.
Fig. 37 shows the curves for No. 24 bar. The upper curve is obtained when the
resonator is used. The lower curve is obtained when the bars are not mounted on the
resonator but supported by hand. It is observed again that low frequency tones below
the fundamental are produced.

General Conclusion
It is observed that the values of the upper partials in most cases are found to
agree fairly well with the calculated values given in Table I. The lower frequency bars
are found to be rich in upper partials which are almost as strong as the fundamentals
in many cases. The number and magnitude of upper partials decrease as we
pass from lower frequency bars towards higher frequency bars. From bar No. 18
towards the highest frequency bar (No. 23 or No. 24), the notes are found to be monotone,
having no upper partials. The presence of upper partials in the lower frequency region
and the absence of them in the higher frequency region have been confirmed by viewing
the wave forms in an oscilloscope. Upper partials, particularly the second partial, which
dominate in the low frequency bars are extremely transient in character. It has been
confirmed by the patterns in the oscilloscope. For this reason, the ear detects the more
sustained fundamental note. Upper partials are heard when the bar is struck continuously
for some length of time.
The part of the bamboo bar at which the striker strikes can cause a change in tone
quality. If the bamboo bar is struck at about a quarter of the length from one end (i.e. at
the node of the fundamental), certain partials appear and also the magnitude of the partials
changes in some cases.
The padding of the striker knob also plays an important part. If the striker without
felt covering, but having only a few layers of muslin is used, higher partials, which are not
found ordinarily, appear. Upper partials appear even in the high frequency region of the
pattalar, where upper partials are ordinarily absent.
The effect of the resonator is not detected easily: that is, the difference between
the presence and absence of the resonator cannot be detected by ear from a distance. But
near the pattalar, the low frequency tones having frequency lower than the fundamental of
the bamboo bar are observed. The low frequency tones are believed to be due to vibra-
tions of the resonator itself.
The effect of moisture content in bamboo bars on the frequency of the note emitted
by them is found to be quite remarkable. Frequency of the note is found to fall with
increase of moisture content in the bamboo bar. The variation of pitch of the bamboo
bars ranges from 40 to 70 cents which is by no means negligible. The variation of pitch
of such magnitude is easily detected by a skilled musician.
