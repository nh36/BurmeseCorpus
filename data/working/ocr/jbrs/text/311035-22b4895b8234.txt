EUROPEANS IN BURMA
THE EARLY PORTUGUESE
By far the best account of Asia at the arrival of the Portuguese is
that given by Duarte Barbosa. He went to India with the fleet of 1501
and returned to Portugal about 1516. He then entered the Spanish ser-
vice and sailed with Magellan on his voyage of 1519, which is famous
for the first circumnavigation of the world; but unfortunately was killed
in 1521 not long after the death of Magellan. Most of his time was
spent in the west of India, but he seems to have had personal knowledge
of some parts of Coromandel, and of Malacca, North Sumatra and possi-
bly Pegu. His notes are fullest and most trustworthy where he can
speak from first hand knowledge, but he was diligent in collecting in-
formation about other places and must have been remarkably acute in
sifting fact from fiction in these travellers' tales, for there is little that
it is necessary to reject. The work seems to have been completed in
1517-18. The following notes are taken from a translation edited by
M. L. Dames for the Hakluyt Society (2 vols. 1918 and 1921).
In his account of Indian ports there are some references to trade
with Pegu and the Far East. Thus we read that in Coilam (Quilon in
Travancore).
Hither come Moors, Heathen and Christians in great numbers. The Moors and
Heathen are great traders and possess many ships dealing in goods of divers kinds,
in which they sail in all directions, to Charamandel and Ceilam, to the Isla, to
Benguala, Malaca, Camatra and Peeguu, but they trade not with Cambaya.
His note on the Isles of Palandura (the Maldives) is of interest as
illustrating the use made by the Portuguese of their sea-power:
Many ships of Moors which pass from China, Maluco, Peeguu, Malaca, Camatra,
Benguala and Ceilam towards the Red Sea touch at these islands to water and take
in supplies and other things needful for their voyage. At times they arrive here so
battered that they discharge their cargoes and let them go to the bottom. And
among these isles many and rich vessels of Moors are cast away, which, crossing
the sea, dare not through dread of our ships finish their voyage to Malabar.
With regard to the kingdom of Bengal he writes that there are
strangers of many lands
All of these are great merchants, and they possess great ships after the fashion
of Meca; others there are from China, which they call juncos, which are of great
size and carry great cargoes. With these they sail to Charamandal Malaca,
Camatra, Peeguu, Cambaya and Ceilam, and deal in goods of many sorts with this
country and many others.
On the far side of Asia he notes that the Chinese are great
merchants, and "sail to Malaca with all the Chinese goods which have
a good sale there." Apparently in his time the Chinese did not sail
beyond Malacca, and the junks west of this were owned by Moors.

Barbosa seems to be the first European to mention Burma. That
is not strange. For within the limits of modern Burma the old-establish-
ed kingdoms were Ava, Pegu and Arakan; Burma was the name given
to the rising principality of Toungoo.¹
Going past the Kingdom of Bengala and following the coast towards the south
there is another Heathen Kingdom called that of Burma, and the King thereof is
called the same. There are no Moors therein inasmuch as it has no seaport which
they can use for their traffic. The inhabitants are black men; they go naked from
the waist up, and are clad in cotton garments below. They have their own indul-
gences (or idolatry) and houses of prayer. They are oftimes at war with the King
of Peeguu.
As to this kingdom there is no further information for the reason that there is
no means of sailing thither, 2 save that on one side it is bounded by Bengala and on
the other by Peeguu.
The next entry relates to Arakan, or, as Barbosa gives it Aracangil,
possibly Aracan-gri.
In the immediate interior of this Kingdom of Berma there is another Kingdom.
also of the Heathen, which possesses no port on the sea bounded on one side by
Bengal and on the other by the Country of Daba (i.e. of Ava). The King thereof
is a Heathen also, and a great Lord. They say that he possesses towns and cities
in great numbers, he has also many horses, and elephants to boot, which come from
the Kingdom of Peeguu. His folk are tawny and go bare, save that from the waist
down they are girt with raiment of cotton. They are given to the use of bows
(? ornaments) of gold and precious stones. They worship idols and have great
houses of prayer. The King is ofttimes at war with the Kings his neighbours, of
whom some do him obeisance and some pay him tribute.
It is strange that Barbosa should know so little of Aracan as to say
that it "possesses no port on the sea," but his ignorance suggests that
at that time the trade of Aracan cannot have been of much importance,
possibly because of the decline of Ava. Perhaps if he had known more
of the place we should have lost a pleasant story. The king, he says,
lives luxuriously in very good houses, and wherever he may be sojourning he
has many tanks of water and right pleasant gardens, for the more part in twelve
chief cities of the Kingdom, in each he has a very fine palace and a governour in
charge thereof, and each one of these takes every year from his own city twelve
maidens born in that year.... (These are brought up in luxury)....and every year
they take to the king twelve who have reached the age of twelve years, where-
soever he may be, all are sent well washed and clean with very thin garments, in
a white robe, on the edge of which the name of each is written. Early in the
morning they are sent to an open space and there seated in the sun, and must stay
there fasting till noon in the full force of the sun, whereby they sweat so much that
the garments they wear are completely wet....The wet clothes they carry where
the King is with many of his kinsmen and Governors of his Realm with other Lords
and Gentlemen, and he smells them one by one.... the King keeps for himself
those that smell well.
Those that have not a good smell he gives to his courtiers.

The following entry relates to Pegu.
This following the seacoast and leaving the Kingdom of Berma there is to the
south another Heathen Realm of great fertility and with much trade by sea in
many kinds of goods. They call it Peeguu; it possesses three or four havens where
there are rich merchants and great towns inhabited as well by Moors as by the
Heathen who possess it. The city of Peeguu is situate inland seven or eight
leagues from the sea on a small river which is a branch of another river, very great,
which flows through this Kingdom coming down from certain mountains. During
some months of the year it brings down so much water that it overflows its natural
course and inundates widespread lands on which grows and is harvested great store
of rice with which this city is right well supplied, as well as with fish and other foods
which are laden at its ports. There are in that place great ships of three or four
masts, which they call Juncos, which sail to Malaca (and Samatra) and many other
places. They carry from this Kingdom of Peeguu great store of white cane sugar
in loaves. Hither come every year many moorish ships to trade and bring abund-
ance of printed Cambaya (and Paleacate) cloths, both cotton and silk which they
call patolas (Canarese = silk cloth).
==
These are coloured with great skill, and are here worth much money; they also
bring opium, copper, great store of scarlet-in-grain cloth, coral threaded, round
(and in branches and well set) vermilion, quicksilver, rosewater, and many other
Cambaya drugs, and they take here cargoes of very fine lac which is produced in
the country, and mace, cloves, and many other goods from China brought hither
from Malaca, and with the rest of their money they buy abundance of musk (and
rubies) which comes from an inland city, called Ava, whereof I will speak below.
The Heathen of this Kingdom are much given to Idol-worship, they go bare cover-
ing only their middles; they are not good fighting men.
They are very luxurious in their habits. They wear on their members certain
hawk-bells, round, closed and very large which are joined and fixed inside the skin
and the flesh so as to make it very large. Of these they wear as many as five,
some of gold, some of silver or other metal according to those who carry them,
and when they walk they give out a loud sound which they hold to be a distinction
and to be admired, and the more of them the more honourable. The women delight
in this and do not like the men who have them not. I say no more of this on
account of its indecency.
The King of Peeguu is called the King of the White Elephant, there are in this
Kingdom great mountains where many wild elephants breed, and it is their rule
to take one every day to be trained, and this they ever have a great number which
they sell in many lands but most of them in the Kingdoms of Narsyngua (Viziabagar).
Malabar and Cambaya. They have also in this land many very proper nags, great
walkers, whereof they make use perpetually and many horses also which they ride
on high-pommelled saddles. With these and their elephants they go to war as well
as with a multitude of foot soldiers. They have also great stores of sheep and
swine bred indoors and wild as well. These Heathen are mighty riders and hunters.
As may be seen from the above account, he knew much more of
Pegu than of other places in Burma; possibly because it was the chief
port and most frequented by foreign merchants, or else from personal
experience the "high-pommelled saddles " suggests the eye-witness;
they would not have made such an impression at second hand. Here, as
with Varthema, it is difficult to understand the reference to sheep.

Leaving this city of Peeguu and sailing towards Malaca there are three or four
sea-havens which belong to the Kingdom of Peeguu, whereof I know not the
names. Between these is one called Martabam whither come many ships from
divers regions to trade and obtain cargoes of food and many other goods, for the
most part of lac which is produced in that land is is better by far than that of
Narsyngua. This the Indians and Persians call laquar Martabam "Martaban lac."
As to this lac some say it is the gum of a tree and others that it grows on the fine
branches of the trees as in our own land the grain grows on the holm-oak, and
this argument appears more in accordance with nature as it grows on trees and
fine twigs, which for that reason cannot yield so much gum. At this town are
made also many great porcelain jars, very big, strong and fair to see; there are
some of them which will hold a pipe of water. They are glazed in black and
greatly esteemed by the Moors who take them from this place with great store of
benzoin in loaves.

In the inland country of this Kingdom of Peeguu, going thence towards Malaca
between the Kingdoms of Racanguy (Arakan) and Anseaom (Siam) there is a
Heathen Kingdom wherein among other great towns there is one exceeding great
city which they call Ava inhabited by wealthy merchants where there is much trade
in valuable precious stones, rubies and spinels which are found there. Hither gather
Moorish Heathen and Chati merchants from divers lands to purchase them, and
also the abundance of musk which there is there. And these stones and musk the
King takes for himself, and they are sold to foreigners who come hither to seek
for them on his account. From Cambaya they bring here copper, quicksilver,
vermillion, saffron, rose-water, opium, coloured Meca velvets, and many other things
valued among them, in exchange wherefor they take away the afore said stones
(and musk) which the King causes to be gathered among the mountains and rivers,
and to find it they dig very deep pits, the spinels are found on the ground level and
the rubies at the bottom, there are always skilful lapidaries in this city who know
them right well. And for the musk, it is found in certain animals as large as
gazelles.. (Here follows an account of how the true musk is obtained, and how it
is imitated)..and it is purchased for foreign lands in this city, as the true musk
is so strong that if you put it to the tip of your nose it makes you sneeze violently
and blood flow from your nostrils,
The King of Ava is a very great Lord owning plenty of jewels and gold. He
has great numbers of elephants and fighting-men. The land abounds in food.
He then passes on to the region of the ruby-mines, then called
Capellan.
And yet further inland beyond this city and Kingdom there is another Heathen
city under its own King, who nevertheless is subject and under the lordship of
Ava; which city or Kingdom they call Capellan. Around it are found many rubies
which are brought in for sale to the Ava market, and are much finer than those
of that place.

Going further forward, leaving the Kingdom of Peeguu, along the coast to-

Straightway after leaving the Kingdom of Pegu(sic)) there is a great city, a
sea-haven which they call Tanacary. Here are many merchants both Moors and

whole whereof they prize greatly in this kingdom of Anseam, and the merchants
take away hence everything which comes from Peeguu.

An Appendix on Precious Stones includes an account of rubies
"derived from divers merchants, both Moors and Heathen skilled in such
trade."
In the first place rubies are produced in the Land of India and are found chiefly
on a river called Pegu. These are the best and finest, and are called Numpuclo by
the Malabares, and when they are clean and without flaw they fetch a good price.
To test their quality the Indians put them on the tongue; those which are finest
and hardest are held to be the best. To test their transparency they fix them with
wax on a very sharp point and looking towards the sun they can find any blemish
however slight. They are also found in certain deep piys in the mountains beyond
the said river.
In Pegu they know how to clean but not how to polish them, and they there-
fore convey them to other countries, especially to Paleacate, Narsinga, Calicut and
the whole of Malabar, where there are excellent craftsmen who cut and mount them.
He also gives a table of values from_eight fine rubies weighing
1 fanam worth 10 fanams to one weighing 6 fanams 12 quilates, worth
1500 fanams. "Those are the prices commonly given for perfect rubies.
Those which are damaged or flawed or of a bad colour are worth much
less according as the purchaser may settle it.'
,,
As to the Ceylon rubies he remarks that they are worth less because
of their pale colour. As in Ava "the King keeps them for his own
profit," and those of especial value were not allowed to leave the king-
dom, and after treatment by fire were worth more than Pegu rubies.
The spinels "are found in the same manner as the fine rubies in the
Kingdom of Pegu; they are found in the mountains on the surface
of the ground. They are not so fine or of such good colour, and re-
semble rather garnets. Those which are perfect and clear fetch one-half
less than rubies."
