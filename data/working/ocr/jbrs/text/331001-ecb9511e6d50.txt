THE DAWN OF NATIONALISM IN BURMA.
Looking through some old papers recently I came
across one which, for me, has always represented the
dawn of Nationalism in Burma. It was a lecture delivered
by the late U May Oung over forty years ago. I had first
made his acquaintance when I was under training in
Moulmein as a newly appointed Assistant Commissioner.
He was then a young advocate but he went to England
to study for the Bar at about the same time that I was
transferred to my first post as Subdivisional Officer. In
1908 I was stationed in Rangoon and happened to notice
an announcement of a forth-coming lecture by him on
"The Modern Burman". Accordingly I took the oppor-
tuntiy to renew my acquaintance with him by attending
his lecture. Not long afterwards U Tun Nyein, the Gov-
ernment Translator, lent me a copy of the Journal of the
Siam Society * which gave me the idea of creating a
similar society in Burma that should bring together Bur-
mans and Europeans with a common interest in the wel-
fare of the country. It was with U May Oung and Pro-
fessor Duroiselle that I discussed the project. This lecture
by U May Oung may therefore be regarded as the earliest
germ of the Burma Research Society; and our members
may like to hear this distant echo from the Nationalist
dawn when "there was a spirit of reform in the air (and)
a gradually increasing demand for improvement."
J. S. Furnivall.
THE MODERN BURMAN
(A lecture by U May Oung, as reported in the Rangoon Gazette of 10th
August 1908)
At a meeting of the Rangoon College Buddhist
Association held in the College Hall on Sunday Maung May
Oung, B.A., LL.B., Barrister-at-law, President of the
Rangoon Young Men's Buddhist Association, delivered a
lecture "The Modern Burman: His Life and Notions,"
under the presidency of Mr. E. D. Marshall, Principal,
Rangoon College.

The lecturer said his hearers on hearing the title.
of his lecture must have at once thought of that excellent
work by "Shway Yoe," wherein that eminent "Subject of
the Great Queen" so vividly and completely described
their national life as it was a quarter of a century ago.
Recalling thus to their minds that great classic on Burma
and the Burman it might happen that when he had said
all he had to say they would experience a sense of disap-
pointment. By the expression "The Modern Burman" he
did not mean the native of Burma in general at the present
day it would require a whole volume to treat of him,-
but by modern Burman he meant the Burman who had
received the not unmixed blessing of a Western education.
He intended to speak of themselves, the large majority of
those assembled together in that hall. For it was on them
and those like them-on their training, acquirements, exer-
tions, that the future of their race would in no small
measure depend. On all sides they saw the ceaseless, ebb-
less tide of foreign civilisation and learning steadily creeping
over the land, and it seemed to him that unless they prepared
themselves to meet it, to overcome it, and to apply it to their
own needs, their national character, their institutions, their
very existence as a distinct nationality would be swept away,
submerged, irretrievably lost. In the course of this lecture
he could not do more than take just one or two points.
Let them then take their New Burman (from) the
time of his infancy. He was loved and petted and made
much (++) as of old by his fond parents, but (after) a
different fashion: where (formerly) his baby mind was care-
fully (trained to) be good and truthful, obedient and respectful,
the modern child had few opportunities to learn those simple
virtues. The father was generally too busy with his work or
his pleasures-(the) mother, imperfectly educated, (thought)
more of dress and (+) her neighbours' affairs than her
own offspring. The children (++) no longer the subduing
(influences) of the monastery and the religion. At an early
age-sometimes (as) early as five or six-the young Burman
of today must be off to the (English) school, where the
teaching was (purely) secular, and to pass his (examinations)
was henceforth his sole aim and object. In the class-room
there was little or no sympathy between teacher and pupils
in the playground the latter were left to themselves. When

they went home they were fed and sent off to play. Not
one father in fifty really sought to know what his child was
learning or how far he had progressed. Thus the boy
grew up, uncontrolled at home, insufficiently regulated at
school, free to follow his own bent in almost everything.
Even if he escaped the snares and pitfalls which beset his
youthful path he arrived at manhood with very little know-
ledge of anything outside his English school-books, with
much less comprehension of his own religion and literature,
with no settled convictions and no fixed purpose except to obtain
some easy and ready way of earning his livelihood. He went
out into the world, a Burman to all outward appearance, but
entirely out of harmony with his surroundings. He laughed
at the old school of men; they did not understand existence
as he did; their beliefs and superstitions were childish and
silly, their way of life was exceedingly uncomfortable-in
short, he thought there was nothing to be learnt from them.
Then he married, usually for money or for mere good looks,
begot children in his turn, and so the dismal round went on.
It was one of the greatest curses of their generation that
their young men were learning to drop Burman ideals, to
forget and even to despise the customs and the habits of his
ancestors, and to hanker after much that was bad and very
little that was good in those of alien races. Thus, for inst-
ance, he had adopted the luxuries but not the steadfastness
and high-souled integrity of the European, the lavish display
of wealth but not the business instincts of the Indian, the
love of sensuous ease but not the frantic perseverance of the
Chinaman. And it was chiefly in these directions that reform
is needed.
Let them proceed to one or two details. The first
question that naturally arose was that of religion. For if
there was any one thing that may be said to be peculiarly
distinctive about the Old Burman it was his intense devotion
to his faith, which entered into every thought and word
and deed of his ordinary life. A renowned scholar, arriving
in this country to study their faith, said of them :-"If I were
asked to describe in one word the people of Burma, if
I were asked, 'What is a Burman?' the answer that I
should make would be,_'A Burman is a Buddhist"." The
lecturer quoted at length the passage commencing with
these words, and continued that what they had to consider
to-day was, was the New Burman a Buddhist? Were they

there in that hall, Buddhists in very truth as well as in
name? It was in the eiucidation of this problem that they
would find a solution of a great many of the questions
which had arisen and were still arising at the present
moment with regard to the allegations concerning Burman
deterioration and Burman decay. No more did the present
day boy he who was brought up in towns-spend his early
years in the kyaung. It was true that the exigencies of
modern life did not permit of a similar training. But had
they any substitute for the old system, any method by
which they might preserve for their youths the regard and
the reverence which their fathers entertained for their
faith? Hitherto, none whatsoever. They went to school,
where religion formed no part of the curriculum, more
often where a strange faith was actively taught, and they
lost touch with the observances of their people. Their
parents and guardians, on whom lay the whole responsibility
of training their religious and moral faculties, were mostly
indifferent, and did not even call their attention to that
necessary part of their education, And meanwhile, the
higher they proceeded in their school and college career,
the more difficult became their tasks, the less leisure had
they for extraneous study; till the time arrived when they
had to face the world and fight their own battles, and then
(provided they had the time and the inclination to
analyse themselves) they found they had no more than
the slightest working knowledge of their Dhamma, not
enough even to comfort them. Twelve years ago he was
a student in that College, exactly like one of his audience.
Since then it had been his lot to be a teacher of the young,
and a traveller in many parts of this province; he had
. had many opportunities for the intimate study of their
countrymen, both young and old,-and he was compelled
to admit that his feeling was very nearly akin to one of
depression, disheartenment. For some he had met who
had no knowledge even of the Life of the Buddha, others
who recited their prayers were ignorant of their meaning,
still more, those who, when asked to explain their religion
for the benefit of an enquirer, hopelessly failed in the
attempt. Where was this going to end, what was this
state of affairs tending towards? It was coming slowly, but
surely; there were instances even now of Burmans who,
nominally Buddhists, had no religion in their hearts; nay,

he had himself met more than one who openly confessed
that he did not care for religion, it did not interest him.
And this was what they must not allow, what they could
not allow if they had any regard for their country-men and
their welfare. He would far rather wish to see them
Christians, Mahomedans, Hindus (so long as they were good
Christians, good Mahomedans, good Hindus), rather see
them followers of any faith that preached morality, than
being without any religion at all, enemies to themselves, most
dangerous examples to others. Thus, they saw that their
Modern Burman could hardly be called a true Buddhist, and
therein arose their first great calamity, a calamity which
underlay and explained almost everything else. For Bud-
dhism, with its unparalleled doctrine of selflessness (Anatta)
taught them Love and Compassion, drew them into one great
Bretherhood wherein one rejoiced in another's happiness.
and wept for another's woes, while yet preserving in his
mind an even balance of feeling for his own affairs. Take
away Buddhism, put nothing in its place, and they had the
powerful, iniquitous god of self, untrammelled, unchecked,
unhindered. Was it not then high time that they took
decisive measures to stop this growing evil? It was the
strongest proof of his assertions that he was to day address-
ing an Association of Young Burman Buddhists who had
recognised the evils of which he spoke and who had set
out resolutely to remedy them. He understood that they
themselves were endeavouring to secure a good grasp of
the principles of their Faith, and he trusted that when
their turn came to leave these pleasant surroundings and
to find themselves face to face with the grim realities of
existence, they would look around them and try to give to
others the benefit of their training and their knowledge.
Had the New Burman assimilated and adapted the
new notions to his own life, or had he merely substituted
them for his own? He feared that in a great many ways
the latter was true. And the main reason seemed to be
that he had lost his pride of race, and with it nearly all
feeling of community with his fellows. His nationality
was not with him, as it was with other races, an ever-present
reality, a circumstance to be proud of, a bond of sympathy
between him and those around him. It was this lack of
national feeling that kept him aloof from the people at
large and made him even look down on his less-cultured

brothers. It was to a great extent this absence of communal
sentiment that prevented him from forming commercial or
even social combinations. Thus, take Rangoon and other
large towns; they would find that the Chinese were clannish
to a marked degree, that the Indians supported each other,
but they would soon see that the Burman regarded all
whether Chinaman or Indian or another Burman-in exactly
the same light. He would hold such traffic as is absolutely
necessary with each one in exactly the same spirit, but
they were all equally "outsiders" so far as his own self
was concerned. If a neighbour got into trouble, it was of
no consequence to him; if another prospered,-well, it was his
own good fortune. It did not occur to him that the other
was a fellow-Burman, entitled to his congratulations in the
one case, to his commiseration in the other. Take, next,
their officials. Many a time had he come across an unfortunate
being, who had remained year in and year out in the same
locality, and who during that time had come to know few
individuals except his fellow officials, some clerks, and the
Chinaman from whom he got his stores. Of the general
mass of Burmans in his charge he knew nothing except in so
far as he had to deal with them in the course of his duty.
Then, again, their mode of life had altered almost beyond
recognition. Walk into a modern Burman's house at any
time, and they would find scarcely a single note-worthy
trace of his nationality. By all means, if they could afford
to do so, go in for anything that might conduce to their
comfort and to that of their guests; but why efface nearly
every sign of their "Burmanity," why do away entirely with
household requisites which were peculiarly Burman? In some
households he had seen articles of Western furniture,
totally unsuited to Burman needs, not at all used for the
purpose they were intended, and presumably kept for show.
Were it not for their women who had fortunately not yet
been infuenced to the same extent as the men he was
sure matters would be considerably worse than they were
now. And in passing, he might state his opinion that it
would indeed be a sad day for their countrymen if their
women were to be educated in the same manner and with
the same results as some of the men had been.

to make the Modern Burman a distinct species from the
simple, sincere Burman of old. Lost humility, and with it, old
notions of respect, had been the chief subject of reproach in
recent years, but it was not so bad as some people took it to
be. They might take, however, some good points-method
no longer haphazard, foresight, sense of duty. Above all,
they were no longer indolent. They had always been accused
of laziness, but such criticism had been a bit overdone. When
it came to the push, the Burman could work as hard, if not
harder, than others. But the new product was becoming
systematically energetic. He needed it in these days of
worldly strife, and his capabilities were beginning to show up.
What he now required was a little ambition, a spirit of emula-
tion, and new openings for his labour. The lecturer concluded
by saying that he had told them little or nothing that was
new. He had but repeated in a new form the sentiments
which had been uttered time and again by others; but he had,
he thought, this advantage, that he spoke at a time when
there was a spirit of reform in the air, a gradually increasing
demand for improvement accompanied by unmistakable signs
of social and religious activity. And at a juncture like this,
every contribution to the general discussion appeared to him
to be of some use.
