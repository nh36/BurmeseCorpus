To judge more fully of this proposal, it is necessary to describe
the country together with its administration.
It extends from North to South from Chatigan to Johnceylon
with three harbours fit for men of war. From East to West, from
the sea to China. 1
This immense country has always been divided in two Kingdoms,
both healthy and of an excellent soil.
The seaside, to the extent of ten leagues, is inhabited by the
Pigouans 2 and composes the Kingdom of Pegu. And the whole
of the interior part of the country by the Burmans and forms the
Kingdom of Ava. These two nations, though of the same religion,
have an unconquerable aversion to each other, occasioned by their
former wars and still more from the difference of their occupations.
The Pigouans make salt, hunt tigers, buffaloes and elephants,
live constantly in the woods in a state of independence, and are
supported by the production of their forests. Their neighbours to
the eastward are the Siamese, and further the Malays. To the
westward it is bounded by Arakan, now conquered by the Burmans,
and adjoining Chatigan, an English province. These people are
more warlike, less subject to control and less enlightened than the
Burmans who, from their earliest youth, study the laws, the art of
applying them, and trade. They are fond of riches and all manner
of luxury, more civilised, but less honest and sincere. They are
bounded in the East by the Siamese, and further by China; to the
West by the high mountains of Arakan, Assam and Thibet.
There is a third nation scattered among the other two called the
Carions, 3 whose humane and peaceful disposition entitles them to
the first rank. They manufacture cloths, cultivate the earth, draw
various productions from the earth [and] forests, never bear arms
for either of the other two and not even for their own defence, and
fly when the oppression becomes insufferable, preferring to live

The rich are exposed to continual apprehension of their lives, but
their wealth enables them sometimes to get into places and honours,
which have however no salaries annexed to them, and even very
moderate dues, but they furnish them with the means of extending
them and abusing them, which becomes indeed indispensable, as
well for the sake of retaining them as to pave the way for their

Plurality of wives is allowed by law as far as four. The fifth 9
enjoys all the honours; the others have the power of procuring a
divorce and may be divorced from their husbands, which cannot be
done with the first. The laws however are so enlarged that men in
general have as many as they can maintain.
Their dress is regulated by law and consists in three cubits of
silk or cloth round their middle. The rich wear over this a loose
morning gown which makes their walk very lascious. 10 The laws
however punish incontinence with the most barbarous and
humiliating sentences.
A Government which would render them their former privilege
would derive great advantage from them.
The revenues of the state are just what the King pleases, it
being an established maxim that the Sovereign is the disposer of
the lives and fortunes of his subjects. Thus he levies whatever
money or property he judges necessary to keep his nation crushed
under his iron sceptre.
A Government which would be satisfied with a duty of twenty
five per cent. on the different production[s] of the earth, together
with a sum of money proportionate to the wealth of every individual,
besides the duties on importations, would draw immense riches
from that country and receive the benedictions of all ranks, for there
is not a cooly in the Kingdom (in which there are no beggars) who
would not think himse f happy to pay twenty rupees per annum to a
Government who would ensure his liberty and property.

In the beginning of the troubles in that Kingdom about 50 years
ago, their celebrated Patriarch Barman declared before his death
that since they violated their holy laws they never would enjoy tran-
quility until they should be governed by a nation wearing hats, for
which reason the King and the Grandees do always wear hats and
do cause the Europeans to be put to death whenever they suspect
them.
The first step to be taken and which would not fail of producing
a great effect would be to publish a manifesto which should fix the
happiness of the conquered and the advantage of the conquerors,
which should ever go hand in hand and which would make as many
partitions 19 as there are inhabitants in the country who wish to be happy.
The example of Hyder Aly Caun, known for a barbarian without
faith or principle, is a proof of what I have now advanced. When they
entered the Carnatic in 1780 he promised the inhabitants the half
of their crop, and that promise alone brought deputies to him from
twenty leagues before him.
Those who have seen Bengal thirty years ago and who are
witnesses to its present state, notwithstanding all the misfortunes it
has suffered, will be able to calculate in some degree the mass of
wealth and happiness which would accrue from the possession of
Pegu and to see at a distance the vast advantage which would
result from it.
The two islands Andaman opposite to Pegu deserve in a parti-
cular degree the attention of the English nation, for they would belong
of right to the people who would be generous enough to make them-
selves masters of them and civilize their inhabitants. A foreign
captain who passed through the channel which divides the two
islands has assured me that he had found an excellent harbour in it
and no where less than seven fathoms.
