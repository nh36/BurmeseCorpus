An account of the Burmese Mission headed by Maň : Krī: Mahā Cañsū is given in
the journals entitled Bodhi pan swa: cā tam: 1 :: and Bangalā: swā: că tam:2
The mission left Ava on Saturday 9 October 1830 (8 waning of Satan:
Kywat 1192) and made their way to Akyab across the Arakan Yomas via the Am: 6:
pass. From thence they sailed on the Irawadi to Calcutta. During their stay in India
they had been to Barrackpur, Murshidabad, Monghyr, Patna, Benares, Allahabad, Cawn-
pore, Farukhabad, Agra and many other smaller towns and villages. They also visited
the Cotton Mill at Fort Gloucester, the Mint, the Aquarium, Taj Mahal and other places
of tourist interests. They went to Gaya and paid homage to the Maha Bodhi. The
Mission returned on board the Swallow and arrived Ran Kun sms on 12 July 1833 (11
waning of Wachui 1195) bringing with them an image of the Buddha and a young Bhodhi
plant.
Mańkrī: Mahā Cansū Ū : Rhwai was entrusted with the task of presenting the
following four points to the Governor General :
1. The return of the Kabhoau mes valley to the Burmese ;
2. The giving up by the British of that part of Martaban to the east of
Salween ;
3. The restoration of Arakan and Tenasserim provinces and
4. The abrogation of the clause stipulating the exchange of Residents.
This is the main object of the Mission. Hoping to see the Govenor General Mahā
Cansu waited in Calcutta for about eleven months. After that he had to perform a
tedious journey to the interior of the country as far as Farukbahad. Man: Krī: Mahā
Cansu arrived in Calcutta on 6 December 1830 but was presented to the Governor General
at Agra only on 23 November 1832. It was, however, merely a look at Lord Bentick
from close quarters as no discussion was entered upon at that meeting. Latter, in Calcutta,
on 16 March 1833 the Burmese Envoys were informed³ that the British Resident at
Ava would be instructed for necessary arrangements regarding the return of the Kabhoau
valley.

In résumé, Man: kri: Maha Cañsū was born at Ava in 1777 (s.1139). His name
was Mon Rhwai. At the age of ten he became a novice and began his studies. When he
was nineteen he left the monastery to become a server of betel to King Bhui: toau bhura:.
Later, he became a Chan Khre tuik wan, a Mre tuin: wan and a Mrac Cañ wan. Under
King Bha kri: toau he served as a Twań: sań: wan and afterwards became a wan thok. He
was given the town of Mui: tā: as his fief. Bhui: toau bhura: conferred upon him the
title of Cinta Kyoausu and thus was popularly known as Mui: tā: Mrui, că: Cinta
Kyoausū. As Twan: san: tuik wan he received the titles of Ne Myui: Kyoau than and
Nemyui: Sankhaya. Later, he was given the title Man: Kri Mahā Cañsū. He was a
man of learning and was noted for his knowledge of history and geography. In the year
1830 (1192) at the age of 53 he was sent at the head of a Mission to India. The main
object of the mission was to present the Governor General with the four questions of the
Kabho valley, Martaban District, Arakan and Tenasserim, and the withdrawal of the
Residency. The Kabho valley was returned but the British made it clear that it was
through Henry Burney's recommendation. He spent nearly three years in India trying to
obtain an audience with the Governor General. Man: krī: Mahā Cañsū returned in 1833
(s.1195) bringing with him an image of the Buddha and a young bodhi plant. On his
return he was appointed a wan thok and held this office till the end of King Bhakri:
toau's reign. It seems that his service was terminated when King Saya wati ascended
the throne. Probably he was dead by then. Twań: sań: wan Maň: krī: Mahā Cañsū
U: Rhwai is an important figure in the history of the Anglo Burmese relations during the
reign of King Bha kri: toau. He was the composer of literary works such as the Kapila
Moaukwan: and the Campeyya pyui, sac. His authorship of the Bodhi pań swā: cā tam:
is somewhat doubtful.
