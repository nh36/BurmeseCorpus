PREFACE
ANGOON UNIVERSITY, in starting on the work of publishing facsimiles of the inscriptions
of Burma, is following the good example set by the French Academie des Inscriptions et
Belles Lettres, which since 1926' has been doing the same for the inscriptions of Camboja.
It is not necessary to lay stress on the importance of these inscriptions; they are the main source of
our knowledge of the older history of these countries; they are also the first extant specimens of their
language and literature. All must sooner or later be edited, with full annotation and translation. But
it will be long before this labour is completed; and meantime there seems to be no reason to delays
the publishing of facsimiles, and thus securing, as we hope, the interest and help of orientalists
outside Burma,
The difficulties we have to face may easily be imagined. There is first the difficulty of expense
-no slight matter, when the number of inscriptions is considered. In his List of Inscriptions found
in Burma, Mr. Duroiselle, late Superintendent of the Burma Archaeological Survey, mentions no less
than 1525 inscriptions, including about goo originals, all contained in the six quarto volumes published
by the Survey between 1892 and 1913. In his preface to the Lit (published in 1921) he mentions,
besides these, 'a collection of over three hundred estampages, which is being added to continually"
We must reckon also with the fact that large parts of Burma, notably the Shan States, still await
archacological exploration. Indeed the full cost of the work contemplated might well deter the
authorities of a young, and far from wealthy University; but it was decided that the best of
finding means to finish the work was to begin it.
way
The facsimiles are reproduced by the collotype process from photographs of paper rubbings of the
stone inscriptions. Our second difficulty has been to find the rubbings, and even the inscriptions. Of
the former, University College, Rangoon, has long possessed a number sufficient for the purpose of in-
struction; and members of the staff and research-students, on holiday visits to Pagan and other centres,
have steadily added to the number. But not all of these were good in quality, and the set was far
from complete. In many cases the stones themselves are hard to find. Some have been moved from
the place they occupied when the six volumes were compiled; the local names given in these volumes
have sometimes changed, or been forgotten; and not infrequently the descriptions given in the books
are too vague to show the spot. The Archaeological Department was invited to help by loan of
rubbings. Mr. Duroiselle most kindly offered to do what he could; he pointed out, indeed, that the
collection in his office was also incomplete, that most of the rubbings were taken many years ago by
the negative process, and that many were so old and brittle that they could scarcely be opened.
None the less he was good enough to send a number of those we asked for, to Rangoon, for com-
parison with ours; and though in every case we chose our own, or found his rubbings too brittle to
be used, the loan at least enabled us, in the case of some inscriptions we had failed to find, to make
our own transcriptions, to distinguish copies and originals, and so to limit our attention to the latter.
Then a final search was made.
The plan of this portfolio is to furnish as complete a set as we can at present of the original
dated stone inscriptions of Burma, in any language, down to 399 Burmese Era, i.e. 1237 A.D. Inscrip-
tions undated and non-lithic-the latter mostly ink writings on temple walls are for the time being
omitted. We exclude also inscriptions already edited, with facsimiles, in Epigraphia Birmanica or

To summarize the result. Of the 125 inscriptions published and listed as original, only 63 are
included in this portfolio. 37 are rejected as not original. 12, deemed original, have not yet
traced. 14 other original inscriptions are omitted for reasons noted above. In place of these, we
have admitted on the ground of date 6 inscriptions with a late Liit number:-273a, 27, 465 a, 479,
1251 and 1299 a, b; and also no less than 50 inscripcions, or faces of inscriptions, which are not yet
listed nor published by the Archaeological Department. The Index gives the details of these, as well
as of those listed, mentioning, wherever it is possible, (a) the serial Lart number, (6) references to
published books containing the text of the inscription or its translation into English, (c) the original
provenance, and (d) the present position, of the stone. The plate-number at the top right corner of
each plate refers the English reader for details to the Index. At the foot of each plate the Burmese
reader will find the same plate-number and a brief identification of the inscription in Burmese.
